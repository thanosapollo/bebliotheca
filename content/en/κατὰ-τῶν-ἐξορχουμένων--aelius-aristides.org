They are charming, at any rate, those who, that I may begin auspiciously,
blame the hearers for their own good things in speeches, and say that it is
for this reason they step out of rhythm and correctness, that they may be able
to please as many as possible. And yet if, asking pardon, with this pretext

they make use, how do they even claim to be praised? For praise and pardon are
different things, and when they do not even praise themselves, they would
hardly be reasonably praised by the others. But if on the other hand there is
nothing disagreeable in the manner, but it is correct, why must they blame the
many and take refuge in this excuse, rather than show that the manner is
correct? But I think they are some clever people and they strike a double
blow, so that if they win, they may have conquered in this way, but if someone
detects them, they may have a retreat and say that they themselves were not
ignorant of the better, but were erring for the sake of others. And this is
the first and greatest vote, which they themselves cast against themselves
through excess of wisdom. For I would pray most of all to be well thought of
among all the others as well, but that the beginning at least be from the
hearth for me and that I first persuade myself, and not suffer the fate of the
dog in the fable, nor, while I gather the reputation from outside, destroy
that which it was proper for me myself to have about myself, nor get ahead in
taking against myself the conviction of being base, before confirming among
the others the conviction of being serious. And so far do they seem to me to
fall short of a defense, whenever they say these things, that they make two
charges instead of one against themselves, one if they behave so wantonly
about speeches, another if they do it for this reason, as if they were going
to be judged better that way. And moreover whenever they seek to mislead us as
if we would immediately believe what they invent and would not understand the
truth, they display this other third ignorance, if they do not think they are
detected. And the matter, I think, is of this kind. No one willingly lets go
of the best; but Plato and Pindar are wise in many other ways too, and not
least according to this argument, the one saying thus "no one found evil
willingly" and again

starting from the stories about Eriphyle, "Alas," he says, "how the thought of
creatures of a day is deceived, not knowing"; and the other everywhere, I
suppose, distinguishes and shows that errors are involuntary and that no one
is willingly base. Let not us either be persuaded by these men, as if having
something better in themselves, they then willingly let this go and exchange
it for the worse. They would not, at any rate, if they had gold, exchange lead
for it, not even if all men were going to applaud; nor millet for wheat, nor
dregs for wine, and those rotten, nor, I think, a rush-mat for a fine robe,
nor again to smell of hide, when they could of myrrh. But these are the words
of men disguising their own baseness and misfortune with fair names, who do
not wish, I think, to say what is just and true, that they neither know the
straight path nor can walk it, but they say they do these things gratifying
the hearers, just as if of the actors those who are hissed off and cannot
finish the play according to nature should say they do these things gratifying
the spectators. And one might say they are hissed and clucked at by the
spectators themselves in return for this favor. So also you by the hearers.

But not these things yet. But what prevents even the beggars who wrap
themselves in rags from saying that at home they have other quite noble cloaks
for themselves, but that they put on a show toward those outside? Well, the
same poet, whom I mentioned a little before, said that the good turn their
fair things outward. So that if they converse as about speeches that are
fairer, why must they blame the many, if they love these? But if they confess
they have gone wrong, they do the opposite of what is proper. For it was far
more reasonable that they should consider how they will say something better
for the sake of those who will hear and understand, than, making the existing
good things disappear, to display the worse, and that too procuring them on
purpose. We do not, at any rate, go to the processions having the worst
things, but even

if someone should not have them from home, we borrow, so that we may appear
having as finely as possible the cloaks, the gear, the horses, everything of
which the procession has need. So that whenever they say these things are
imported for themselves and procured and that they willingly step out of the
ancient form, one must ask whether it is of a worse or of a better. For if of
a better, how are they not infatuated, if leaving their own and more valuable
they pursue the cheaper and at the same time in no way belonging to them? But
if they will say of a worse, it is not possible to say that they willingly let
go the better, which they neither acquired nor ever even saw. For this is not
a defense concerning what they do amiss, but a confession that they would say
even worse than these things, if they were prevented from these. And I would
gladly learn from them whether, even if they were maimed, either, by Zeus, in
the eyes or the hands or any other part of the body whatsoever, they will
readily serve in these things if the hearers would gladly see it; or whether
they would give up nothing of the body's parts for the pleasure of others, but
destroying the best thing in the soul they count no penalty. I, at any rate,
whenever I hear these men saying these things, have not given up hope that
even the lame will dare to say that they have not come unwillingly to this
fortune, but willingly gave up their legs, so that they might appear as
pleasant as possible to those who see them. But whoever is so badly in love I
at least do not know, and let us grant, if you wish, even all men; but who is
so unfortunate as to choose to be unfortunate willingly? Or who for another's
favor ruins himself? For when we see that even doing good is pursued for this
reason, that from it one can expect to be treated well in return, surely to
put one's own things in a bad state for the sake of gratifying someone is
something everyone would altogether shrink from. Or there would be much
absurdity in thinking those of one's friends who are unwilling to do good in
return base, and oneself making such an excess as to use oneself worst first,
before doing any good

to those who associate with one, and before it is clear what thanks one will
get for the eagerness, to deprive oneself of the best of what one has. For we
would not, I suppose, cite the example of Zopyrus and the Cephallenian, of
whom the one, having mutilated his own nose and ears, entered Babylon, and the
other, having tortured himself with blows, "entered the wide-wayed city of the
Trojans," the one eager to take Babylon, the other Troy. For first, I suppose,
the prizes are not equal, to defeat enemies and to tickle some people's ears.
Next, the very opposite meets us from the examples. For if those men knew how
to endure so strongly as not even to spare their own body, but to undergo the
last pains and toils so as not to abandon anything of what they had resolved
from the beginning, while these men, when it is possible without trouble and
hardship to preserve their own premise, choose to grow soft, how would they
not justly suffer anything whatsoever? For I think that what those men did to
themselves, these would reasonably suffer from others, because of prostitution
and outrage against speeches. Or they would quite imitate those men for the
sake of not leaving their post, who now do everything, not to say suffer
everything, for the sake of leaving it. Further, Odysseus and Zopyrus
underwent these things when perhaps there was no other way for them to take
the enemy; but what will these men even say? That it is not possible to lead
and persuade men with the best and most correct speeches? But the argument is
shameful, not only false. Or that, when it is possible to lead that way, they
rather choose this way? Then they add superfluous shame.

Let us take it up again. It is not possible to lead men any other way than
this, let us grant for the sake of the argument, if it seems good. Well then,
if the better is separate, and leading is separate,

why must one let go the better, if indeed, even if we do not lead, we at least

better, it is possible to lead that way, but if it happens that one can lead
only this way, this is better. Why then do they divide and set the better
apart, and apart the things they were erring for the sake of others? For they
seem to me to be downright drunk about speeches whenever they take refuge in
these things. And moreover that they neither speak true things nor things like
the truth, but that for one who is going both to persuade men and in general
to have them under his hand, one must go and choose the best and strongest and
in every way uncorrupted and blameless of the possible, needs both much
argument to show and very little, little I say because it is so exceedingly
clear, but longer because one could show it with countless proofs and
examples. First, if you wish, let us consider the combatants of wars, in which
way they would both themselves be more saved and would turn and master those
drawn up against them, whether using firm and sound and close-covering arms,
or carrying a sword of tin, and a breastplate made of bark, and the spear of
fig-wood, and helmet and shield as one may guess from these? For I think that
exactness in these things and having been equipped from the beginning from the
best and as best as possible and holding out through the battle contributes to
safety and glory and victory together and all that one would put in this
class, for those who have and use them. But if they are torn or broken, or
smashed in some other way, or even from home at once are spongy and rotten,
the bodies inherit the fortune and not even for the very brave is it possible
to find what they will do with themselves; Homer too bears witness to these
things. For in him of the heroes

also wonder at, if for men at war it is shame to say what is not fine, but for
speakers what it is shame to say is no worse.

Come then, let us also examine those at the crown contests, for example
Dorieus the Rhodian and Glaucus the Carystian and Milo and Polydamas, and all
whose images are of bronze, whether those who arranged the contest for
Olympian Zeus crowned them when they were wanton and acting drunk and twisting
the same things as dancing-girls, or when they displayed some marvelous
endurance and strength of soul and body together, which procure the greatest
and most secure pleasures both for those who have them and for those who share
in the knowledge. By Zeus, are not the best and most marvelous horses, and
those most according to the pleasure of the spectators, those who finished as
strongly and lastingly as possible and having gone nothing outside the course,
preserving their proper excellence everywhere? And by nature the same thing is
both best and most pleasant for its own use; since indeed the beauties of
bodies have this manner, whether you name Ganymede or Pelops or anyone
whatsoever, together with the allotment of fortune they also draw those who
see; and very reasonably. For there must,

Then in the other things each, saving its own principle thus, leads and

And moreover if speech was invented for the sake of something else, not of
persuading, perhaps there would be a dispute; but when it is quite clear that
the whole habit of the rational faculty has set out toward this one thing, one
of two things is necessary, I suppose, either that the speech is not correct
nor sufficient, or that it is at once both best and masters the hearers. So
that there is no need to corrupt it for the sake of mastering, but just as
siege-engines are by nature able to take, even if none of the enemy know from
what and how they were put together, so a speech well put together does its
own work, even if it meets the most inexperienced. And I wonder if of wine and
grain and oil and honey and meat and sweetmeats and milk and money and water
and wood and stones not even one would say the corrupted are more pleasant
than the uncorrupted, but that speeches that are as pure and unmixed as
possible are more unpleasant than the corrupted. Not even one would say these
things, and that this is not said for the sake of paradox, but that it is
impossible to be otherwise, the things themselves accuse. Who is the best poet
of epic? Homer. And who pleases as many men as possible and in whom do they
most rejoice? Or did he himself also foresee this on his own behalf? For

And we have poets as witnesses of these things too, who say that the best and

why must one list the ancients, either poets or orators? But of us here, and
may a god kindly escort the speech, who ever stirred greater uproars in
assemblies? Or who more, to speak with the gods, for let this lead all my
frankness, touched both nations, I mean both the skilled and those we name the
many, who even with no law compelling them bring their vote under oath. I pass
over the rest. But what you now utter upon these things you see, I suppose. Is
there then anything we ever said to gratify the hearers? Not a syllable. And
yet many things and of every kind. For we alone of those who ever took up
speeches pray, to speak most moderately, having shut all the goods that there
are and are thought to be about speeches into one power, to display them with
excess. So I. And I would say both that I gratify in nothing whatsoever and
that there is nothing I do not gratify, in return for which I reasonably
gratify with what is proper. Then I, having observed in the facts and in my
own speeches that speaking the best things does not come about without reward,
do I concede to these babblers to lie against the many, that they play the
fool for their sake? Not so long as I continue to know myself. And if some,
claiming in turn to have chosen better than these, do not hit the many, but
are quenched before anyone sees the flame, this at least is nothing against
the more solemn speeches or those stronger in general. For they suffer this
from want of the greatest things, having furnished neither sufficient judgment
nor order about speeches. But if they were perfect in the art of the Muses,
what is said to belong to Orpheus

Such prizes do those who honor the better carry off from the gods of speech,

fortune, but should say he became such by forethought. But you neither speak
true things, my good man, nor are you sound of mind at all, if you say you
honored these things willingly. And now I am in danger of being more humane
toward these men in these speeches than they themselves toward themselves. For
I show them as unwilling and suffering this by some misfortune, while they say
they variegate on purpose. And the laws command those who go wrong willingly

to pay double the penalty of those who harmed unwillingly. And I think I
cannot even from the beginning conjecture what it is they say. For they seem
to me to say both that they do not give way unwillingly, and again that they
do not willingly, but unwillingly give way. For whenever they refer it to the

riddles. And yet Cratinus says, I suppose, that it is among shameful things,
gratifying friends, to be base oneself; and these men, if they treat them as
friends, are in a similar state to them; so that if they delight in the same
things, they gratify themselves no less than these; but if again they do not
think them friends, in return for what do they gratify? For whether as to
lovers or as lovers, the shame at least is inescapable. And they riddle here
too. For they say the many are in love with them, but they themselves appear
to be in love with these, if at least they are wanton in order to please
these. And moreover if they care nothing for them, why on earth will they say
they gratify? But if they care, would they be better educating them and
showing the existing path of speeches, or themselves inheriting these men's
wickedness? Just as if some physician, claiming to be one and needing himself
to advise the best things to the sick, should himself serve their wishes and
define the art by gratifying. Well then, you will destroy those men, and you
yourself will be destroyed among judges of good sense, since nature too thus
ordered the general to lead the soldiers, not to the soldiers

Or for rulers in general? Not even for any age of them all. For is it for the
younger? But they will seem to play the companion, if they welcome these
things. Or for the men? But they will not seem to confirm the name. Or for the
elders? But there is much untimeliness of the shame. It remains then for
women, and those the most wanton, against whom it is right to judge these men.
And yet they emulate them, but fall short by some considerable amount. And I
wonder if for those who corrupt the coinage, Demosthenes said, death is laid

stamped we shall grant to speak freely, or that this first be allowed to be
said; and if someone stumbles in a word, he is ignorant at once, but if with
his whole choice he corrupts what is ordered, he will not then stand far from
all festivals. And moreover the counsel is not about speeches only, but about
all seemliness and good order and whether one must abide by the laws, or do
whatever one wishes for the sake of the pleasure of the day. For if what I say
wins, law and order win, by which not only the cities, but all earth and
heaven itself both has been put together and is preserved, but what these men
counterfeit is hostile to all both divine and human affairs.
