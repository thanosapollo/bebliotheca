* First Homily on the Creation of Man, Recension C

2a I have come to discharge an old debt, whose repayment I postponed not by
ingratitude of choice but by weakness of body, a most necessary debt and one
owed to your hearing. For it would indeed be unjust that we who have been
taught the things concerning beasts and swimming things and cattle and birds,
and heaven and the things in heaven, and concerning earth and the things in
the earth, should not also be enlightened from the God-inspired 3a Scripture
concerning our own generation. For just as our eyes, looking at the things
outside, do not see themselves, unless they happen upon something smooth and
solid and from there the sight, being reflected as from a reflux, makes them
see their own things behind them, so also our mind does not otherwise see
itself, unless it stoops into the Scriptures. For the light here, being
reflected, becomes the cause of each of us being seen, since we are without
understanding of ourselves and still without survey of our own constitution,
not knowing what we are and who we are. For we are most neglectful of
ourselves, having no knowledge of the things of creation ready at hand and of
the smallest things in us. And yet many treatises concerning our body, and not
a few of those before us, have spent all their 4a zeal upon this, and if you
go upon medicine, you will find how much it recounts to us concerning the need
of our members, how many hidden ways it discovered concerning our inward
constitution in the anatomical operations, coexisting in the unseen, and
various activities toward one common breathing of the body: channels of
breath, water-courses of blood, the drawing of respiration, the dwelling of
the hearth of the heat upon the heart, the continuous motion of the
pericardial spirit. Myriad other things have been philosophized among them, of
which none of us is experienced, by having assigned no leisure to this part of
the inquiry, nor does each know himself, who he is. We are ready to know
heaven rather than ourselves. So do not despise the wonder in you; for you are
small, 5a as you think, but the account coming upon you will find you great.
For this reason wise David, knowing how to examine himself accurately, says,
Your knowledge was made wonderful from me, that is, I was able to discover the
knowledge concerning your wonders. From where? From me; for considering the
art in me, with how much wisdom my body was constructed, from this small yet
wonderful construction I conceived the great craftsman. Let us make man
according to our image and according to likeness. It was shown recently in
passing, and sufficiently shown, what the word is and to whom the word is. The
Church has the proofs concerning these things, or rather it has a faith more
firm than proof.

Let us make man. Learn yourself beginning from here. Not yet concerning
another of the constructions is this voice recorded. Light came to be, and by
a simple 6a command it came forth; for God said, Let there be light. Heaven,
and it stood without counsel; luminaries, and he took no counsel beforehand
concerning them; sea and infinite deeps were brought into being by the
command. Kinds of fishes of every sort came to be by the order. Beasts and
cattle, swimming things and birds: He spoke and they came to be. Here not yet
man, and there is counsel concerning man. He did not say as concerning all the
others: Let there be man. Learn from here the honor of yourself; he did not
toss your generation to a command, but a council was convened in God
concerning you, how the honored living being is about to be brought into life;
for he says, Let us make. The wise one takes counsel, the craftsman considers.
Does something perhaps lack of his art, and from the care does he wish to make
his work of art complete? No; but only in order to show you that you are
perfect with God. You learned then that there are two persons so far in God,
the one speaking and the one to whom the word is. Why did he not say, I will
make man, but, Let us make? So that



7a you may understand the equal lordship, so that recognizing the Father you
may not be ignorant of the Son, so that you may know that the Father made
through the Son and the Son created by the paternal will, and you may glorify
the Father in the Son and the Son in the Holy Spirit. Thus you have become a
common work, so that you may also be a common worshiper of both, not splitting
the worship but uniting the Godhead. You see a history simple in form, but
theology unmixed in thought. For after Let us make he added: And God made man.
He did not say, they made man, so that you may not take occasions of
polytheism. For if the making also had been introduced in the plural, men
would have become unsparing toward ascribing to themselves many multitudes of
gods. First then: Let us make man, so that you may know Father and Son and
Holy Spirit; then: And God made man, so that 8a you may conceive the three
persons as one God, not making the hypostases into one, but reckoning the one
essence and power, and may have one glory, not being divided about the worship
nor falling into polytheism. For he did not say, And the gods made man, but,
God made. For even if there is a proper hypostasis of the Father and a proper
of the Son and a proper of the Holy Spirit, yet not also three gods, because
the essential Godhead conceived in the three is one and the same. For the
Godhead which I see in the Father, this also in the Son, and that which is in
the Son, this also in the Holy Spirit; because the form contemplated in these
is also one and the beginning the same. For this reason also from us there is
one worship and doxology to the three as to one God. The prelude of our
generation, then, has been understood as true theology. Let us make man
according to our image and according to likeness. That we have come to be
according to the image of God is quite clear from Scripture. But how is the
According to the image of God to be understood? One must not think anything
bodily and earthly. Let us cleanse our uninstructed heart, an uneducated
preconception, ignorant assumptions about God. Such as some say: if we have
come to be according to the image of God, God is altogether of like form with
us. And perhaps there are eyes about God 9a and ears and a head and hands and
a haunch upon which he is seated [and all our things] (for it is said also in
Scripture: God sits) and feet for him, through which he walks? But God is not
such. Put away from the heart unseemly imaginings, cast out from yourself a
thought that does not fit the greatness of God. God is without shape and
simple; do not imagine a form about him. Do not diminish the Great in Jewish
fashion, do not encompass God with bodily thoughts, do not circumscribe him
with your mind. He is ungraspable in greatness. Consider the Great and to the
great add more than what has been thought, and to the more the still more, and
persuade your own reasoning that you cannot reach the boundless. Do not then
think a shape; for from 10a power God is conceived. Do not think magnitude;
for he is everywhere and overflows beyond the all, intangible, invisible,
incomprehensible, neither encompassed by shape nor circumscribed by magnitude,
not measured by power, not enclosed by time, not bounded by any limits. For
there is nothing at all about God such as about us or even the things only
intelligible. How then did Scripture say that we have come to be according to
the image of God? Let us recognize what we have that is proper to God, and
that the According to the image can in no way be taken in the bodily form. For
the one is upon some seen body, but the invisible is not shaped in the
visible. Nor indeed would the perishable ever be an image of the imperishable.
For the body grows, diminishes, ages, is altered; it is one thing in youth and
another in old age, another when it is in good condition and another when 11a
it is in sufferings, another rejoicing and another grieving, another being
confident and another fearing, another in peace and another in war, another in
fullness and another in want. There is one color of one awake and another of
one sleeping; for of the one it blooms and grows red as the heat goes toward
the outer surface, of the other it has been cooled because the heat sinks into
the depth. Therefore also the bodies of those sleeping are somewhat pale. How
then can the changing be like



the unchanging? That which never stands still nor remains upon the same, like
that which always has itself in the same way and according to the same things?
According to our image: what does he say? Is the flowing an image of the
unmoved nature, and the formed of the formless?

How then shall we find the According to the image? In that again which God
himself said close at hand 12a. For I do not insert into the account some
invention of my own from outside. Let us make man according to our image and
according to likeness and let them rule the fishes. According to the pattern
of rule, then, or of authority, our likeness toward the divine is to be
understood. And the ruling is not in body at all † or in reasoning nor in
flesh but in the soul. For the human body is weaker than many irrational
animals, and our flesh would never be compared in power to that of the camel
or of the elephant or of the horse or of the ox or of each of the greatest
beasts you may name. But our soul and the mind in it has been able to subject
all these easily, and man shifts even the great and hard-to-handle burdens by
technical inventions, not by bodily strains. So that our ruling and According
to the image and likeness of God is to be contemplated in the constitution of
reasoning, not in the form of the body. But perhaps you will say: and how did
God not speak about reasoning, but says that man has come to be according to
13a his own image? Because reasoning also is called man, and the soul is the
inner man. Hear the apostle saying: Even if our outer man is being destroyed,
yet the inner is being renewed. How then? Shall we recognize two of the same
man? Yes, he says, one the appearing and one hidden under the appearing, the
more proper and truer man. For the man formed from God is in a way twofold,
the one seen, the other conceived. And by the greater and truer part the one
within and not appearing is by nature man. For the inner is I myself, but this
outer and bodily is perhaps mine but not I. For surely the hand and the foot
and the remaining parts of the body would not be I, but the intellectual and
rational of the soul, using the body as an instrument. Let us understand Let
us make man according to our image and according to likeness, then, as: we
will give him an abundance of reason; and thus Let them rule the fishes and
the beasts and all. He did not 14a say that Let us make man according to our
image, and let them be angry and desire and be subject to passions. For the
passions were not taken into the image of God, but the reasoning which is
master of the passions, the ruler of all the bodily things and standing above
the appearing and the false. But marvel, I pray, at the care and providence of
God concerning you from the very beginning, that you were created at once and
you were created a ruler and you have the rule continuous and without
successor. For a man receiving some authority from a man, as a mortal from a
mortal and as one who does not have from one who does not have (for what
authority of soul does a man have?), reasonably has also very quickly thrown
it away again. But you received this from God himself and you have it
indelible, not written on woods nor on perishable tablets, consumptions of
moths, but engraved in nature itself from the first divine voice of Let them
rule. For from then and until the end all these things are slaves of human
rule. Let them rule, he says, the fishes, the beasts of the earth, the birds
of heaven, the cattle, the reptiles that 15a creep upon the earth. He did not
say Let us make man according to our image and according to likeness, and let
them eat every fruit-bearing tree which has fruit in itself. For these things
later, after the planting of paradise, so that you may be taught to have the
necessary things of the flesh as second beside the leading things of the soul,
since first the power of ruling was voted together with us, then also the
enjoyment of paradise was lavished together. You are a ruling living being, O
man, and why do you serve passions? Why do you cast down your own dignity and
become a slave of sin?



Why do you make yourself a captive of the devil? You were appointed ruler of
creation; do not throw away the dignity of your own nature. Have you been set
in some worldly rule? You have more through obligation, that reasoning
likewise have as co-ruler and master of the passions, so that you may not be a
laugh and a mockery to those being ruled. Instead of a ruler and one standing
above they consider you as subject and led, equal even to a bound and useless
slave. You were called a slave? Why does the slavery of the body grieve you?
Why do you not think greatly of the lordship given you by God, that you have
reasoning as master of passions? When then you see your master being a slave
of pleasure, and yourself prevailing over it by the power of reasoning, know
that you are named a slave only, you are not; but that one, even if he has the
lordship in name, has in deed drawn upon himself a confirmed slavery. For
whenever perhaps the passion of fornication drags that one, enslaving him, and
you have stood above this through the lordship of reasoning, how would you
yourself not be properly master, the one standing above pleasure, and that one
rather a slave, the one subject to the passion trampled by you? Where then the
power of ruling is, 16a there is the image of God. And where the image of God
is, there is the man formed by him. And first he said that man rules the
fishes, because these also have been settled farther from him than the other
animals, so that through the more distant and watery he may afterward include
much more also the nearer, that is, the land animals and those reared with us.
How then do we who do not live with them seem to rule the fishes? If ever you
have known yourself appearing beside a lake and have seen how your shadow
alone frightened all the little fishes dwelling in it, you would have
recognized at once also the thing of the rule. For who is so much a master of
a house, when the house is in uproar, that having stood by suddenly he made
quiet and changed all into good order by his own presence, as all the watery
creation, when one man has appeared, changes its form and no longer has the
free way of life? It does not dare to swim upon the backs either of the lake
or of the sea. The dolphin, when it perceives a man being somewhere near,
although itself the most kingly of the swimming things, is as it were ashamed
and is no longer moved nor leaps as is its custom. Thus the rule over the
swimming things was given to man. And when you consider your reasoning
extending through all and enslaving 17a all by the arts, how do you not also
rule the great sea-monsters? For I myself have seen human invention, that they
make a construction of certain hooks and putting upon these baits commensurate
with the size of the sea-monsters that swallow them. Then of those ends of the
ropes, from which the hooks hang at the other extremity, stretching bladders
with breath and binding them they send those ends aloft over the deep. The
wild sea-monsters, then, being borne to these baits and receiving the hooks
inside themselves, drag the bladders into the depth, but the nature of the
bladders, being upward-tending because of the lightness of the breath enclosed
within, again pulls them up to the surface. And being stuck fast in their own
food they rage up and down and wandering they search through the depths and
change deeps of deeps. And they undergo much ineffectual toil and at last by
this small hook the great and unwearied beast becomes capturable, tamed by the
toil and worn down by hunger, and dead, being borne with the bladders, 18a it
becomes a catch to the fisherman; the immense by the small, the unconquerable
in strength by the weak. Why? Because having received power of ruling by the
abundance of reasoning, man brings the most disobedient as bad runaways to
obedience, and those who cannot be led by gentleness he enslaves through
necessity. Thus everywhere the power of ruling exists, being born in man from
the one who created. From this swordfish and hammerheads and whales and
sawfishes and seals and all those fearful



kinds and names of sea-monsters have become subject to man. Let them rule the
fishes of the sea and the beasts of the earth. Do you not see the lion, the
heavy, the roaring one, whose name also is unbearable to the hearing and whose
roar makes the earth tremble, whose charge it is far from anyone to endure?
For none even of the irrational and greatest animals has such an abundance of
power as to stand against a charging lion. But nevertheless 19a you often see
even this unbearable and fearful one shut up in a small cage. Who then is the
one who confined him, the one who devised the small prison for the great beast
and by the sparseness of those wooden threads prepared breath for the one shut
in, so that it may not be choked by its own panting, and by the free breathing
managed its security beforehand? Is it not man? Does not the same also mock
the leopard, when he stretches a paper shaped into a man, and the one tore the
paper, but the man lying below laughed at the folly of the beast? Does he not
also make the most savage and hardest to combat of the remaining beasts into
playthings by his own inventions? Does he not hold all by abundance of mind?
How shall I speak about the flying things? That he does not himself go up into
the air with the body, but nevertheless he flies along with the birds, using
reasoning as even wings. For nothing holds the mind of man. The things in the
depth it searches through, the things above earth it hunts, the things in the
air it anticipates 20a and having dragged them down to earth it carries them
off. Have you ever seen a bird sitting on the tip of a branch and laughing at
men turning about the earth below, in trusting the lightness of the wing? But
nevertheless here again one may see a mischievous boy putting reeds under
reeds and attaching birdlime at the tip of the reeds, then stealthily through
the branches and the leaves hiding the presence of the birdlime and leading
astray the eye of the bird, and thus by a small touch and hold mastering the
air-traveling, and carrying off bound by birdlime the winged thing just now
borne through the aether. For the man lies below and both his hands and both
his feet are below, but his mind goes up together into height with the winged,
and through art the heavenly things become reachable for him and the feathered
capturable. For nets are set for the winged and archers take aim at the flying
things and the more gluttonous are hunted by baits of foods. Have you never
seen an eagle falling greedily upon a catch, then being pierced through with
speed by the traps below? Thus the high becomes below, drawn down by human
inventions. For God brought all under the hand of man, and filled the creation
and did not make man without a share 21a of the lordship of these things. Do
not say: how do the things borne in the air concern me? Since through
reasoning those also become subject to you, together with the reptiles
themselves that creep upon the earth. Do you see how you ought to understand
the having come to be According to the image of God? According to the ruling,
clearly, and the intellectual and rational of the soul. And God made man. What
then is man? Let us also define from what we have read and from what we have
heard. For we no longer have need to borrow foreign definitions nor to
introduce the things from vanity into the reasoning of the truth. Man then is
a rational work of God that has come to be according to the image of the one
who created him. If then anything is lacking to this account toward a complete
definition of the subject, let those who have spent much beforehand on the
taking up of the perishing wisdom inspect it. But let us hold to what is
before us: Man has come to be according to the image of God. And God blessed
man and said: Increase and multiply and fill the earth. This is also a
blessing of fishes. For God said: Let the earth bring forth reptiles of living
souls, and it was so. And God said, Increase and fill the waters. What then is
extra? Of necessity he both granted you these common things 22a and the proper
things await you. For you also increase as also the remaining



animals and from the small of the first you come to completion by additions
little by little. So also horses and dogs, so also eagles and swans and all
that you may name, having received the completion of growth through a small
first conception by addition little by little, again through diminution turn
back to the less. The things, then, that were common of nature, he granted
also to us.

Increase, that is: be nourished, being born small become large and let there
be a limit of the increase. For it is not that since in the first seven-year
period we are nourished and a change of age takes us upon the second week of
years, we already ought similarly in every part of our life to change age by
weeks. The first week has the childhood age 23a and the limits of the
childhood age are clear, the change of the teeth. For some fell away, others
grew under. Growth took a second beginning until the end of the fourteenth,
and this is that of the boys. For first a little child, then a boy, then from
fourteen years an adolescent and next a man. Here are the limits of the
increase. Increase then not into everything. For if you become a hundred
years, you do not admit increase from the first to the hundredth year, but
this Increase up to a certain point. For the things of the increase are not
without measure. Increase, one word spoken wisely, is arranged providentially.
For according to the first constitution that was laid down in the womb the
principles of the increase also were laid down. But it is not that afterwards
the gift of age comes on as something newer, but from the beginning the
maternal deposits have the aptitudes toward Increase laid down together. The
teeth fell: we knew 24a that it was increased such a measure. The father
measured the three-year-old child. He knows that it will receive double this
size in the completion. For as much as the man appears in length in the
three-year period, twice so much he will be in the filling up of the increase.
This measure of human nature passes as far as a limit from the first week to
the second. There then the heat is more; for the sparser formation of the body
and the moisture fail and the members begin little by little to be
strengthened, but it has not yet the complete. For their flesh is still newly
compacted and unsuited toward enduring labors, having received only the light
and easily moved. But in the third week then it also receives together the
completion of the increase. Still however the stretching of the body, that is
the increase, comes on according to length. But after the third week, when
nature has breathed from the labor of the increase toward the above, 25a it
begins then to pass the increase into the breadths and to put thicknesses
around the raised members of the body and thus to make them solid and strong
and to restore them to the more vigorous. These things nature does each time
without fail according to its own sequence. But this came to be for it from
the beginning as a signal from a master's word and command, and what was then
spoken once proceeds always through all the creation as far as the end.
Increase and multiply. Increase, so that the work may not remain incomplete;
Multiply, so that the creation may not be limited in one.

But fill not by the dwelling; for thus we would have been straitened living,
if the earth were as much as measures us. But fill by the account of the
authority. He says Fill according to ruling all the earth. For not surely also
the burned and uncultivated and the frozen and impassable, not also this 26a
do men inhabit and fill the earth. But we fill it properly when we know by
reasoning how great a measure of the burned and uninhabited, how great the
northern clime, which because of the exceeding cold is uncultivated and
useless, when having chosen the useful of it we dismiss as much as is useless
and unprofitable for the way of life. So that Fill the earth is instead of:
become lords of the earth. For



it is not that because we do not have the use of all of it, we are not for
this reason thought to rule all of it. For neither when you have bought the
grain are you for this reason not lord of the whole, because in the grain you
find the edible and the thrown away and you throw it away, and you throw away
the little stones as useless, picking them out from there, and if anything
else has been mixed in with the grain unsuited toward food, and having blown
off the chaff and separated the tares, you choose as much as is clean only 27a
toward your own eating. Thus then also the earth, the one was granted to you
as suited toward dwelling, and the other as necessary toward farming, another
as let go toward pasturage of four-footed beasts, and another according to
some other need of those fitting you according to the way of life. And to
arrange † you what you wish and as you wish concerning it, since the master
has set you as lord of his own creation. This then is the blessing of Fill the
earth, this the legislation, this the dignity given to us by God. And God made
man, in the image of God he made him. Did you not mark that the completion is
incomplete toward the first counsel? There Let us make man according to our
image and according to likeness, here In the image of God he made him. The
counsel had two: the According to the image and the According to likeness. The
crafting has the one only, the According to the image. Was the counsel
otherwise and the work otherwise? Did some regret follow in the making, or
perhaps weakness of the maker, having chosen great things but having completed
small? Or might garrulity perhaps be supposed in the former words and the
addition of the second 28a superfluous, as the two words having the same
meaning and signifying the same thing without difference? But whichever of all
these we suspect and put forward as a pretext, this will end in no small
accusation of Scripture. For whether one should say that the According to
likeness is something redundant, as saying the same thing twice, he convicts
the word as idle, and this is a terrible blasphemy against Scripture, which
never knows how to talk idly. Therefore both are necessary and of proper
meaning, both the According to the image and the According to likeness. How
then was the According to the image and according to likeness not said
likewise also after man came to be? God made him, but only According to the
image. If because perhaps the maker grew weak, the account is impious and out
of place; if because he repented, the thought is often more impious; if also
because he perhaps later in the making corrected the former counsel as not
altogether right, this thought likewise is of atheism. But neither does the
craftsman grow weak, nor does the good one who knows all things repent and
delay about the promises, nor does the wise one change his counsel, nor does
Scripture say any such thing. What then is the account of the divine Moses
saying only the According to the image in the making concerning man, having
kept silent the According to likeness, although in the former counsel both
were likewise taken together by him?

29a The solution is easy for the one attending and wishing to understand. For
the According to the image has been given to us by nature and is unchangeable
from the beginning and is present together until the end, but the According to
likeness we accomplish later from choice and from ourselves. In the first
counsel then, saying Let us make man according to our image, God also added
the According to likeness, showing that he will also put into us a
self-determining choice able to make us be likened to God; and we were also
completed such according to the prediction of God. For many are those who have
been likened to him, and altogether also those who will be likened, even if we
do not all hasten toward this but rather walk the opposite from willing evil.
But in the construction later he said only the According to the image, as
having constructed this alone complete and unalterable in human nature; but
the According to likeness, since he sowed it in man so far only in power, and
it had not yet been shown also into a result but still needed toward a result
the action of the choice of the one who received, he reasonably kept silent.
If then



God, having taken nothing beforehand in the counsel, said also According to
likeness and through 30a that first word at once granted us the power of
coming to be like God, we would not have been able later from our own
authority alone to receive this great gift in addition. And for this reason it
was necessarily taken then. But since we were already set also into being
created and were receiving being from not being, what was given by the creator
to our nature as it were co-essential and from there complete, this we had at
once and from it we were called according to the image of God, clearly; but
what was not at once completed together with us nor accompanied naturally from
the forming but was going to fit us later from the choice that would come on,
that is our activity, I mean the likeness of God, this we neither yet were nor
could be said to be. For the creator left this incomplete on purpose, so that
the working of the good might be completed by us and the good might seem ours,
so that we might also have a reward owed from this; so that we may not remain
as lifeless images completed by a painter but lying in vain and contributing
nothing from ourselves toward 31a the beauty for themselves, which the one
looking and considering them written accurately reasonably both marvels at and
praises the painter, but the colors themselves through themselves or the
panels on which they have been written he thinks worthy of no account. In
order then that the marvel may become mine also and I may share with God the
praise from the fair crafting, he left to me the coming to be According to the
likeness of God.

There is then in power the rational and intellectual in me, which also shows
that I have been made According to the image of God, but in activity the also
claiming virtue and accomplishing the good by deed and thus coming through the
best way of life into the According to likeness of God. So that the According
to the image of God is a beginning and root of the good, which I have had at
once in being created, laid down together with my nature, but the According to
likeness of God comes to me last from my works and the labors about the fair
things and the virtuous way of life through the whole of life. Reasonably then
the creator did not declare that I also at once came to be According to the
likeness of God together with being created. For hear also what he says in the
Gospel: Become perfect as also your heavenly Father is perfect; Because he
makes his sun rise upon evil and good and rains upon righteous and
unrighteous. Do you see where and when 32a the Lord restores to us the
According to likeness? When you become a hater of evil, not remembering evil,
not taking vengeance on the enemy, when you become a lover of brothers,
sympathetic, you have been likened to God. If such as God is toward you the
sinner who opposes him each day, you also become such toward the brother who
has offended against you, by perfect love and compassion toward the neighbor

likeness. Let him have, he says, in the creation itself the According to the
image, but let him also come to be thereafter According to likeness through
his own zeal, since he has received the power of this in his own nature. If he
had also made you according to likeness of God from the beginning, where is
your grace? From where would you have been crowned? If the craftsman had given
back the whole to nature 33a, how would the kingdom of the heavens have been
opened to you? For this reason the one was given, the other was left
incomplete, so that having perfected yourself you may become worthy of the
recompense from God. How then may we become according to likeness? Through the
Gospel. For what is Christianity but likeness of God according to what is
possible to the nature of man? If you have accepted being a Christian, hasten
to become like God, put on Christ. But how will you put on, you who have not
yet received baptism? For the one who has not received the garment of



immortality is set very far from the likeness toward God. If I were saying to
you: Come, become like a king, would you not have judged me a benefactor and
with much speed have hastened upon what is called? But since I wish to make
you like God, do you flee the word that makes you 34a god, having blocked your
ears, so that you may not even have hearing of the saving words? And God made
man, in the image of God he made him. The woman would perhaps say here: the
account is nothing to me; the man came to be then. For he did not say the
woman or simply a human being, but by the addition of the masculine article he
indicated this. So that no one may ignorantly or even maliciously suppose that
by the appellation of man the male only is indicated, he added immediately
Male and female he made them, so that you may know that the woman also has
both the having come to be According to the image of God and the having come
to be According to likeness of God just as also the man. For the natures of
both were formed alike and of equal honor and their actions equal, equal the
prizes, equal the condemnation. Let the woman not put forward the weaker as a
pretext; for this is in the flesh. The soul however has its own power equally
with the manly. Since then of equal honor 35a they have obtained the According
to the image of God, they have of equal honor also the virtue and the display
of good works. And there is no excuse for the one who happens to put forward
the weakness of the body. For even if it was simply tender by nature, yet it
is ready toward sympathy, vigorous in endurances, intense in vigils. For the
female far surpasses even the manly nature concerning the persistence of the
good and the enduring, and a man would never be equaled to a woman either
concerning the exercise of fasting and the rest of self-control or the
abundance in tears or the labor-loving in prayers or the ungrudging in
well-doings. I myself have seen a woman secretly taking away the needful
things of the house, a praiseworthy and saving theft, and distributing them to
the poor in the hidden and benefiting even the unwilling and unknowing husband
and the household. For on behalf of that one and of the increase of the house
and of the remaining of the children she was doing the well-doing. 36a The
female then also has no less than the male both the According to the image and
the According to likeness, since these are both contemplated and constituted
not in the outwardly appearing man, that is the body, but in the inner and
more proper. And for this reason we also make the discourse toward both as
toward one and we exhort with all zeal into the According to likeness. And
this will altogether belong to you through kindness, forbearance, sharing,
distribution, love of one another, love of brothers, hatred of evil and
mastering the passions of sin. For from this there follows also the ruling of
the irrational.

And let them rule, he says, the beasts. Perhaps then you will say: and of what
beasts am I about to rule and where are these present to me? But you do not
know then that myriads of beasts are about you and you carry around a great
such 37a crowd in yourself and it dwells within you continually? Is anger a
small beast, whenever it barks <in> the heart and grows savage worse than
every dog? And deceit, when it lurks in the soul and prepares the plots, is it
not a beast more savage than a fox and harder to tame than a bear? And the one
sharp toward insult, is he not a scorpion outright? And the one taking revenge
secretly, is he not more difficult than a viper? And the greedy and rapacious,
is he not a wolf itself? What beast do we not nourish within and in ourselves,
since also the woman-mad is a horse raging? For Horses, it says, became
woman-mad, each was neighing upon the wife of his neighbor. For he transferred
himself upon the nature of the irrational through the passion to which he was
made akin. Many beasts then in us of which struggle to rule them. For if you
rule the outer beasts but leave the inner unruled, that is unsubjected,
consider what you will seem to those seeing you, holding an irrational lion
38a and despising its roar, but being defeated by the anger about the heart
and grinding the teeth,



and often sending forth an inarticulate voice under the madness that has
seized, and perhaps even stretching out hands toward revenge on the one
standing by. Are you not from this more wretched and more laughable than every
one being ruled but well-ordered, because the anger rather rules you, having
thrust aside the reasoning and having taken over the dominion of the soul, and
leads you wherever <ἂν> it happens equal even to a bound one, you whom God set
as ruler of all? But you, I pray, keep the dignity given. You were created a
ruler. Rule passions, rule beasts, rule reptiles, rule birds; do not be lifted
aloft in reasonings, do not be light in mind and unstable. For you were judged
to rule birds, not to be likened to birds. Do not then be puffed up, do not be
lifted above, do not think greater than earthly and human nature, do not be
inflated being praised, do not glorify yourself nor magnify your things, so
that you may not be reckoned with the birds, being lifted equally with those
and 39a being carried about here and there by the flight. Rule the reasonings
in you, so that you may become ruler of all the things that are. Thus the rule
given through the animals trains us toward ruling ourselves. For it is out of
place for one being ruled at home to be king of nations and for one being held
by a courtesan within the house to be publicly a patron of a city. One must,
arranging the things at home well first and having ordered the inner things
with good order, thus take the oversight of the outer and foreign, since the
account will be turned around by those being ruled that Physician, heal
yourself. Let us then first be eager to heal ourselves. For no one was ever
blamed for not having hunted a lion, but if he did not master anger, he was
mocked by all; so that the one who has not been able to prevail over wild
beasts is not yet worthy of blame, but the one who has grown weak to master
the troubling passion is altogether under condemnation, because that does not
contribute to the one deemed worthy of rational rule, but this is necessary
and owed.

And the Lord who arranged that these things be written, who also prepared our
small and weak tongue to converse with you as far as 40a this, who through our
weak mind has sown for you sufficient treasures in the brief shadows of the
truth, may he give you through the small things the great, through the few
seeds the completion of knowledge, and to us the full reward of the choice,
and to you a filled fruit of the enjoyment of the divine oracles; because to
him is the glory and the power unto the ages. Amen.
