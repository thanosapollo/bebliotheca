75a And God planted a paradise in Eden toward the east and set there the man
whom he had formed. Let us conceive, hearers, a planting worthy of God and a
paradise fitting the love of beauty of a craftsman of such a kind and of such
greatness. For in what was said before it is written, Let the earth put forth
herb of grass, fruit-bearing tree sowing seed, making fruit; and it was so.
But here again he says, And God planted a paradise in Eden. If paradise had
been filled from the common trees, it would have had its proper constitution
from the first generation of plants, and the trees later grown up in it would
not have needed an exceptional planting of God. But now the various kinds of
plants later artistically wrought in paradise by God himself stood as things
far more different both in kind and in taste than those previously put forth
from the earth of themselves, and this is made clearly known by the Scripture
itself. For it says, And God caused to spring up still from the earth every
tree pleasant to the sight and good for food. For as he deemed man worthy of
an exceptional forming, apart from the rest of the animals, so also he made
the dwelling-place of man 76a a work of his own hand, choosing a place
excelling the whole creation and unshadowed because of its height, wonderful
in beauty, secure in its situation, splendid in its preeminence over all and
in being lit by all the risings of the stars, bathed in air most translucent,
and having the temper of the seasons both most pleasant and most excellent at
once. There, then, God planted the paradise, where there is no violence of
winds, no excess of seasons, no hail, no fiery whirlwinds, no gales, no
thunderbolts, no winter frost, no damp of spring, no summer burning, no autumn
dryness, but a temperate and peaceful concord of the seasons, each adorned
with its own beauty and not plotted against by its neighbor. For neither did
summer, running ahead perhaps of its own time, spoil the beauties of spring,
nor again did the fruits of summer and of late autumn, often scorched
beforehand by disorder of the air, drip away and perish. But all the seasons
danced round that place in good order, intertwining and being bound with one
another evenly and without pain, each bringing as gifts at its own time the
things exceptional to itself and unassailed. 77a And the earth there
was fat and soft and wholly flowing with milk and honey and fit for every
fruit-bearing, watered with most fertile waters; and the waters were most
beautiful and sweet and exceedingly fine and transparent, having much delight
from the sight, and furnishing more of the beneficial than of the delightful.
First, then, he created the place itself worthy of receiving the plantings of
God; then he planted in it every kind of beauty of trees, granting to man at
once a most graceful sight and a most pleasant enjoyment. How shall I be able
to bring clearly before your sight the beauties of the fatherland from which
you have fallen, so that, having been duly pained at the loss of them, you may
at last be able to recall yourself from exile and run back to those sure,
delightful things, unmixed with the base and the painful? For there are here
too flowering meadows, most beautiful to the sight, but imagine in your mind
what is most graceful of these. How the rose is here hidden with the thorn,
having the unpleasant running together with the grace, all but bearing witness
to us that all the delights of this life, O men, have been mixed with the
painful. 78a For in truth none of human goods is unmixed, but at once grief
has been fixed beside every worldly gladness: to marriage, widowhood; to the
rearing of children, anxiety; to good offspring, loss; to high repute,
dishonors; to prosperity, damages; to luxuries, enfeeblements; to healths,
sicknesses. The rose is in flower, but it produces dejection in me; for as
often as I see the blossom, I am reminded of my sin, because of which the
earth was condemned to put forth thorns and thistles. Here, then, the grace of
the spring flowers is short-lived, failing us while we still long for it; for
we have not finished gathering before it has withered in our hands. There,
however, the blossom does not shine out for a short while and then fail, but
has a lasting and eternal delight, graceful and immortal the sight, unending
the enjoyment; the fragrance unsated, the fine color flashing out continually.
No blasts of winds distress them, no new moons wither them, no frosts choke
them, no flaming of the sun parches them; but a moderate breeze, furnishing a
fine and sweet air and blowing upon them lightly and gently, preserves their
grace unconquered by time and unfading. And the beauties of the plants are
themselves also worthy of the creating and the planting of the one who made
them: all that are of brushwood and all that are bushy, the single-stemmed,
the many-branched, the high-crested, those flourishing on every side, the
leaf-shedding, the ever-leaved, those that are stripped, the ever-blooming,
the fruit-bearing, the 79a barren; some serving for use, others contributing
toward enjoyment; all excelling in size and beauty, shady with branches,
teeming with fruits, abundantly filling for some one use and pleasure
together, for others another. For of each of these fruits there is much that
is graceful, and again more of the needful than of the delightful.
What speech will set before you the excess of that pleasure? For if you
compare the things there with the things here, you rather insult by the
comparison, the image falling far short of the truth of the original. All
things perfect, all things ripe, not grown little by little; for they do not
come from blossom to their prime by time, but have sprung up from the first
with their proper prime, and they are accomplishments of nature, not of human
contrivance. There too kinds of birds of every sort, adding by the bloom of
their plumage and the clearness of their voice a certain wonderful enjoyment
to the things seen; so that man feasts through all the senses, seeing some
things, hearing others, touching others, smelling others, tasting others. And
with the birds also spectacles of land animals of every sort, all tame, all
familiar with one another and hearing and speaking, easy to take in at a
glance. The serpent was not then dreadful, but gentle and tame, not heaving
wildly and crawling on the face of the earth, but standing high upon feet.
Just so mild also were all the remaining irrational animals, as many as are
now altogether untamed and implacable. There, then, God set the man whom he
had formed. He formed him elsewhere and led him in there. For as he first made
the lights and then set them in the firmament, so also he formed him dust
from the earth, and set him in paradise. Note that it is not said whom he
made, but Whom he formed. For what he made is that according to the image; and
this is bodiless. And the bodiless is not circumscribed by place. Nevertheless
the thing made also follows of necessity upon the thing formed, that is, the
soul upon the body. For the compound must exist together, but place belongs
principally to the body, and the soul is enclosed in place by following,
through its relation or mixture with the body, and not from its own nature,
that is, from the intellectual substance. Have I gladdened you by the account
of the delights of paradise, or have I pained you more by the comparison with
perishable things? For surely your mind, thinking high things beyond the
world, as having its citizenship in the heavens and looking toward the dignity
above, was considering the things of the promise and looking around to hear
something of the 81a goods promised to us, Which eye has not seen nor ear
heard nor have they risen upon the heart of man. Who then is the one who
recounts, if eye has not seen? If ear has not heard, how will yours receive
it? If they have not risen upon the heart of man, how will our mind contain
them? We therefore do not cast out the bodily things even as we seek the
spiritual. For as in the case of the law there was also the sensible, but it
made a type of the intelligible, so also we understand paradise both bodily,
and we allegorize it spiritually as well.
God planted a paradise in Eden toward the east. The names of the plants have
been passed over in silence, but the place in which he planted has been made
known, that is, in delight; for Eden is interpreted delight. Does he then name
delight that which is through the mouth, which is handed over to the belly and
whose end is the privy, and is this the grace from God toward man: filling of
the stomach and ease of the flesh and a pouring-out that produces
pleasure? Or is it not even pious to say this? For it is somehow a provision
for licentiousness and insolence and all the forbidden things together, to
fatten the body excessively and to drown the indwelling soul with the
multitude of foods, making it readily inclined toward every sin. Therefore one
must understand another delight worthy of God, so that the planting also may
be 82a reckoned fitting to the husbandry of God. What delight, then, can there
be that is fitting for saints? Hear the one who says, Delight yourself in the
Lord, and he will give you the requests of your heart. Since, then, the
variety of virtue is infinite and its beauty beyond contrivance, having taken
its beginning from the manifold wisdom of God, for this reason it is written
that God planted a paradise not toward the east, but Toward the easts. For
each of the plants planted by the Lord, adorned in its own light of virtue,
flashed out. There are The streams of the river that gladden the city of God.
And elsewhere it is named a torrent of delight, nourishing and increasing the
beauties of those intelligible plants, which also in what follows of the
present Scripture is called A river going forth from Eden to water the
paradise. In what was written before, then, it had been written that first
having filled the creation he thus brought man in, as it were, into rule and
lordship over it; but now the reverse: first he formed man and then planted
the paradise. And the reason is that there, in order to fill the house, he
brought in the master of the house at once, and so that he might not create
him poor and afterward grant him plenty; but here, having granted the
perfection that comes from progress, he created him elsewhere and afterward
settles him in paradise, so that, having thoroughly learned the difference
between the life outside and the manner of life in paradise, from the
comparison of both and from experience itself he might know the preeminence of
the beauty in it, and fear the damage of the falling-away. 83a And that you
may understand a planting worthy of a divine hand and work, remember the
things said in the Gospel by the Lord to the disciples, that You are the
branches, and my Father is the husbandman; clearly as having been planted by
him. Of this planting also are those planted in the house of the Lord, as it
is written, and flourishing in the courts of our God. And the same also in the
prophet: I planted a fruitful vine, all true. And Paul, the imitator of
Christ, ventures even to say, We are fellow-workers of God; you are God's
husbandry; I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase. And the
righteous man in David is likened to the tree planted by the outlets of the
waters, which will give its fruit in its season and its leaf will not fall
away; and again, The righteous will flourish as a palm tree; and A vine you
brought out of Egypt, you drove out nations and planted it.
Try, then, through such teachings to come to an understanding of that paradise
and yourself also to arrive at the rays of the divine light; where the light
of knowledge rises, where the paradise of delight has been planted. And even
if you understand some bodily place, capable of receiving the saints, in which
all who have shone on earth in good works dwell, enjoying the grace of God as
a worthy recompense and the true and blessed delightfulness, perhaps even so
you do not fall away from the fitting conjecture about it. There, then, the
good powers are rooted and firmly established, ministers of the saints, to
whom man was handed over, being newly born and needing much 84a joint
training toward perfection. In which is the company of the saints, in which
are the risings of the light, in which is the delight of the soul, there God
set the man. If, then, you are fleshly and think great the enjoyment of
sensible things, you have the sketch of that bodily paradise and the most
pleasant filling, and press toward these things as one who will find there an
unceasing supply of delight. But if you are spiritual and think things higher
than fleshly enjoyments, go up in mind to the beauties of the angels,
thoroughly learn the fruits of righteousness in them, behold as in a mirror
the river of God filled with waters, whose Streams gladden the city of God,
Whose craftsman and maker is God. This river, beginning from Eden, the one
watering the paradise, flows through that city. Having thoroughly learned all
these things in mind, glorify the maker of them, who has made goods so many
and so great for you and for your enjoyment and has deemed you worthy of such
glory, if you always turn toward him and understand, from whom and for whom
you were brought into being; because to him belongs the glory unto the ages of
ages. Amen.
