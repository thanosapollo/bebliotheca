So I have not missed both occasions. It is a gracious thing to receive and
greet a man coming home from travel abroad, especially one returning from the
very things we prayed for together; and there would be something too in
sending him forth with the song. You have granted that this occasion has come.
We received him, then, since the

occasion so brought us together. When a god has shown a beginning, says
Pindar, the straight path is to take up what lies before us. For you, then,
may you sail with a fair wind and on good terms, and may later news be
reported better still; and for us in turn, may we contend in our work with a
fair wind, and with rushing oar outstrip all the Greeks as far as we can. May
Asclepius our patron, and Zeus who is lord to allot all things, grant this
throughout. So much for that. I turn to another prelude, after Stesichorus. I
know that in a way I must fight shadows, for those to whom this will be spoken
have not come. So the speeches are almost wasted, and yet it is perfectly
clear that they will be spoken rightly and as they should. That fact itself
shows the fault is not ours, neither now nor ever, if I may say so with the
gods, but these men's habitual laziness in everything. Let us look at it as a
counter affidavit or an indictment. They say I do wrong because I do not keep
my declamations continuous. Everything would be in my power and under my
control, they say, and everyone repeats the same words: "if he wished." And if
he ever does wish, that is the charge. If you want a gracious speech to hear,
of the kind I give when I happen to be keeping festival, you are talking
nonsense. If you will bear with a man telling the truth, I do not think I
shall seem much to blame. This is no festival, and there is no great crowd of
strangers among whom you would take it hard to be shown up (I speak as if the
men were present). The frankness will be among fewer people than at the
Lenaea. I spoke with you about these things before as well, when the plague
was at its height, the god having commanded me to come forward. What I say now
will hold to the same judgment, so that you may know that I, even in the most
dangerous times,

did not think it right to be idle. There are others who care little for
speeches.

I used not to know what the saying meant, that the thieves bring a counter
charge. Now I think I do. When those who ought to come to the declamations and
the recitals skip them, and then complain as if nobody is speaking, how are
they not putting their own faults down to other men? It is as if people shut
their eyes while the sun is blazing clearly and said that what is in sight is
not there. These are the tricks of men who deceive themselves, though they
seem to deceive others. Hear, if you wish, how it is reasonable to think about
this, and how one must. I hold that the arts and the pursuits belong to those
who follow them as far as the practice goes. The end no longer belongs to
them. Every art has a double use: one for the man who does the work himself,
the other for those who use it. If a man leaves off his proper work, he is
answerable for that, because the person who wanted to use it has been blocked.
But if he does his own work as fully as he can, and the others stay away, it
is not the man doing what is needed who is answerable, I think, but those who
will not use him. What must the good doctor do? Keep ready the drugs, the
hand, the knowledge. Ordering people to use these is no longer part of the
art. The sick man must walk to him, or send for him. If the doctor should need
the man who needs to be saved, or, by Zeus, the pilot those who need to sail,
or the judge those who are in dispute, or the general the soldiers, or the
orator the hearers, everything would be rivers running backward. This was
never the rule even among the vulgar crafts, let alone in the serious arts
that have come so far in worth: that the artisans should go to those who need
them and beg to be used. Men who need a house need the carpenters; the
carpenters do not need them. A farmer will walk to the smith's door when he
needs a hoe or something of that kind, and the smith will go to him in turn
when he needs grain. Have you ever yet seen a barber come up to someone and
say, Best of men,

grant me to cut your hair, or to take off the nails, or to scrape, for this is
my art? Even if a man wears purple and is as solemn as you please, he goes to
that man himself, and even calls him to his house, whenever he needs something
of his.

Why stretch out a long catalog of arts? Almost everything, so to speak, stands
so. Then those who would not even claim to think themselves better than
anyone, nor, I think, even equal to the rest, but have serving the many as
their very art: these wait for those who will need them, and will not humble
themselves beyond their station. Will those who profess the fairest and most
liberal power, the power by which all affairs are examined and tested, small
and greater, fairer and the other sort, not imitate at least those men, if
they do nothing else? Will they not hold that the care and practice of
speeches belongs to themselves, but that using the speeches, or using the
speakers, is the work of others? If the cobbler and the weaver leave the work
they sit at
and go about collecting those who will come to them, they will not only fail
to do what falls to them; they will destroy their arts. If the orator leaves
the speeches and collects those who will hear, will he in this way best
counsel about his own affairs? No. In this way most of all, as I at least
would say, he will destroy what he has. If leaving one's post is shameful
everywhere and against the laws, an orator would leave his post most of all by
courting chance people and making the station of the art meaner than himself.
Consider whether this seems to you well ordered, or the conduct of people who
have left reason: that those eager to marry sit at the girls' doors, courting
them or their parents or some other of the kin, but that those who claim to
desire speeches neglect to court the orator himself and to try to persuade him
somehow to admit them into the partnership, and then

bring a charge and say that he does not wish it. Who, by the gods, what
Calchas or Mopsus prophesied this to you? You have not tried it yourself. Nor
can one say that those who pursue the other sciences or arts know well how
they must plan about them, but that those who live by speeches, alone of all
men, or most of all, will be ignorant. This is the fairest and greatest and
truest thing to say on behalf of speeches: nothing prevents a man who does
something else from knowing his art and still not knowing how he must plan
about it. Speeches, apart from the rest of their power, also teach how one
must approach them. That is reasonable. Those whose part it is to judge about
other things are not likely to be wholly ignorant about themselves.

Come then. These things being so laid down, look at my case. If you find me
idle, or making anything at all of more account than time spent on speeches,
now or formerly I mean, or rather if you find I have fallen wholly short of
any excess of eagerness and zeal in this, accuse and blame whatever you wish.
But if I have left no room even for a jest, perhaps, that he is speech
incarnate, and beyond this I have never done extra work except what is toward
the gods, and those things have been done and performed, if I may say it with
them, as carefully as our power allows, but have in no way been overdone: if
this is how things stand, cover your faces on both counts, you who neither
dare to use me and who moreover imitate the women. They say that whenever the
women themselves do nothing they should in their mind, they turn their own
faults and evils upon the husbands. Look then, examining as if these were
accounts from the beginning, as briefly as possible. The god granted me, while
I was still young, the use of teachers, and first reports from them that I
would surely come to be first.

From that time to this I have always given my leisure to studies and speeches,
so far as the body's need did not stop me, or seasons that brought misfortunes
greater than my purpose. Even in those we came through like some Odysseus,
clinging to the raft. We at least were not sailing forsaken, but under the
greatest and most humane of pilots, who kept the breezes so they would not
sink. Alone of the Greeks we know of, we did not take up speeches for wealth,
or glory, or honor, or marriages, or power, or any extra prize. We became
lovers of them purely, and the speeches honored us with what is fitting. For
others the company of boys is sweet; for some, to bathe as much as possible;
for some, to drink to fullness; horses and dogs have struck others out of
their wits. By Zeus, those who attend to play stand off from speeches, and so
do those in earnest about other things. For me speeches have all names and all
powers. I have set them as children and parents, as actions and rests, as
everything. I call Aphrodite upon these. These are my play and these my
earnest. In these I rejoice, these I greet, I go to their doors. I would have
still more to say besides, but I omit it so as not to seem vulgar. As for
glory, I thought thus: to receive it when it comes to meet me, and to cherish
it, for otherwise I would be an utter fool; but to busy myself about nothing
toward it outside the speeches themselves and a life lived rightly in accord
with them. If someone looking to these should wish to admire, well. If not,
let him be.

I have made as many public appearances as any I know of. Do not look far off,
if you wish: consider these present times. What festival or
gathering did we omit, so as not to adorn the city in public? And

this not from lack of glory. If I may say it with the gods, the glory has
become so great that one need not contend to get it. It is safer to keep
silent, to preserve what exists. To those eager to come together privately I
offered myself not only as a competitor, but also as one showing them fairly
what I thought would make them somewhat better. So be it. That is our side
and things like it. Now consider your own in turn against this, you wretched
lovers. You love so exceptionally, and you have believed you are the peak of
the Greeks, and you shout this in the gymnasia and in the marketplace. But you
have suffered (I will say nothing harsher: take heart) the same as the sons of
distinguished men. For those it is enough that their fathers are such, and
they themselves for the most part live in luxury. For you it is enough that I
am present and speak. Coming and taking part you have thought belongs entirely
to the strangers. Instead of walking to the recitals, most of you spend time
about the swimming pools, and then you wonder if some of the speakers escape
your notice. You do not wish, I think, to tell yourselves the truth: that men
in love with stones, hanging upon baths, honoring what they should not, cannot
know the occupations of speeches. You lay your own faults on me and slander
with fair speech, saying "if he wished," and such things, as if I were clearly
nodding off now. And yet speech answers speech, as Homer says, and a man must
expect to hear the sort of things he says. For one who claims to be an orator,
having taken an unfitting charge, not to use his own instruments is the
extreme of misfortune.

I, then, the gods being gracious, would not much say I fear the contests in
the speeches themselves. I fear the terrible rivals. Shall I say whom? These
men anointed with oil here, and the palm

they carry about: not, by Zeus, calling people together to recitals of
orators, as I once saw happen in my own case both about this Meropis, Cos, and
in Cnidus. You know they praise cisterns, and "Come here and moor your ship,"
and to those putting in they advertise such things and play host in such
things. When from one side the orator comes in to contend, and from the other
your flourishing rover arrives promising such things, it is no wonder, I
think, that the victory falls to that side, and that neither older man nor
younger readily wishes to be temperate. These are the things that trip up the
speakers. These, like a cloud or a darkening, overcast all the fair things
about education. Besides this the abominable sophists persuade you that
Homer's greatest possession too was this: to have been born son of Meles. So
everyone rushes in haste to his father to philosophize there. You cannot even
take these very facts to heart, if they speak the utmost truth: that not
even for Homer himself was it enough to have pitched tent by the banks of his
father, nor to swim alongside the fish who were his brothers, as these men's
account has it. He lived so austere a life that in many places he is clearly
content if he has plenty of the necessities. He accepted improvised baths, and
indeed for the benefit of bodies that were weary, as Plato says, and he did
not allow luxury further. What has put into all the greatest neglect of all
things is this indescribable corruption. Everyone, fearing he may be ruined
first, destroys himself by hoping. He sets all the most shameful things in the
rank of gain, and takes all that is good as loss and labor in vain, as if
unless he goes off to the majority ruined in both mind and body, there would
be no place for him there. But it was, I think, altogether the contrary. Since
such things have withdrawn, one should enjoy the

fairest things in life while it is possible, so that if we should be of the
portion that is saved, we might be saved among the fairest things, in studies
and speeches, and not roll about night after night and day after day, tuned to
the harmony of swine. And if not, everything one took in advance should at
least be gain. Or, by the gods, is it gain to bathe while living, which will
remain, I suppose, even after a man has died, but to enjoy while living the
speeches from which one must be barred after death, both speaking oneself and
being present for another, is this a disgust? To be with a courtesan is the
sum of happiness, but to be with the first man of the Greeks even a little
amounts to a curse? One must gratify the belly, from which most of the
diseases come, but whatever belongs to speeches must be taken as idle chatter
and a shadow, O good men who educate the inhabited world before yourselves? As
you think it right that I should wish to speak, so exhort yourselves to make
me wish to speak. If not, each of us will do his own. Even so I will not stop
speaking, so long as a god is gracious, and you will always be ignorant. These
things then, if you wish, are a defense; if you wish, a rebuke from goodwill.
If they will be said as both, still they have been said as true and profitable
for all to hear. Either then one must stop these customs, says Demosthenes, or
do not so readily slander Hippolytus and Bellerophon.
