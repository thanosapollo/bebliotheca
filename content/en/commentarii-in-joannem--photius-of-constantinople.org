* Commentaries on John
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

1 Jo 1, 1 "In the beginning was the Word": he says without beginning according
to time, and "the Word was with God," as not without beginning according to
cause. See how from both sides he props up and binds tight the right opinion
concerning the Son. Then since he named the Son Word and <again> Word, but the
Father God, lest having supposed the Father greater according to essence than
the Son you fall <from the right>, at the same time as calling him God he
immediately joined and proclaimed the Son also God. What then? Was the Father
not in the beginning, and how not? For if the Son who is in the beginning was
with God and the Father, how would he himself not also be in the beginning,
that is, timeless and without beginning? You see how from the Son's being
without beginning, that is timeless, we learn that the Father likewise is
timeless. But perhaps, he says, even if he was in the beginning and even if
the Son is without beginning and even if he was with the Father, still it is
not yet clear from these that the Father also is the timeless and co-unbegun;
for nothing prevents the Son both being in the beginning and being with the
Father, yet not being in the beginning toward the Father. Far from it, he
says; for this, he says, the Word who is in the beginning was also in the
beginning with the Father, so that from the Son's being timeless we are taught
that the Father also is timeless and co-unbegun, <as> the Master well said. 2
Jo 1, 1–2 "This one was in the beginning with God." He was turned and always
inclining toward the paternal cause, he says; for even if he has been begotten
from him, yet he did not stand apart from him. But consider a theology high
and complete. He said "in the beginning": he showed the timeless. He said "the
Word": he presented the passionless of the generation, and that just as
through the word among us the things under it are shown and known, so also
through the Word beyond mind we know the Father; for he who has seen the Son
has seen the Father, and no one can know the Father except him to whom the Son
may reveal. Well then he says: "in the beginning was the Word"; then lest you
think the Word has a private beginning split off from the paternal, he
immediately adds: and the Word, the timeless, the one passionless in
generation, was with God. He has him as cause, he is turned toward him. Then
again lest you think that he was in the beginning, but later took the Father
on as cause, he adds: "this one was in the beginning with God." This one, he
says, who is in the beginning, being timeless, both in the beginning and
timelessly had the Father as cause. And neither is the being of the Son older
than his being from the Father and being toward him, nor is the Father older
either than the Son or than the Son's having been begotten from him, that is,
neither than the existence of the Word nor than his passionless generation.
Through such words of the Spirit pre-sewing shut many mouths of the heretics,
through "in the beginning" shaming Arians and Marcellus and Photinus and
countless other heresies, through "the Word" those who dare to attach some
passion to the generation, through "and the Word was with God" Marcion and as
many as were so foolish as to call the Son another beginning and an anti-god
to the giver of the law and Father, through "this one was in the beginning
with the Father" both the aforementioned Arians and the offshoots later cut
from them as from a rotten tree, those split off from there who monstrously
claimed that the Father is always God, but not always Father, but became so
later; for if in the beginning the Word was with God, when was it that God was
not Father, when the beginning allows nothing before itself even to be
imagined? Through these striking every such blasphemy and sketching the
timeless and passionless and undivided toward the Father and co-eternal, and
that he is at once Word and at once Son, and that God is at once God and
Father, arranging and unfolding these things well and all-wisely, he uttered
somehow in the midst also what is gathered from these: "and the Word was God,"
he says, just as also the Father; for this had been said about the Father. He
was God not simply having the title, but such as the things said before about
him require one to conceive, the without-beginning according to


time, the passionless, the from such a Father, that is, consubstantial. The
Father was God toward whom the Son was, and God was the Word who was toward
the Father, and likewise he was both God and God likewise one God was; and he
was, for the Father is co-unbegun with the Son. And consider also how great
the evangelist's zeal to proclaim the Word God; for since he said Word, he
said also "with God"; lest someone, cataloguing the other properties as well,
suspect the Word something other besides God, he did not wait to speak the
particulars, but immediately cried out "and the Word was God," not enduring to
say anything else next after "and the Word was with God" except that "and the
Word was God." Then having spoken the thing aimed at, he again goes through
also the characteristic and proper things of deity contained in this. 3 Jo 1,
3–4 He says not only "all things came to be through him," but also if anything
has come to be. "In him was life," that is, the only-begotten Word of God, the
beginning and constitution of all things visible and invisible, the one
granting being and living and being moved in many ways to the things that are,
not going according to some partition and alteration into each of the things
standing apart by nature. And the life is one, going through all things toward
each as may befit it and as it can partake. 4 Jo 1, 4 "In him was life," that
is, he is a fountain of the life that is not corrupted nor dissolved. And if
of this, much more is he cause also of this perishable one that is dissolved
for what is expedient. But in whom was such life? Clearly in the Son; for he,
coming into the world, brought it to all men through faith in him and supplied
it. 5 Jo 1, 5 The darkness is either death or error or sin; for those who have
believed in him purely and become keepers of his commandments and through
these received the true life in themselves, neither does death prevail over
them; for "he who believes in me," he says, "even if he die, shall live." Nor
sin; for "you are not of the world"; nor even error, nor even affliction or
maltreatment; for "take courage," he says, "I have overcome the world." And if
those deemed worthy of the life supplied from there enjoyed so much light as
to have become stronger than every darkness and to scatter the gloom from
there, what must one think also about the very one who supplies the unending
and light-bearing life? 6 Jo 1, 6 "There came to be a man sent": instead of,
he came to be for this very thing, to have been sent ahead of the Word and to
proclaim the dignity of his deity; for it was not by the way or by some
coincidence or otherwise according to human ambition that he proclaimed what
he proclaimed about Christ, but as a debt and as having been born for this
very thing, to be sent and to be named his forerunner and herald. 7 Jo 1, 6–9
Since he said in a more human way "that he might bear witness about the
light," lest it seem to some that the Word gains something from John's
witness, he ran back again to the high and fitting and says: "and the true
light, which lights every man." It was, he says, it has no need of witness;
for it was light and is always, but the witness is for the sake of men, that
having believed and been led by the hand through the fellow-servant they may
be saved. The light was and lights every man, so that the light lights John
himself also; for John was a man sent. Therefore the light did not need his
witness, but as we said, he bears witness so that those who hear, having
believed, may be saved. 8 Jo 1, 10 This, shaming and stopping the mouths of
those who say the world is of the evil one; for the good would not have
endured to be in the product of the enemy and adversary. Wherefore also more
clearly destroying this suspicion, "and the world," he says, "came to be
through him"; for above he said universally: "all things came to be through
him"; but now also particularly this very world, which some, because of the
seeming irregularities in it, imagined of another, evil beginning, but not of
the good-making one. Whose unreason and shamelessness he clearly pilloried by
saying "and the world came to be through him." And "he was in the world" not
as contained or circumscribed in it, far from it, but as everywhere. Thus also
in the world as maker in the thing made, as craftsman in the


crafted work, as providing in the thing provided for. 9 Jo 1, 11 How did he
come into his own, he who is always everywhere and present in the world? As
appearing in flesh, as living with us bodily and being truly recognized as
man. And the world is his own as product and creation and as being governed
and provided for by him, and now still also as more beloved, because he even
deigned to become man for it, that is, for the man in it. 10 Jo 1, 12 "He gave
them authority" and the rest. What then? Did those before Christ's coming who
lived by the law not have authority to become children of God? Not indeed so
much, nor nearly. Hear Paul saying: "but when the commandment came, sin
revived, and I died." And "the law came in besides." And "the trespass
increased," he says. And "I did not know sin, except the law said: you shall
not covet." And "for apart from law sin is dead," so that the law was a
teacher of virtue and a turning away from vice, but it did not give grace and
cooperation toward fulfilling the commandments, so that they did not even have
so much authority except as much as from free will to become children of God.
On the contrary, from circumstance the law even hindered; for "sin, taking
occasion through the commandment, worked in me every desire," he says. And
again, that also from works alone was the salvation through law, but now there
is need of works too, yet the whole is of grace; for freely through baptism we
are adopted and become children of God from willing alone, so that unspeakable
and great is our authority to become children of God. But in the law not so,
but "the man who has done them, that one alone shall live in them." But now
everyone who wishes has received all authority to become children of God. Yet
after becoming so one must not shame the Father nor dishonor the one who
sonned him freely and as a gift and not, as the law, through works, but one
must also himself contribute something toward thanksgiving for so great a
benefaction, a life pure and blameless, and this when the practice is not even
hard, since grace also in the working of the virtues takes hold with us of the
greatest things and helps. So that in two ways the authority to be called and
become children of God has been lavished on us, both from being adopted freely
and as a gift and not from works, but from willing alone, and from finding
after the adoption, even toward the very working of the virtues, <power> from
grace. But those in the law were neither without works deemed worthy of any
gift or adoption, nor in the works themselves did they enjoy any alliance or
cooperation from the law. On the contrary they even found sin from there
rather revived and attacking them more fiercely, so that they had been
allotted only the authority from free will, and besides this none other. But
we have both this freed and the one from grace many times greater and higher
than this, so that he says wisely and reasonably "he gave them authority to
become children of God." And if having received such authority to be sons of
God we still serve the passions, what defense will be left us, or rather, what
punishment will not be worthy to be brought upon those who have set themselves
in such slackness and neglect as to choose, instead of being children of God,
to be named slaves of passions. 11 Jo 1, 12–14 And how is it possible, he
says, for men to become children of God? Do not wonder, he says; for the Word
also for this very thing deigned to become flesh and tabernacled among us, so
that he, having become man by economy, might make us sons of God by grace and
love of man. He became flesh, so that he was not seen in fantasy, but truly
became flesh and tabernacled among us, so that he was not turned into flesh
and became one nature, but having truly become flesh he kept both of what came
to be and of what he was before both the nature unchanged and the property.
"And we beheld the glory." Of whom? Of him, of that one, he says, not of them;
for he is one and the same according to hypostasis, even if the difference of
the natures is not abolished. 12


Jo 1, 15 He was always first, for he is by nature God and Son of God, but I a
slave and his herald. He was always first, but he comes after me in the time
of the generation according to flesh and in the supposition and opinion of you
many, but already to those worthy to discern him. And he has become before,
and still more and more and incomparably to all of them the majesty of the
deity will be revealed. 13 Jo 1, 17 "Grace and truth came to be through Jesus
Christ." "The grace and the truth," he says; for even the things of Moses, if
they were grace, were yet in types and shadow. But now the grace is not in
type, but grace itself and truth itself, so that he well says: the law through
Moses, but grace and truth came to be through Jesus Christ. 14 Jo 1, 18 Since
he said God the Father, God the Son, their without-beginning, the unspeakable
and beyond-mind relation toward each other, and that in the beginning the Word
was with the Father, since he let himself down into this high and wondrous
theology, lest someone say: man, high and wondrous are the things you say; but
from where did you get to know these things? Did you see God? But no one has
seen. From where did you learn these secret things? Who is the one who taught?
So that no one thinking such things may revolt in his reasonings, he himself
anticipating says: God naked and without flesh no one has ever seen. Then you,
from where having learned do you say these things? The only-begotten, he says,
whom I said before, that he is God, that light, that in the beginning, that
with the Father, that he lights every man, that one explained, that one
taught; for we beheld him with flesh, we saw his glory, glory as of a true
only-begotten. That only-begotten, who never stands away from the paternal
kinship and intimacy, but is always in the bosom of the Father, that one
revealed these things to us, and because of this we also boldly proclaim these
things. 15 Jo 2, 6–8 And that he may show that he himself is the creator of
the elements and he himself is the one when he wishes changing the water into
blood, at another time into wine, at another time <calling it forth> from
stones, and that as to one with authority and master of the all all things
yield to him and are easily recast toward the will. 16 Jo 2, 9–11 The master
of the feast calls the bridegroom as marveling at the beauty of the wine and
not having recognized the sign, but thinking that he had brought in that
fragrant wine. Wherefore he also says: "every man sets out the good wine" and
the rest. Thus the Master did the signs without display, so that when so great
a wonder was being accomplished, able to strike the whole inhabited world, he
did not even take care to be displayed to those spending time in the same
house. Yet nevertheless it did not escape; for "his glory was manifested," he
says, "and his disciples believed in him." Proof then of his wrapping himself
and not working wonders for display is that neither the master of the feast
nor the bridegroom himself had recognized it; for he would have answered
something, if he had seen it, to the master of the feast accusing, but he too,
as hearing something new, was silent and marveled at the word; and of the
greatness and brightness of the sign, that even being done thus it did not
escape; for he says: "this beginning of the signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee
and manifested his glory," not through what he displayed, but through what he
rather worked wonders without display. 17 Jo 2, 14–16 And this he did, so that
he might always have, even being silent, through a memorable deed to refute
the Jews, that they boast in vain of keeping the law and honoring the temple;
for those who made it a market and trade and showed it a workshop of oaths and
perjury and injustice and much other unseemliness (for these no less than the
wares are the tokens of the market): what other honor did they practice, or
what other care for its seemliness would they be trusted to have laid down
with longing? But where there was profit, you see them having shown their
renowned temple a market and emporium and no care or rebuke about this; where
they needed only to put on airs with empty words, avengers of the temple,
zealots of the ancestral and


figures of honor and great words, or rather where there is profit of a little
silver, the temple or the law is nothing; but where there is great salvation
of men, there the great envy and the covering of the envy the temple and the
law and the words of madness. So the Master, having cast them out of the
temple in deed and word and having shown them brightly and without doubt
despisers of the temple, raised a great stele against them, that if they
should speak for the law and the temple, they would not speak these things
from longing and faith, but either being puffed up or shadowing their own envy
or for their own gain, that they may be called rabbi, that they may widen the
phylacteries, that they may devour the houses of the widows and of the others.
18 Jo 3, 12–13 He said "if I told you the earthly things and you do not
believe"; and yet being earthly they ought more easily to be led by the hand
through things akin, but still if I told you these and you do not believe, how
if I tell you the heavenly things, you being earthly, will you believe; for
the heavenly things stand farther from your mind than the earthly. How then
will you learn the heavenly things not believing the things said by me? To go
up into heaven and learn is not possible for you; for no one has gone up into
heaven even until now, not of the prophets, not of those who otherwise lived
according to virtue, nor at all except me who have come down from there. If
then it is not possible for you to go up, and no other has gone up, but to me
who have gone up and come down and not been separated from there (for this is
what "who is in heaven" shows), if then you do not even believe me promising
and teaching the heavenly and divine and high doctrines, how otherwise will
you be able to learn these things? And he said these things driving Nicodemus
from every side into believing him, that unless one is born of water and
spirit, he will not enter into the kingdom of the heavens. And he more
strongly, "he shall not even see," he says, "the kingdom of the heavens." 19
Jo 3, 19 And this is the judgment, as so bright, so undoubted as all but even
being shown. This, he says, the judgment, as the condemnation of those who
disbelieved. And from where is it clear, from where conspicuous and bright and
admitting no dispute? From the thing itself, he says: "that the light came
into the world and men loved the darkness rather than the light." Jo 3, 23 And
that that one also was baptizing. He was not baptizing into the opposite of
the disciples of Christ, but rather confirming their things; for baptizing he
proclaimed Christ, that this is the bridegroom and the Son of God and the one
from heaven, so that baptizing he strengthened the faith in him just as also
the disciples of Christ. 23 Jo 4, 12–15 If when the Samaritan woman said "are
you greater than our father Jacob" the Master answered her: yes I am greater,
he would have pained the woman from seeming at once to cheapen the ancestor on
whom she thought much. And he would have given nothing great to suspect about
himself; for what is worthy of Christ, to be thought greater by comparison
with Jacob? Wherefore he does not say "I am greater" nor by comparison with
Jacob set himself before that one, but at once he shows his superiority by an
incomparable measure and does not himself say this dryly in the words, lest he
seem both to cheapen that one and to grieve the woman, but through what he
says he gives that very woman to reckon and conceive this; for what one
himself reasons out and thinks, he does not likewise hold burdensome as if he
should hear it from another. And how does Christ do this? From the difference
of the water, both what Jacob found for them and what he himself promised to
give. And he does not simply state their difference being unspeakable and
great, but also with a promise of providing, so that also from the desire of
receiving the difference may seem to them nothing unseemly. Wherefore also the
woman, who not yet endured him to be greater than Jacob, immediately raised to
a higher mind both hears the saying with good will and asks the water from
him, having despised what she had until then formerly admired of Jacob. Thus
then from the incomparable difference of the water she was also led by the
hand to the incomparable superiority of the persons


and advanced little by little, so as not only to confess Messiah and Christ,
but also to proclaim to the city and fatherland and to begin for them the
faith in Christ. 24 Jo 4, 16 What sequence has it, when the Samaritan woman
asks the living water, for the Master to say: "go call your husband"? Much
indeed; for since to receive that living water is not otherwise possible
except through faith in Christ, he says to her: "go call your husband," so
that through the woman's answer and through Christ's foreknowledge upon the
answer the woman, marveling, may recognize the Master and believe, and having
recognized and believed may be deemed worthy of the request, that is of
obtaining the living; for she learned from such converse that he is the
expected Messiah. She proclaimed to those in the city that he is the Christ;
for "is this perhaps the Christ" she does not say as doubting, and this is
shown from the great zeal and from calling the city together to the sight of
him, but she says "is this perhaps the Christ" so that, said straight and with
assertion, it may not strike some as offensive appearing from the first. She
proclaimed then that he is the Christ. She had through this what she sought,
living water that cleanses her from sin and cools from that inflammation of
the passions and sets her into eternal life. And from where did she obtain
these? Clearly from the Master's saying: "go call your husband"; for this
saying became a beginning of the woman's recognizing and believing that he is
the Christ, from which she also receives the living water. You see how, the
woman asking the living water, the Master says fittingly and very
sequentially: "go call your husband," all but saying: I will give it to you,
if only you well recognize who it is whom you ask; for when this happens your
request also will follow. And otherwise he also gives us a type not to
converse long with the woman without her own husband nor indeed without him to
grant her something she asks; for even if she did not have a lawful one, yet
among those who have, and everywhere, this must be guarded; for if the Master
of the all, the Lord of every breath, guarded this, what must those do who are
daily plundered by reasonings and unseemly passions? Because of this he
himself also, being sinless and passionless (for he did no sin nor was deceit
found in his mouth), yet for our example and type without the housemate he did
not endure either to converse more time or, according to the apparent and
ready sense of the word, to bring the woman's request to an end. 25 Jo 4, 19–
And I think that not even wishing in every way to show her own things
preferred to those of the Israelites, because of this she brings forward: "our
fathers worshiped on the mountain," but she produces what is thought to be the
confirming of the Samaritans' opinion. Then thus as from a prophet she sought
to learn from him the truer and more honorable of the opinions. Wherefore also
beginning she said: "Lord, I perceive that you are a prophet." 29 Jo 4, 23 And
"now is" signifies what will altogether and quickly come to be. 31 Jo 5, 19–27
So that if what the Father does, these the Son also does and especially
likewise, he is equal to him in all things. And "the Father loves the Son and
shows him and will show and has given all judgment," he says, to stop their
mouths, that he is not an anti-god; for if he loves the Son, he says, how
would he love the enemy? And how did he allow, and not only allows, but also
shows him to do the same things as if delighting in his works? And how did he
also entrust to him all the authority? And otherwise: just as when he says
that he gave him all the authority, that is the judgment, he does not put
himself out of the authority, but says this for the honor of the Son; for thus
men are accustomed rather to honor the one who has the authority to punish and
to save. As then he has said this for the honor of the Son according to human
reasoning, so also "he gave" has been said for the honor of the Father; for
they honor also the one who gives the authority. If then he had said that the
Son has all the judgment, the


for the Father judges no one, he would have given a suspicion that he himself
stood out of the authority. And because of this he sets "he gave," so that he
may honor the Son and the Father alike, the one as having given, the other as
having received all. Again if having said "he gave" he had not added "all,"
but said he gave to share the authority, he would have lowered the dignity of
the Son. But having said "he gave" and having added all the judgment and that
the Father judges no one, he showed equality of honor brightly. 32 Jo 5, 30
"As I hear I judge" some have taken, that teaching us in judgments to busy
ourselves with nothing more than the things appearing and said and <not> from
suspicions and human reasonings to harm and ruin those being judged, most
human reasonings being able to be dragged aside and swept away from the just
by friendship or enmity. Turning us away from this, they say, he said that "as
I hear I judge." And if I the knower of hearts thus, because of this very
thing, even in the things appearing and said make the judgment, busying myself
with nothing beyond these, neither must you busy yourselves with anything else
besides the things seen and appearing and said and trust your own reasonings
and commit to these the judgment of things unclear. Thus then some, not as it
seems to me. For first the Master appears in many places that he did not judge
as he heard, but as God knower of hearts, as when he said to the woman "you
have well said that you have no husband; for you have five," and when he said
"now you believe; an hour will come when you all will be scattered," and
countless others, and that no such aim even lay before him here, but about the
Father and himself and equality and authority and such things. What then is
"as I hear I judge"? As I hear, he says, from the Father I judge; that is, as
that one judges, so I also. And just as "he shows him" and "he will show him"
and "he gave him authority" we have taken in a God-befitting way, or rather
just as the sequence of the word trains and requires to take these in a
God-befitting way, so also "as I hear I judge" will be taken in a
God-befitting way; for since he said that an hour is coming in which all who
are in the tombs will hear not of the Father, but of the voice of the Son,
having said this high and God-befitting thing about himself, he immediately
added also what contributed to the honor of the Father, as: the Father has not
been deprived of the judgment, but I judging bring out the vote in accord with
him. 34 Jo 5, 31–34 Or rather one might say that "if I bear witness about
myself" and the rest is not by way of assertion nor as put forward from the
Master, but as from an objection of the Jews and brought forward by them; for
since the Master said many and high things about himself, that he who does not
honor him as the Father does not honor the Father either, that he who hears
his word does not come into judgment but has passed from death into life, that
the dead hearing his voice will live, and such things. Since then as he said
these the Jews were stung and were going to say to him: "you bear witness
about yourself, your witness is not true"; for this they have done also
elsewhere. Anticipating then this objection he shows them even on this to have
no argument, as: you will say to me, he says, that saying these things I bear
witness about myself and my witness is not true, but even this pretext of
yours has been cut away; for I am not the only one bearing witness about
myself, but also John, whom you marveled at and hold trustworthy. And why do I
say John? But also the Father himself through the Spirit bore witness, of
whose witness you have become hearers neither now nor of old, nor have you
acquired a right supposition about his blessed and undefiled essence. And here
by objection he refuted their supposed counter. And going on he also throws it
down by counter-objection saying: even if I alone bore witness about myself,
not even this ought to have been a pretext of unbelief for you; for it is not
as among men that the witness about themselves is false, but mine is most
wholly true. 35 Jo 5, 31–34 For if someone bears witness for himself, as far
as


into human supposition and judgment, his witness is not true. This then the
Lord also, making clear toward their supposition, said: if I alone, he says,
bear witness about myself, perhaps it was possible for us according to human
suspicions to disbelieve the witness as not being true. But now, he says, even
this pretext of yours has been cut through; for I alone do not bear witness
about myself, but also that one, whom you, having supposed trustworthy,
brought the inquiry, so that both from bringing John as witness and from
saying "you have sent" it is clearly shown that he brought the word thus
toward their supposition and weakness, and also what is added: "but I do not
receive the witness from a man; and these things I said," that is, because of
this I brought John forward to you into witness, that you may be saved, has
plainly presented the thought spoken, that he used the words thus toward their
supposition and weakness. 36 Jo 5, 32–33 And "I know that his witness is true"
instead of: and I know that not even to you would it be denied that his
witness is true, if indeed you do not fight with yourselves; for you, having
supposed him trustworthy, sent asking him. And he himself bore witness to the
truth, to the truth, he says, because even before John's witness he was truth
itself and did not need the witness of others. Therefore I know, he says, if
you are well-minded, that to you also his witness is true. 37 Jo 5, 36–39 As
