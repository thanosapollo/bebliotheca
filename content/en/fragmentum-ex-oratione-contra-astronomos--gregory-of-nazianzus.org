* Fragment from an Oration against the Astronomers

Of Saint Gregory the Theologian, from the discourse against the astronomers.
Just as the fishes that live in the waters are led into generation of life
through the humors and waters washed together in the womb, yet after the birth
it is not by expiration, but by inspiration and respiration of the life of the
air that every ensouled animal enjoys; and the bond of the soul is the body,
and the bond of the body is the blood, or rather the substantial heat of the
blood, which when it is cooled, the separation of the soul from the body takes
place, the blood being congealed. Whence the same-hour and same-day dead
bodies of human beings, when they are dissected, perhaps still have bile
within, and likewise also phlegm and humor, but blood in no way; for by the
failure, as I have already said, of the hot element the separation of the soul
from the body takes place. And for this reason most of the infants are born
also at night, the womb being cooled and driving out the infant through the
nocturnal cold; in the same way also most of the bodies die at night, the heat
of the blood being as it were driven out in turn and ... under the cold of the
night. And this then is the account of the separation of the souls.
But it is necessary that you also know the proper separation of the elements
of the body, so that you may hold the account of the resurrection in
certainty. For when the soul is separated from the body, the four elements
from which they came together ... in them and are carried away, the hot of the
blood in the fire as into its own deposit, the wet in the water, the cold in
the air; remaining then in the earth, that is in the tomb, is the dry,
=36.676= that is the dust that was taken from it by God from the beginning. At
the time of the resurrection then, as from certain deposits of the said parts
of the world, each element gives back to the dust what is its own, and again
the body is constituted from four, and the soul runs to it, and thus man is
formed again. And you will wish, as one who inquires into all things, to treat
this also in particular: I mean of the operations of the soul through the
body, of memory I mean and of reason, of desire and of anger, of laughter and
of breath, where these are, and through which parts they are operated. For the
memories and the discernments of these are operated by the soul through the
brain; wherefore often some who have received a mortal blow upon the head have
not died at once, but have become out of their mind and without understanding;
for this reason the hinder parts of the head are called metaphrena. And the
rational faculty the soul operates through the heart. And the respiratory
through the lung; and the irascible through the blood; whence one may often
see in those who are angry, at the hour of anger, their appearance bloodshot
and inflamed. And the concupiscible the soul operates through the liver;
whence when the liver is cooled, indigestion immediately following, the human
being has no appetite for foods. For observe that all greedy animals have a
complete liver; such as pigs become, and sparrows. And you will know again
that the smiling faculty the soul operates through the spleen; they are
ill-disposed toward laughter; and those who are often dragged about by many
who laugh =36.677= say they are spleened. And you will altogether wish to
understand also concerning the sperm-producing parts, through which and from
where they proceed, which are the kidneys; whence also those skilled in such
things are fond of saying that those who are too unstinting concerning
intercourse are bent down by the pain of the kidneys.
Now those who have inquired must know what is unclear to the many, that a soul
torn away from the body and from the numbered parts of the body, and being
naked by itself in fleshlessness and ...ness, neither remembers any longer,
nor cultivates, as he testifies who said, that In that day all his reason...
(perhaps reasonings) shall perish. And you will inquire, O wise one, when you
have come to the place, carefully the account concerning ... (perhaps the
soul). For this reason also it is both incomprehensible to the many and
insoluble ... both how a bodiless soul is separated from the body, and in the
separation of the soul death does not take place; for there is one and only
separation of soul from body. And consider this with all things: from where
and for what reason some of human beings by nature, and not from any training,
from their very young infancy some are sharp, some gentle, some lovers of
pleasure, and others temperate, and others quiet, but others turbulent; and
some prudent, some more simple, and some deceitful, some foolish, and many
other things. Greeks. The uneducated of the philosophers, who have fabled the
much-talked-of astrology of the lofty =36.678= stars, and have dreadfully
dogmatized fate, have dreadfully dogmatized that our affairs are steered
altogether by the administration of the stars and not by God.
But those who have scientifically grasped the well-understood and God-given
qualities and the true substantial ones concerning the human being's natural
gentleness and sharpness, softness and terribleness, of temperance I mean and
of incontinence and of all such natural advantages, have said wisely thus:
that not from one and only manner do such natural opinions and constitutions
come upon nature and are born together with it, but also from a second and
third and fourth and fifth and sixth and seventh cause; and first from the
parental likeness of the fathers, second from the difference of the elements
of daily and nightly motion. For know, O philosopher, that three motions from
the intervals of the three hours belong to the elements; from the first
daytime hour until the third the bile being moved, as the eyes of some often
bear witness at dawn, appearing .....; and from the third until the sixth,
with the heat of the sun, ..... motions and boilings of blood, sharper and
more bitter, but more ..... after food; and from the sixth hour until the
ninth, the motion of the ... is accustomed to take place, and thus then that
of the humor until evening.
