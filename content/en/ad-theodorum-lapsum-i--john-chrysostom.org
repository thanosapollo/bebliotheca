* To Theodore After His Fall I
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

To Theodore who has fallen, and concerning repentance, discourse 1.
1 "Who will give water to my head and a fountain of tears to my eyelids?"
It is timely for me too to say this now, and much more than for that prophet
then. For even if I am not about to mourn many cities nor whole nations, yet
a soul worth many such nations, or rather even more precious. For if "one
who does the will of God is better than ten thousand transgressors," you
too then were formerly better than the myriads of the Jews, so that no one
would blame me now if I should even write down laments more numerous than
those lying in the prophet and display more vehement wailings. For I do not
mourn a city's demolition, nor a captivity of lawless men, but a desolation
of a holy soul and a pulling-down and disappearance of a Christ-bearing
temple. For the ornament of your mind, which the devil has now burned up, if
anyone knew it well when it shone, would not have groaned going through the
laments of the prophet, and hearing that barbarian hands both defiled the
holy of holies and, sending fire, burned everything, the Cherubim and the
ark, the mercy-seat, the stone tables, the golden jar. For this, this
misfortune is so much more bitter than that, by as much as far more precious
symbols than those were stored in your soul; this temple is holier than
that; for it did not gleam with gold and silver but with the grace of the
Spirit, and instead of the Cherubim and the ark it had Christ and his Father
and the Paraclete established in itself. But now no longer, but it is desert
and stripped of that beauty and seemliness, having been stripped of the
divine and unspeakable ornament, and desert of all safety and guard. And
there is neither door nor bar, but it is opened to all soul-destroying and
shameful thoughts; and if that of arrogance, or that of fornication, or that
of love of money, or those more defiled than these, should wish to enter in,
there is none who will hinder; but formerly, just as heaven is untrodden by
all these, so also was the purity of your mind. And perhaps I shall seem to
some to say things unbelievable, to those who now see your desolation and
overthrow; for this I beat my breast and mourn, and doing this I will not
cease, until I see you again in your former brightness. For even if this
seems to men to be impossible, yet with God all things are possible. For he
is "the one who raises the poor from the earth, and lifts the needy from the
dunghill, to seat him with princes, with the princes of his people." He is
"the one who settles the barren woman in a house, a mother rejoicing over
children." Do not then despair of the best change. For if the devil was
strong enough to bring you down from that summit and height of virtue to the
last of wickedness, much more will God be able to draw you up again to that
confidence; and not only such, but also to make you much more blessed than
before. Only do not fall down, nor cut off the good hopes, nor suffer the
things of the ungodly. For it is not the multitude of sins that is
accustomed to cast into despair, but having an ungodly soul. For this
Solomon did not simply say that "everyone who has come into a depth of evils
despises," but "the ungodly" only. For this passion belongs to those only,
whenever they come into the depth of evils. And this is what does not let
them look up and return from where they fell. For this defiled thought, like
some wooden collar, lying upon the neck of the soul and forcing it to bend
down, prevents it from looking up to its Master. And it belongs to a noble
and admirable man to crush this wood and the


executioner who set it on him, and to utter the words of the prophet: "As
the eyes of a maidservant toward the hands of her mistress, so our eyes
toward the Lord our God until he have pity on us. Have mercy on us, Lord,
have mercy on us, for we have been much filled with contempt." These are
truly divine teachings and doctrines of the highest philosophy: "We have
been filled," he says, "with contempt" and have undergone countless hard
things; but nevertheless we will not stand off from looking up to God, nor
before receiving the request will we cease asking him. For this belongs to
a noble soul, not to fall down nor to give up before the multitude of the
terrible things that hold it, nor, asking often and failing, to withdraw,
but to remain "until he have pity on us."
2 For this the devil casts us into the thoughts of despair, that he may
cut off the hope toward God, the sure anchor, the foundation of our life,
the guide of the road that leads up to heaven, the salvation of perishing
souls. "For by hope," he says, "we were saved." For this, this, like some
golden chain hung from the heavens, bears up our souls, drawing little by
little toward that height those who hold it strongly, and lifting us above
the swell of the evils of life. If then someone in the midst, having grown
slack, lets go this holy anchor, he has at once fallen and been drowned,
having come into the abyss of wickedness. Knowing these things, the wicked
one, whenever he perceives us ourselves weighed down by the conscience of
wicked deeds, coming on he too lays on the thought from despair, which is
heavier than lead; and if we receive it, it is necessary thereafter, having
been dragged down at once by the weight, and having been broken off from
that chain, to go down into the depth of evils, where you too have now
come. Having left the commands of the meek and humble Master, and
accomplishing all the orders of the cruel and implacable tyrant, the enemy
of our salvation, and having broken the good yoke, and having thrown off
the light burden, and having put around yourself instead of those the iron
collars, you have besides hung the millstone of an ass from your neck.
Where then will you still stand, drowning the wretched soul, having laid
upon yourself such a necessity of being borne downward continually? The
woman, then, who found the one drachma, called the neighbors as partners of
the joy, saying "Rejoice with me"; but I will now call all the friends, both
mine and yours, upon the opposite, not saying "Rejoice with me," but "Mourn
with me, and take up the same lament, and cry out with us a wailing song.
For a last loss has seized us, not that so many and so many talents of gold
fell from my hand, nor that a great number of precious stones, but that the
one more precious than all these, sailing with us this great and wide sea,
I do not know how, having slipped, has fallen into the very bottom of
destruction."
3 And if some try to lead me away from lamenting, I will utter the things
of the prophet toward them, saying: "Leave me, I will weep bitterly; do not
prevail to comfort me." For I do not mourn now such a mourning as to bring
us even a condemnation by the excess of the wailings, but one over which,
even if Paul, even if Peter were, they would not have been ashamed weeping
and mourning and lamenting and thrusting away every comfort. For those who
wail this common death one might reasonably charge with much smallness of
soul; but when instead of a body a dead soul is set forth, having countless
wounds, and in the very deadness displaying its former good nature and the
good condition and the beauty that has been quenched, who is so cruel and
unfeeling as, instead of laments and wailings, to bring consoling words.
For just as there not to mourn belongs to philosophy, so here it is to


mourn. He who reached toward heaven, he who laughed at the vanity of life,
he who looked at the beauties of bodies as those in stones, he who despised
gold as mud, and all luxury as mire, this one of a sudden, seized by a
fever of unseemly desire, has destroyed both the health and the courage and
all the bloom, and has become a slave of the pleasures. This one then shall
we not weep, tell me, and beat our breasts, until we raise him up again?
And where is this of a human soul? For of the deadness of the body the
release cannot be found here, and yet not even this stands the mourners off
from the laments; but a deadness of soul it is possible to make disappear
here only; "For in Hades," he says, "who will confess to you?" How then is
it not of much stupidity that those who mourn the death of the body do this
so vehemently, and this knowing that they will not raise the one lying by
the wailings; but we display nothing such, and this knowing that there is
often hope to bring the lost soul back to the former life? For many both
now and in the time of our forefathers, having been turned aside from the
straight path and having been dashed down from the narrow entrance, so rose
again, as to hide the former things by the second, and to take the prize
and bind on the crown, and to be proclaimed with the victors, and to be
numbered into the choir of the saints. For as long as one stands in the
furnace of the pleasures, even if he have countless such examples, the
thing seems to him to be impossible; but if he take only a small beginning
of the exit from there, going forward he always leaves the more vehement of
the fire behind, and he will see the things in front and before the feet
full of dew and much ease; only let us not despair, nor give up the return;
for he who has suffered this, even if he have acquired countless strength
and eagerness, has acquired it in vain. For having once shut the door of
repentance against himself, and having walled off the entrance into the
stadium, how will he be able, remaining outside, to work either a small or
a great good? For this the wicked one contrives everything, that he may
plant this thought in us; for he will no longer need sweats and toils
toward our resistance; how so? of those lying and fallen and not willing to
rise against him. For he who has been able to dissolve this bond will both
recover his own strength, and will not cease wrestling with him to the last
breaths, and even if he fall countless falls, he will rise again, and will
crush the enemy; but he who has been bound by the thoughts of despair and
has paralyzed his own strength, how will he be able to prevail and stand
against, having fled from the opposite?
4 And do not tell me those who have sinned a little, but let there be
someone filled with all wickedness, and let him do all the things that shut
him out of the kingdom, and let this one be not of those unbelieving from
the beginning, but of the faithful, and of those who formerly pleased God,
and later let him become a fornicator, an adulterer, a soft one, one
who lies with males, a thief, a drunkard, a reviler, and all the other things
like these; for not even this one will I accept despairing of himself, even
if he come to last old age with the unspeakable and so great wickedness.
For if the wrath of God were a passion, one might well despair as no longer
able to quench the flame which he kindled through so many evils; but since
the divine is without passion, even if it punish, even if it take
vengeance, it does not do this with wrath, but with much loving-kindness
and care, one must be very confident and trust in the power of repentance.
For even those who have sinned against him he is not accustomed to pursue
for his own sake; for no harm passes over to that nature; but looking to
what is profitable for us, and that our distortion may not become worse,
when we practice despising and overlooking him. For just as he who makes
himself outside the light has harmed that nothing, but himself the
greatest things, having been shut in darkness, so also he who to despise
that all-powerful power


has grown accustomed, has harmed that nothing, but himself the last harm.
And for this God both threatens the punishments and often brings them on,
not as avenging himself, but drawing us to himself. For even a physician at
the insults of those raving does not feel pain nor is stung, yet he does
and contrives everything that those may cease behaving so unseemly, not
looking to his own things, but to their benefit; and if they display some
small bit of sobriety and wakefulness he both rejoices and is glad and lays
on the medicines much more vehemently, not defending himself against them
for the former things, but wishing to increase this benefit, and to bring
them back to pure health; so also God, when we fall out into the last
madness, not defending himself for the former things, but wishing to
release us from the disease, both says and does everything; and this it is
possible even from right reasonings to see.
5 And if someone should dispute with us for these things, we will confirm
them also from the divine oracles. For who, tell me, has become more
defiled than the king of the Babylonians, who, having received such a trial
of the power of God as to worship his prophet and to command that an offering
be poured to him and incense, was again carried out into the former pride,
and threw those who did not prefer him to God bound into the furnace. But
yet he calls even the one so cruel and ungodly and a beast rather than a
man to repentance and gives him also other occasions of changing, first the
miracle itself that happened in the furnace, and after that the vision
which the king saw, but Daniel interpreted, enough to bend even a stony
soul; and besides these, after the exhortation from the facts, the prophet
himself also advised saying: "Therefore, O king, let my counsel be pleasing
to you; redeem your sins by almsgivings, and your lawlessnesses by
compassions of the poor; perhaps there will be long-suffering for your
transgressions." What do you say, O wise and blessed one? Is there after so
great a fall again a return? is there after so great a disease again
health? is there after so great a madness again hope of sobriety? The king
deprived himself beforehand of all defense, first by not knowing the one
who made him and brought him to that honor, although he had proofs of his
power and providence to tell, both in his own case and many that had
happened in the case of his forefathers; and after this again, having
received clear signs of his wisdom and foreknowledge, and having seen magic
and astronomy and all the scene of the devilish sorcery overthrown, he
displayed things harder than the former. For what the wise magi Gazarenes
were not able to make clear, but confessed to be greater than according to
human mind, these, having made a captive child solve for him, he was so
won over by that miracle that not only he believed, but also became a
herald of this doctrine to the whole inhabited world and a clear teacher;
so that even if before receiving such a sign he was unpardonable not
knowing God, much more after that wonder and his confession and the
teaching that had been made toward others. For he would not, unless he had
believed well that God alone is true, have displayed so great honor toward
his own servant, and have legislated such things for the others. But yet
after such a confession he fell out again into idolatry, and he who fell on
his face and worshipped the servant of God, this one was so bacchic with
madness that he threw the servants of God who did not worship him into a
furnace. What then? Did he pursue the apostate as one ought to pursue? He
rather provided him greater proofs of his own power, after so great
senselessness drawing him again to the former things. And the more
wonderful thing: for that the things happening might not be disbelieved
through the excess of the wonders, he did not make the sign in some other,
but


upon the furnace, which he himself kindled against the holy children, whom
he bound and threw in. It was then even the quenching of the flame
wonderful and paradoxical; but the loving-kind one, that he might put in a
greater fear, and make the astonishment more, and dissolve all his
hardness, did the thing greater than this and more paradoxical. For having
let it be kindled as much as that one himself wished, thus he displays his
own power, not destroying the devices of the enemies, but making them
standing of no effect. And that no one seeing them having prevailed over
the flame might think it to be a phantom, he allowed those who threw them
in to be burned, showing that what was seen was truly fire; for it would
not have eaten naphtha and pitch and vine-twigs and so many bodies; but
nothing is stronger than his command, but the nature of all things follows
the one who brought it from not being into being; which then he also
showed. For the flame, having received perishable bodies, held off as from
imperishable ones and gave back the deposit sound, and with much
brightness. For just as kings from some palaces, so those children came
forth from the furnace, no one thereafter enduring to look at the king, but
all having transferred their eyes from him upon the paradoxical spectacle,
and neither the diadem, nor the purple, nor any other of the royal display,
so turned the crowds as the sight of those children, who had lingered in
the fire, but had come out from there as those who had suffered this in a
dream were likely to. For even that which was most easily broken of all
things in us, the nature of the hairs, this stronger than adamant mastered
the all-devouring flame.
6 And this was not the only wonderful thing, that having been taken in the
midst they suffered nothing, but that they were also speaking through the
hymns throughout; and all know, as many as have been present with those
burning, that as long as they have the lips closed, they hold out at least
a little toward the burning; but if it happen that they open the mouth, at
once the soul flies from the body. But yet, so many wonders having
happened, and all having been struck, both those present and watching and
those absent and having learned this through letters, the king who taught
the others remained uncorrected and again returns to the former wickedness.
And not even thus did he punish him, but he still was long-suffering; and
through a dream and through the prophet advising him. And when he became
better by none of these, then he brings the punishment upon him thereafter,
not defending himself for the things already done, but cutting off the
future terrible things, and checking the wickedness into the front, and not
even this to the end, but having disciplined him in few years he gave him
back again to the former honor, having lost nothing from the punishment,
but having gained the greatest of all goods, to hold firmly the faith
toward Christ and to repent of the former things sinned by him. For such is
the loving-kindness of God; he never turns away a repentance that happens
sincerely, but even if someone come to the very bound of wickedness, and
from there choose to turn back again toward the road of virtue, he both
receives and admits this one, and does everything so as to bring him back
to the former state. And the thing much more loving-kind than this: for
even if someone not display all the repentance, he does not send away even
the small and that which has happened for a little, but even of this he
sets a great reward; and this is clear from what Isaiah the prophet says
somehow thus about the people of the Jews: "For a sin I grieved him a
little and I struck him and I turned my face from him, and he was grieved
and he went gloomy, and I healed him and I comforted him." And that most
ungodly king would also bear witness to us, who was given over to the sins
by the woman, but when he only mourned and put on sackcloth and condemned
the things trespassed by him, thus


he drew the mercy of God, so as to release him from all the evils that hung
over him. For God said, he says, to Elijah: "Have you seen how Ahab was
pricked from my face, because he wept from my face, I will not bring the
evil in his days." And after this again Manasseh, having surpassed all in
madness and tyranny, and having overturned the legal worship, and having
filled the temple of the idols, and having made the error bloom, and having
become more ungodly than all before, when he later repented, was ranked
among the friends of God. And if, looking to the greatness of his own
lawlessnesses, he had despaired of the return and the change, he would have
fallen from all the things which he later obtained; but now, instead of the
excess of the things sinned by him, looking to the infinite of the
compassion of God and having broken the bonds of the devil he both rose and
contended and finished the good course. And not only through the things
that happened to these, but also through the words of the prophet God cuts
off the thoughts of despair, saying thus: "Today if you hear his voice, do
not harden your hearts as in the provocation." And the today can be said in
all the life, and even at old age itself, if you wish; for repentance is
judged not by quantity of time but by disposition of soul. For the
Ninevites did not need many days, so as to wipe out that sin, but a small
season of a day was able to make all their lawlessness disappear; and the
robber too did not accomplish the entrance into paradise in a long time,
but as much time as one would spend uttering one word, in so great a turn
having been released from the things sinned in all the life he also
received the prize of good repute even before the apostles. And we see the
martyrs too, not in many years, but in few days, and often even in one
only, putting on the bright crowns.
7 So that we have need of eagerness everywhere and of much choice; and if
we so prepare the conscience, as with such vehemence both to hate the
former wickedness, and to choose the opposite road, with as much as God
wishes and requires, we will have nothing less from the times; many at any
rate having become last so overshot the first. For it is not the falling
that is hard, but having fallen to lie and not to rise, but being
willfully bad and slack, to hide the weakness of the choice by the words of
despair. Toward whom also the prophet, being on fire, said: "Does not he
who falls rise, or he who turns away not return?" And if you should seek
from us some of those who after believing fell out again, these things too
have been said for these; for he who fell was formerly of those standing,
not of those lying; for how would such a one fall? but other things also
will be said, some through parables, some through clearer deeds and words.
For that sheep too, which was split off from the ninety and nine, then
again brought back by the good shepherd, shows us nothing other than the
fall and the return of the faithful; for it was a sheep, and itself, not of
some other flock, but of the number of the rest, and it was formerly fed
under the same shepherd, and it wandered a wandering not ordinary, but upon
the mountains and the glens, that is a long road and far from the straight.
Did he then let it, having wandered, be lost? By no means, but he brought
it back not driving, nor whipping, but having taken it up upon the
shoulders. For just as the best of the physicians bring those sick with a
long disease back to health with much treatment, not only treating them
according to the law of the art, but there is where even granting; so also
God does not lead those greatly corrupted to virtue with much vehemence,
but gently and little by little and bearing them up everywhere, so that the
split may not become more, nor the wandering longer. And not this parable
only has this


hinted to us, but also that of the prodigal son. For this one too was not
some alien, but a son, and a brother of the child who had pleased, and he
ran aground into a wickedness not ordinary, but into the very bound, as one
might say, of the evils, the rich and free and well-born having been
disposed more wretchedly than household servants and hired men and
strangers. But yet he came back again to the from the beginning and
recovered the former glory. And if he had despaired of his own life and,
having given up at the things that had happened, had remained upon the
alien land, he would not have obtained the things which he also obtained,
but having been destroyed by famine he would have undergone the most
pitiable death of all; but since he repented and did not despair, after so
great a corruption he comes back again upon the former brightness and puts
on the fairest robe and enjoys greater things than the brother who did not
fall down; "For so many years," he says, "I serve you and I never passed by
your command, and you never gave me a kid, that I might be glad with my
friends, but when this your son who ate up your living with harlots came,
you sacrificed for him the fatted calf." So great is the strength of
repentance.
8 Having then these examples, let us not remain with the evils, nor
despair of the reconciliation but let us say, we too: "Let us go to the
father," and let us draw near to God. For he never turns us away, but we
are the ones who make ourselves far; "For God," he says, "I am a God
drawing near and not a God from far." And through this same prophet again,
charging them, he said: "Do not your sins stand apart in the midst of me
and you?" Since then this is what makes us far from God let us take away
this hard barrier, and there is nothing hindering us from becoming near.
And hear this also having happened from the facts themselves. Among the
Corinthians a notable man worked such a sin, which was not even thought
among the nations. And this one was faithful, and of those made kin to
Christ; and some say he was even of those consecrated. What then? Did Paul
cut him out of the hoped-for portion of those being saved? By no means; for
he is the one who countless times up and down charges the Corinthians that
they did not set him into repentance; and wishing to show us that there is
no sin which cannot be healed, he said again about this one who had sinned
things harder than the nations: "Deliver such a one to Satan for
destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of our
Lord Jesus Christ." But this was before the repentance; and when he
repented: "Sufficient to such a one," he says, "is this punishment which
was by the more." And he wrote to comfort him again, and to admit the
repentance, so as not to be overreached by Satan. And a whole nation too,
that of the Galatians, after believing and working signs and having borne
many trials through the faith toward Christ having fallen down, he raises
again. For that they worked wonders, he made clear saying: "He then who
supplies the spirit to you and works powers in you," and that they also
contended much for the sake of the faith, this too he showed saying: "Did
you suffer so many things in vain, if indeed even in vain." But yet after
so great an advance they sinned a sin enough to alienate them from Christ;
about which he himself so declares saying: "See I Paul say to you that: if
you are circumcised, Christ will benefit you nothing," and again: "You who
are justified in law, you have fallen out from the grace." And after so
great a fall yet he admits them saying: "My little children, whom I again
travail until Christ be formed in you," showing that even after the last
corruption it is possible again from above for Christ to be formed in us;
"For he does not will the death of the sinner, as that he turn and live."
Let us turn then, O dear head, and let us accomplish the will of God. For
for this he created us and brought us into being, that he might make us
partake of the eternal goods, that he might grant the kingdom of the
heavens, not that he might throw us into Gehenna and give us over to the
fire; for this was not through


us, but through the devil it has happened; but the kingdom has been made
ready for us and prepared of old. And showing both these he said to those
on the right: "Come," he says, "you blessed of my Father inherit the
kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world." And to those on
the left: "Go from me you cursed into the eternal fire, the one prepared,"
no longer "for you" but "for the devil and his angels." Thus Gehenna has
not happened through us, but through that one and his angels; but the
kingdom has been prepared for us before the foundation of the age. Let us
not then make ourselves unworthy of the entrance of the bridechamber; for
as long as we are here, even if we sin countless things, it is possible to
wash all, having displayed a repentance worthy of the things sinned; but
when we are led away there, not even if we display the most vehement
repentance, will there be anything more thereafter, but even if we gnash
the teeth, even if we beat our breasts, even if we entreat countless
things, no one will drip even with the tip of the finger upon us burning,
but we will hear these things, which that rich man also then, that: "A
great chasm has been fixed between us and you." Let us sober up then here,
I entreat, and let us know our own Master, as one must know. For then only
must one despair of the hope from repentance when we come to be in Hades;
for there only this medicine is weak; but while we are here, even if it is
applied in old age itself, it displays much strength. Wherefore also the
devil moves everything that he may root the thought of despair in us; for
he knows that, even if we repent a little, we will not do this without
reward, but just as he who has given the cup of cold has the recompense
laid up, so also he who has repented of the terrible things he did, even if
he not display the repentance worthy of the sins, will have the return even
of this itself. For nothing at all of the goods, even if it be small, will
be overlooked by God, the just judge. For if the sins are examined with
such exactness, so that we pay punishments even of words and of thoughts,
much more the right deeds, even if they be great, even if small, will be
reckoned to us at that time. So that even if you cannot come back again to
the exactness itself, but if you stand yourself off at least from the
present disease and wantonness a little, not even this will be unprofitable;
only set a beginning to the thing, and open the entrance into the contests;
but as long as you remain outside reasonably this appears difficult and
impassable to you. For before the trial, even if the things are very light
and bearable, yet it is accustomed to furnish us an appearance of much
difficulty; but when we come to be in them through trial and take courage,
the more of the agony stands off, and the being confident, having come in
instead of trembling and despairing, lessens the fear, but increases the
ease and makes the good hopes stronger.
9 For this also the wicked one led Judas away from here, that he might not,
having taken the fitting beginning, return through repentance from where he
fell. For I, even if the thing said is paradoxical, would not say even that
fall to be greater than the help that comes to us from repentance. Wherefore
I beg and entreat, having banished all the satanic thought from the soul,
to come to this salvation. For if I were commanding you all at once and
together to go up again to that summit, you would reasonably be displeased,

who has dared himself; and others having fallen from the vehement despair

who are sick? but yet even of this he gives back to us a great recompense.
Therefore take hold of the eternal life, take luxury of the Lord, and
supplicate him; again take up the good yoke, come under the light burden,
set an end worthy of the beginning, do not overlook so great a wealth
having flowed away. For if you remain provoking God through the things you
do, you will lose him all; but if before the loss becomes much and all the
farm is flooded, you block the channels of wickedness, and


you will be able to recover the things corrupted again, and to add to them
another bearing not small. But having reckoned all which things shake off
the dust, rise from the earth, and you will be fearful to the antagonist.
For he himself has thrown you down, as one who will not rise thereafter; but
if he sees you again raising the hands against, having been struck
unexpectedly, he will be more hesitant toward tripping again. What do I
say? And you yourself will be more secure toward no longer taking such a
wound. For if the misfortunes of the others are enough to teach us, much
more those which we ourselves have undergone. Which I also expect soon to
see upon your head, and again by the turn of God you to become brighter and
to display so great the virtue, as even to stand before others there; only
do not despair, nor give up. For this I will not cease writing to you both
beyond every word and wherever I see and wherever I go even waking; and if
you hear this, you will no longer need other medicines.
