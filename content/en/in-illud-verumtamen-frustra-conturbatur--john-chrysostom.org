* On that saying, Nevertheless every living man is troubled in vain
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

On the prophetic saying that says, "Nevertheless every living man is troubled
in vain"; and concerning almsgiving.

=55.559= 1. A deep gulf also urges on the fisherman, when, having cast the net
and enclosed a great catch of the creatures of the sea, he draws in the seine
that labors under the load; and a wood that feeds wild beasts urges on the
hunter, when, having searched the mountains and unfolded a leafy peak, he
returns with a splendid and rich catch. And if for those who are excited about
profits and hunting the toil about these things is sweet, how much more for
the fishermen of the ecclesiastical gulfs, whose profit is not of a day, nor
of the earth, but is the very kingdom of the heavens? Come then, beloved, let
us strike up the psalmic melody of the Davidic lyre, and, having pilloried
human lowliness according to David, let us say: Nevertheless every living man
is troubled in vain. He is troubled, and the end is lost; he is troubled, and
before he is established he is swallowed up; he is kindled as fire, and as
stubble he is turned to ash; he is lifted as a tempest, and as dust he is
dashed to the ground; he is fanned as a flame, and as smoke he is dissolved;
he is made fair as a flower, and as grass he is dried up; he is spread out as
a cloud, and as a drop he is diminished; he is swollen as a bubble, and as a
spark he is quenched; he is troubled, and by insatiableness he gains a stench;
he is troubled, and taking nothing from the trouble he departs. His are the
troubles, and others' the luxuries; his the toils, and others' the treasures;
his the cares, and others' the gladnesses; his the afflictions, and others'
the enjoyments; his the curses, and others' the comforts; his the seizures,
and others' the pleasures; with him the groaning, and with others the
abundances; with him the tears, and with others the money; he is punished in
Hades, and others again revel among his things, singing.

Nevertheless every living man is troubled in vain. Man: the temporary loan of
life, the debt of death that admits no delay, the animal untamed by choice,
the self-taught wickedness, the self-learned plotting, the skilled in
evil-doing, the ingenious in injustice, the ready for greed, the insatiable
for appetite, the naturally apt for unbelief, the overblown spirit, the
loud-mouthed boldness, the easily dissolved swagger, the easily thrown-down
height, the easily captured daring, the insolent clay, the factious ash, the
high-minded dust, the puffed-up cinder, the easily quenched spark, the easily
withered flame, the easily fanned lamp, the wood ready to perish, the easily
dried grass, the easily killed verdure, the easily spent nature; he who
threatens today and dies tomorrow; he who is in wealth today and in the tomb
tomorrow; he who is in a diadem today and in a monument tomorrow; he who is in
purple today and in a funeral procession tomorrow; he who is in treasures
today and in coffins tomorrow; he who is among flatterers today and among
worms tomorrow; he who is today, and tomorrow is not; he who just now is
swaggering, and after a little is being lamented; he who in =55.560=
prosperities is unbearable, and in adversities inconsolable; he who does not
know himself, and busies himself with things above him; he who does not know
the present, and imagines about the future; mortal by nature, and by conceit,
as he thinks, eternal; that which lies ready as a side-work of every sickness,
the easily traversed lodging of every passion, the indiscriminate daily
gymnasium of fevers, the inn readily receiving every grief. O how great is the
tragedy of our lowliness! O how great is the triumph of human lowliness!

O how many things I have said, and I have found nothing more fitting than the
prophetic voice that says: Nevertheless every living man is troubled in vain!
Finally, see, beloved, whether the affairs of men do not imitate the sea,
whether life is not entangled in the trouble from there, whether we are not
storm-tossed more upon the dry land than upon the wet, whether we do not dash
against one another more violently than the winds, whether money, like
squalls, does not clash us against one another, whether we are not carried
here and there, as in a gloom of the sea. This man has torn away that man's
field, another has seized so-and-so's servants; and one is at law with his
neighbor about water, another wars with his housemate about air. Some fume
over measures of the earth, others crush one another over building; this man
is set upon taking what he did not give; that man goes to law not to give what
he received.

One is insatiable about interest; another insists on stealing even the
principal; this man, being in want, is in pain; that man, being well supplied,
is in uproar; he who has not is reproached, and he who has is plotted against;
he who is in offices is eyed with suspicion; he who is in authorities is
hated; he who is in powers is intrigued against. Wars hold us, envies come one
after another, insatiableness tyrannizes, greed overmasters, falsehood is
lifted up, trust toward one another has fled, truth has left the earth,
friendship is limited as far as the table. The rest have lost their own
strength; the earth can no longer bear the evils, the air has been polluted as
far as the ether itself. Because of money life has become unlivable; because
of money we have sold free elements; roads are taxed, the earth has been
allotted, waters are lorded over, the air is put under purchases;
tithe-collectors and tax-collectors and publicans hold the cities; the rich
are wasted by cares; the lenders wither with anxieties; the snatchers trouble
life; the money-lovers wear down the courts; the merchants traffic in
calamities; the sycophants sell the lie. Lying to one another we have spent
the oaths; we know God only for swearing.

2. Seeing all thus heaped in evils, and calling life wretched, the prophet
said: Nevertheless every living man is =55.561= troubled in vain. Man only, O
prophet, is troubled? Is the rational formation alone accused? I found nothing
among animals or elements that is troubled. Waters are troubled, he says, and
again they are restored; the earth is shaken, and again it is settled; the
winds are moved, and again they are still; every beast is in uproar, and being
filled it ceases; a flame is raised, and consuming the matter lying under it
it is quenched. But man, being troubled over money, never ceases; he has taken
this, and he looks toward that; he has seized that, and he gapes toward
another.

He is eager to double the hundred, he hurries again upon so many to heap so
many, and he never ceases from heaping, until his end is heaped; and being
held by the thirst of love of money, he goes about paler than gold, for the
much-longed-for wealth, the friend perhaps more unstable, the plotting desire,
the mocker of many masters, the much-loved jester, the winged prisoner, the
hostile corpse, the wind that flies off in the world; wealth, the begetter of
every absurdity, the inventor of every vice, the fellow-worker of
soul-destroying luxury, the adversary of self-control, the enemy of chastity,
and the stealthy thief of every virtue. But why do I slander wealth, leaving
those who possess it? It too is wronged, being bound by them and held in
fetters. For it seems to me to send forth a voice toward them: Why, O
money-lovers, do you hobble me, wealth? Why do you squeeze me, as a runaway,
with countless bonds? Why do you embrace me as a friend, and bind me as a
criminal, sending me from the mines into your hands? If you wish to give me
rest even lighter than dreams, let me be sent also into the right hands of the
poor.

But he says: I gather wealth for children, that they may not become heirs of
poverty. Well spoken, the richly imagining rich man; he does not know the
present, and he cares about the future; he is ignorant of his own affairs, and
he cares about the children; whether he is being buried, he does not know, and
he deliberates about the heirs. O fool, tell me your own end, and then make
the children secure; tell me the things of today, and then I believe you also
the things of tomorrow.

Why do you deceive yourself even after death? Why do you wish to be both a
corpse and mocked? Why do you define for God what is to be done? Why do you
legislate that the providence of God should administer the things given to
you? Nothing is yours concerning the things after you. You cannot be both a
corpse and an administrator of the living, and a judge of the dead, weighing
out the due of each. Why then do you toil in vain, O rich man, treasuring the
things of the poor in your stores, and you do not know for whom you gather
them? Why do you hold the things of the orphans, why when they ask of you are
you angry, as if spending from your own house? They seek their own, not yours;
the things entrusted to you for their sake, not the things born with you. Give
what you received, and gain the use, because you were commanded to give, not
to take. It is enough for you that through the poor man God stretches out his
right hand to you. He who rains from heaven begs of you a drop of bronze; he
who thunders and lightens says to you, Have mercy; he who clothes the heaven
in clouds asks a rag from you. It is enough for you that the poor beseech you
as God. Give, pity, have mercy, that you may be shown mercy.

But you do not even wish to lift your eyebrows, and being besought you are not
bent. Give them their own, before the accounting stands over you; give them
their own, which you are about to receive back before long. They have a king
as father; give them their own, and receive from their father freedom from
care. What kind is this? Inasmuch as you did it to one of these my brothers,
the least, you did it to me. For he who considers the poor and needy not only
wipes out the handwriting of sin, but also receives a confession, the voice
that says, He who gives to the poor lends to God. Let us lend almsgiving to
God, that we may receive from him a return of loving-kindness. But O the most
wise saying! He who has mercy on the poor lends to God. Why then did it not
say, He who has mercy on the poor gives to God, =55.562= but, Lends? Scripture
knew our greed; it observed that our insatiableness, looking toward greed,
seeks increase, and for this reason it did not say simply, He who has mercy on
the poor gives to God, lest you think the gift and the recompense simple; but,
He who has mercy on the poor lends to God, so that the lover of gain, hearing
the name of the loan, may give himself over to mercy. He who has mercy on the
poor lends to God. If God borrows from us, then he is our debtor.

What then do you wish to have him as, a judge or a debtor? The debtor is
ashamed before the one who lent; but the judge is not put to shame by the one
being judged. 3. And it is necessary, brothers, to see also in another way for
what reason God said that he who gives to the poor lends to me. Since he saw
our greed leaning toward increase, as I said before, and nowhere the one who
has money wishing to lend without security (for the lender demands either a
mortgage, or pledges, or the one who answers for another, and through these
three securities he trusts his own money, either receiving sureties, as I said
before, or a mortgage of things); since then God knows that apart from these
no one lends, nor looks toward loving-kindness, but looks only to the gain;
and the poor man is barren of all these, not having a mortgage (for he
possesses nothing), not bringing pledges (for he has been stripped), not
providing the one who answers (for he is distrusted because of want), when he
saw him in danger by want, and the one who has money through inhumanity, he
set himself in the middle, surety for the poor man, and pledge for the lender.
Do you distrust this man, he says, because of want?

Trust me because of abundance. He saw the poor man, and had mercy; he saw the
poor man, and did not overlook him, but gave himself as a pledge to the one
who has nothing, and stood forth for the needy through much goodness. He who
has mercy on the poor lends to God. Take courage, he says, you lend to me. And
what so great do I gain, lending to you? says the lender. I give you a
hundredfold, and eternal life, says God. So that you may pay me back, I demand
the terms, says the lender, wishing to confirm the contract. Give me the time
of the repayment, set the term of the receiving. Hear with understanding when
and where he who borrowed through the poor pays you the debt.

When the Son of man sits upon the throne of his glory, he will set the sheep
on his right, and the goats on the left; and he will say to those on the
right: Come, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you
from the foundation of the world. For what things? Because I hungered, and you
gave me to eat; I thirsted, and you gave me drink; I was naked, and you
clothed me; I was in prison, and you came to me. Then those who served well in
season, looking to their own weakness, and to the dignity of the one who
borrowed, say: Lord, when did we see you hungry, and feed you, or thirsty, and
give you drink, you on whom the eyes of all hope? or when did we see you in so
great need? and when did we do these things? Inasmuch as, he says, you did it
to one of these least, you did it to me. Is the saying not true, that He who
has mercy on the poor lends to God? But just as to those on the right, because
of loving-kindness, he showed the kingdom given as a gift, so also to those on
the left, because of unfruitfulness, he threatened the punishment: Depart from
me, you cursed, into the outer darkness, prepared for the devil and his
angels. Why? for what? Because I hungered, and you did not give me to eat. He
did not say, Because you fornicated, because you committed adultery, because
you stole, because you bore false witness, because you swore falsely; and even
if these too are admittedly so, they are lower than inhumanity and than
unmercifulness.

And why, O Lord, do you bring none of the others into memory? I do not judge,
he says, the sin, but the inhumanity; I do not judge those who have sinned,
but those who have not repented. I condemn you for inhumanity, because having
so great and so mighty a medicine of salvation, almsgiving, you let go so
great a benefaction. I therefore reproach inhumanity as the root of every vice
and of every impiety, and I =55.563= praise loving-kindness as the root of all
good things, and I threaten to the ones eternal fire, and to the others I give
the =55.564= kingdom of the heavens, in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the
glory and the power unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
