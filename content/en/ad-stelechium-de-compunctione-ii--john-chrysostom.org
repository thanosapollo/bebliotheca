marvelous, could nevertheless be suspected as wicked; but the achievements of
the righteous man are freed from all suspicion; and when God is the witness,
the verdict is outside all suspicion; for unless he had shown the most exact
proof of virtue, he would not have obtained the proclamation in the heavens.
What then does God say concerning him? I found David the son of Jesse, a man
according to my heart. But nevertheless after such a verdict and so many manly
deeds, he uttered the words of the condemned, having no boldness toward God,
fulfilling that gospel word: When you have done all things, say that we are
unprofitable servants. What more did that publican utter, who was truly filled
with ten thousand evils, who endured not even to look up to heaven, nor to
make a long speech, nor to dare to stand near the Pharisee? For the one was
reproaching him, saying, I am not as the rest of men, extortioners, unjust,
adulterers, or even as this publican; but the other, as having heard nothing
grievous, accepted the word spoken, and not only did not rage, but honored
that insolent and boastful man with such honor as to think himself not worthy
of the very earth trodden under him; and he said no word at all, so much as to
confess his own sins; but striking his breasts violently, so he entreated God
to become propitious.

But that that man should do these things is nothing marvelous; for the
multitude of his sins compelled him to bow down below, both willing and
unwilling. But that the righteous man, conscious to himself of nothing such,
should come forward with the same condemnation as the publican, this is the
paradox, and a sign of a soul truly contrite. For what difference is there
between, Be merciful to me the sinner, and, Lord, rebuke me not in your wrath,
nor chasten me in your anger? But this is rather of him, and much more. For
the publican did not even dare to look up to heaven, but the righteous man did
something greater than this. For that one said, Be merciful to me; but this
one did not even venture to say this; for he did not say, Rebuke me not, but,
Not in your wrath; nor, Chasten me not, but, Not in your anger; asking not to
be not punished, but not to suffer this too severely.

So that on every side =47.417= one may see his lowliness of soul, both from
his thinking himself worthy of so great a punishment, and from his not daring
to think God worthy to grant him complete forgiveness; which belongs to the
utterly condemned, and to those persuaded of themselves that they are more
sinful than all men. And what is greater than this: that he thinks worthy to
receive even the not being punished too severely from the mercy and the
loving-kindness of God, as one utterly weakened. For have mercy on me, he
says, for I am weak.

What is this? He who enjoyed such a testimony, and did not forget the
judgments of God (for your judgments, he says, I have not forgotten), he who
shone brighter than the sun, does he utter such words? Yes; for this is the
marvelous thing, that achieving so great things he never said or thought
anything great concerning himself, but thought himself last of all, and
thought worthy to be saved from the loving-kindness of God alone, as if
saying: I am worthy of punishment without end, and to be punished immortal
punishments; but because I can no longer bear it, I ask that there be for me a
loosing of the present evils; just as of servants those who have wrought ten
thousand terrible things cannot say that they did not sin, but because the
pain from the scourges is unbearable, they ask to be released for the future
from the blows.

But I think he is riddling also of another weakness now. What then is this?
That which arises to him from despondency and laments. For the excess of
grief, whenever it presses upon us more violently, is accustomed to gnaw away
all the strength of the soul. Which I think the righteous man also suffered,
from his condemning himself exceedingly, and never raising good hopes to
himself, but always the opposite. This at least he makes clear from what
follows: For have mercy on me, he added, for I am weak; heal me, Lord, for my
bones are troubled, and my soul is troubled exceedingly, having before said:
Lord, rebuke me not in your wrath.

And if he who has so clean a conscience thinks worthy not to have an exact
examination made of the things done, what shall we do, we who are held fast in
so many evils, and fall away so far from that boldness, and have not shown
even the hundredth part of that confession? Whence then did the blessed man
thus confess? He had been schooled that no one shall be justified before God,
and that even the righteous man is scarcely saved. Therefore at one time he
entreats, saying, Enter not into judgment with your servant; at another, Have
mercy on me, Lord, for I am weak.

εʹ. For this is worth considering everywhere, how nowhere does he remember his
own achievements, but casts his own salvation upon the loving-kindness of God.
For this is of a contrite soul, this of a humbled spirit, this makes those
achieving great things fear and tremble more than sinners. That he trembled
and feared thus, hear him saying: If you, Lord, will mark iniquities, Lord,
who shall stand? For he knew, he knew clearly, that we are responsible to God
for many debts, and that the smallest of sins is thought worthy of great
punishment; and knowing, he foresaw from above the things which Christ was
about to legislate when he came; that not murders only, but also insolences
and revilings and wicked thoughts and laughter and unseasonable word and jest,
and things yet smaller, he believed the trial =47.418= to lie grievous.
Therefore Paul also, though conscious of nothing in himself, said: I am
conscious to myself of nothing, but I am not justified in this. Why so?
Because even if he had wrought no evil (as indeed he had not), yet at least he
did not think he had fulfilled the measure of the honor owed to God. For even
if we die ten thousand times, even if we display every virtue, we have not
given back the worth of the favors wrought for us by God.

For behold: needing nothing of ours, but being sufficient to himself, he
brought us forth into being when we were not; he breathed in a soul such as
none of those upon the earth; he planted a paradise; he stretched out the
heaven; he strewed the earth; he hung up brilliant lights; and the earth he
adorned with lakes and springs and rivers and flowers and plants, and the
heaven, sprinkling upon it the varied chorus of the stars; and he wrought for
us the night not less useful than the day, because of the rest and strength
arising from sleep. For this feeds our bodies not less than foods; and it is
clear from this: for of hunger one might often see men daring many days, but
of sleep not even a few is it possible. And the flame arising in the day, both
that from the sunbeam and that from the daily toils, cooling and dissolving
it, he raises us again thus fresh to our labors; and in the season of winter,
by the length of the night's course, he grants us rest and warmth in addition,
forcing us to be under roofs. And the darkness was not joined to this season
simply nor in vain, but for greater rest.

As therefore the tender-hearted mothers, when they wish to lull to sleep their
wandering children, taking them under their arms, and casting their sight
under the little garment, lull them to sleep; so God also, spreading the
darkness as a curtain over the world, makes men cease from their labors. For
if this were not so, we should all have been torn asunder by curiosity or by
excessive greed of money and by our labors; but now he releases us even
unwilling from our sweats. Nor does it release our bodies only, but this law
of the bodies releases the soul not less. For what might one say of the calm
in this season, the quiet, and how all things are filled with silence and are
pure from disturbances, and one cannot hear any cry as in the day, some
bewailing poverty, others crying out oppression, others lamenting the weakness
of the body and mutilations, and some the death of their own, others the loss
of money, others some other human thing; and these are many; from all which,
as from waves, snatching it away, the night brings the race of men to its own
harbor and gives it rest.

And through the night so many good things for us; and through the day those
things which we all know. What might one say of the easiness for intercourse
arising for us? That the length of the journey not become a hindrance of the

him, and holding a little part of the earth, as being =47.419= master of the
whole, enjoy the good things happening everywhere; as at a rich table, to each
of the guests that which is set before him being given, it is permitted to the
one lying far off to receive that which lies near him, only stretching out the
hand. But if one should attempt to go over all the other things in speech, he
will fall into unspeakable length, and will go over not even a small part; for
how, being a man, and attempting to measure the infinite wisdom of God?
Consider first the differences of the plants, the fruit-bearing, the
unfruitful, those in deserts, those in the world, those on mountains, those in
plains; behold the variety in the seeds, in the herbs, in the flowers, and in
the animals, the land, the amphibious, the swimming; consider that all the
visible things are for us, heaven, earth, sea, and all things in these.

For as if someone having constructed for himself brilliant palaces, flashing
much with gold and shining with much the gleam of stones, so God having built
the world brought in man ruling over all things in it. And what is much more
marvelous: not having constructed the roof of this house from stones, but
having compacted it from another substance more precious, nor having kindled
the flame of a golden lamp, but letting the lamps from above run through the
roof of this house, he commanded, so that it be not only useful, but also for
us much pleasure; and the earth he permitted instead of a costly table. And
these things he provided for him who had not yet shown any good thing. But
nevertheless, after so great a gift, having become ungrateful toward his
benefactor, he did not even thus deprive him of honor, but casting him out of
paradise only, he punishes him with this, forbidding the ingratitude to go
further, and restraining the inclination toward the worse.

The Apostle therefore reckoning up all these things, and more than these, as
moved by divine Spirit, both the things from the beginning, and the things
each day, and the things toward each one, and the things toward all in common,
and the things openly, and the things secretly, being much more than the open,
and yet the things according to the economy of the only-begotten Son of God,
and the good things to come, and all things simply looking around, and
gathering from everywhere the unspeakable love of God, and having considered,
as fallen into a boundless sea, so he learned of how many and how great things
he was responsible, did not give back even the hundredth part. Therefore he
uttered these things; and the small of the sins he examined with much
exactness, but the achievements he sent away into forgetfulness.

But not we, who of the trespasses, both many being and great, make neither
account nor memory; but if we achieve some little thing, this we turn upside
down, and we do not cease before we exult =47.420= and puff ourselves up,
until we empty even that little thing through our boastfulness. David too
considering these things said: What is man, that you remember him? And not
only this, but accusing also his ingratitude: Man, he says, being in honor did
not understand; he was compared to the senseless cattle, and was made like to
them.

ςʹ. This is of a grateful servant, to make his own the benefits toward the
common good, and, as being debtor of the whole, to care and be anxious. Which
Paul also did (for it is good again to remember him), saying for our sake the
Lord died: For I, he says, who now live in the flesh, live in the faith of the
Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me. These things he said, not
wishing to contract the gift of Christ, but hastening to make himself
responsible for the whole, and persuading each one to be so disposed. For if
Christ came for the sake of one man, the gift is not only not lessened for
him, but is shown greater. How? That he appears displaying such zeal even for
one, as he who was seeking the one sheep, for these things was troubled, for
these things mourned. For if those who have borrowed money, and have it not to
pay back, but are being plunged much in the depth of the debts, neither eat
nor sleep, being bitten by the care; the righteous man, seeing the debt being
not in money, but in deeds, what would he not suffer?

But not we so; but even if we pay a little, we are disposed as having paid the
whole, or rather as exceeding it; and not even this little do we do with the
eagerness proper to free men, but we first examine whether it has a reward,
and whether the reward is great, and whether it is reckoned to ourselves,
uttering the words of some illiberal and mercenary men. What do you say, O
man, small-souled and wretched? Some deed of the things pleasing to God lies
before you, and you stand caring about reward? For if it was set before you
that doing it you would fall into hell, you ought to plunge in, but not with
so great eagerness to touch the work of the good things? Do you do some one of
the things dear to God, and seek another reward? Truly you do not know how
great a good it is to please God; for if you knew, you would have thought no
other recompense equal. Do you not know that your reward increases, when you
do the things that must be done not in hope of the rewards? Do you not see
that among men also they all honor most those whom they see looking before the
rewards to the thing pleasing to them, and zealous more for this than for the
honor from them? Then men are so nobly disposed toward their fellow servants,
but you, having been benefitted so much by the Master, and expecting so many
good things, whenever you ought to do some one of the things that come for the
sake of your salvation, you busy yourself about the reward before the deed.

Because of these things we are everywhere cold and wretched, and prepared for
none of the noble deeds; because of this we have never been able to come into
compunction, nor to contract our soul a little. For neither do we reckon up
our sins with exactness, nor examine the benefits of God, nor look to those
who have achieved the greatest things. Because of this we forget the good
works, because we neither bear our successes moderately, nor do we speak with
truth what we say continually, calling ourselves sinners. =47.421= And it is
clear from this: for when we hear these things from others, we grow savage, we
become wild, we say the thing is an insult. Thus all the things of ours are
hypocrisy, and we do not even imitate the publican, who, when another was
reproaching him with the multitude of sins, having accepted the reproaches,
reaped the gain through his deeds; for he went down justified more than the
Pharisee; but we do not even know what confession is, and this while filled
with ten thousand evils.

We ought, however, not only to be persuaded that we have trespassed ten
thousand times, but also to have all our sins, both the small and the great,
as written in a book, the heart, and to mourn over them as newly come to be.
For so we should also have contracted the mind of the soul, continually
reminding it of its own evils. So great a good is it to remember one's own
sins, that even the blotted out charges the blessed Paul continually brings
forward. For because he had washed away all the former things through baptism,
and after this lived purely, so as to be conscious of nothing to himself, and
he had no sins over which to groan, he remembered the things done away before
baptism, saying that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom
I am first; and again, He counted me faithful, putting me into the ministry,
who was before a blasphemer and persecutor and insolent; for I persecuted the
Church of God beyond measure, and laid it waste; and again, I am not worthy to
be called an apostle. For even if those sins let us go without account, yet
still they are enough to turn the soul, and to persuade it to love God
vehemently. For Simon also, being asked by the Lord which of the two debtors
would love more the one who lent, and saying, I suppose, him to whom he
forgave the more, heard: You judged rightly.

ζʹ. For when we consider the multitude of the sins gone by, then we shall know
the excess of the grace of God, then we shall bow down, then we shall be
contracted. For as much as we are responsible for greater trespasses, so much
more shall we be ashamed. Paul indeed remembered even those things; but we are
not willing to remember even the things after baptism, which have danger, and
make us responsible for the examination concerning them; but even if at some
time we fall into the thought of such a one, immediately we leap away, not
wishing even for a little to grieve the soul with the memory. Therefore ten
thousand evils are born to us from this useless favor. For thus being without
feeling and soft, we can neither confess the things of before (for how, when
we train ourselves not even to bear the memory of them?), nor are we readily
carried toward the things to come.

For it is a beloved thing, that memory flourishing in us always, and fear
shaking down the mind, to be able to take out the lax and dissipated part of
it; but if you take away this bridle, who shall thenceforward hold the soul
without fear, being carried down headlong over =47.422= precipices, and
falling into the chasms of perdition? Because of these things that righteous
man sketched out the punishment to come; because of these things he mourned,
because of these things he groaned, doing these things superabundantly.

For you great men it is enough for compunction to remember the benefits of
God, not to remember one's own achievements, to examine with much exactness
whether some even small trespass may have befallen you, to look to the great
men and greatly pleasing to God, then after all these things to consider the
uncertainty of the things to come, which is inclined toward the fall and
toward sin, which Paul also feared; therefore he said: I fear lest somehow,
having preached to others, I myself should become disapproved; and, He who
thinks he stands, let him take heed lest he fall. Thus David also turned all
these things about with himself; reckoning the benefits of God, he said: What
is man, that you remember him? or the son of man, that you visit him? You made
him a little less than the angels, with glory and honor you crowned him. But
he was so forgetful of his own achievements, that after that ten-thousandfold
philosophy to say: Who am I, O my Lord, Lord, and what is the house of my
father, that you loved me to these things? And these things were made small
before you, O my Lord, Lord, and you spoke concerning the house of your
servant for a long time to come. This is the law of man, O my Lord, Lord,
toward you. And what shall David add still to speak to you? And continually
considering the virtue of his forefathers, he thought himself to be nothing
compared with them. For after saying, In you our fathers hoped, speaking
concerning himself he added, But I am a worm, and not a man. And the
uncertainty of the things to come he had so before his eyes, as to say:
Lighten my eyes, lest ever I sleep in death. And he thought himself
responsible for so many sins, as to say: Be merciful to my sin, for it is
great.

To you the great men then these things suffice; but to us there is necessity,
after these medicines also, of strong and sufficient ones to cast down the
mind and all the boastfulness. What then is this? The multitude of the sins,
the evil conscience; which, when it seizes us, does not let us rise to the
height even willing. Therefore I beg and implore you by the boldness of that
conscience which you acquired through your achievements toward God, to stretch
out a hand to us continually praying, so as to be able both to mourn worthily
the burden of so many evils, and mourning to lay hold of some dear way,
carrying us up to heaven, that we may not go away into Hades, where no one is
able to confess, and suffer the things of the dishonored, nothing
thenceforward snatching us away.

For as long as we shall be here, both we shall be able to reap great things
from you, and you to benefit us with the greatest things; but when we go away
there, where neither friend, nor brother, nor father will be able to help, nor
to stand by us being punished, there is necessity thenceforward to pay the
immortal penalty in want and deep darkness and much desert of those who would
comfort, becoming unquenchable food for the all-devouring flame.
