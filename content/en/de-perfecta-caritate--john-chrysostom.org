Of Chrysostom, On Perfect Love, and on the Due Recompense of Works, and on
Compunction.
1. Every good deed is a fruit of love; wherefore also the discourse concerning
it is abundant, Christ saying: By this shall all men know that you are my
disciples, if you love one another; and Paul crying out: Owe no man anything,
except to love one another. He did not say it simply, but as those who owe it
to one another. For as we always owe food to the body, and always render it,
for such a debt is coextensive with all our life, so he teaches us to do also
in the matter of love; or rather even much more, inasmuch as it also leads to
eternal life, and remains with those who have it. For these three remain, he
says, faith, hope, love; but the greatest of these is love. And we are taught
this not only through words, but also through deeds. And first through the
manner of our constitution. For God, having formed one man, ordained that all
should come to be from him, that we might all count one another as one, and be
eager to live in love toward one another. Then also through the intercourse of
exchanges he wisely worked out for us mutual affection; and how, hear. Having
filled the inhabited world with many goods, he gave again to each country a
peculiar fitness of fruits, that, going to one another because of the
necessity of need, and sharing what is in surplus, and receiving in return
what is lacking to us, we might love those of the same race. This he has done
also in the case of each human being. For he did not give all to know all
things, but to one medicine, to another carpentry, and to another another
craft, that needing =56.280=.10 one another, we might love one another. And in
the spiritual things again one may see the same coming to pass, as Paul says,
that To one is given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge, to
another prophecy, to another gifts of healings, to another kinds of tongues,
to another interpretation of tongues. But nothing is higher than love;
wherefore he also set it before all, saying thus: And if I speak with the
tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass,
or a clanging cymbal; and if I have prophecy, and know all mysteries, and if I
have faith so as to remove mountains, but have not love, I am nothing. And he
did not even stop at these, but even declares that death for piety has no
profit, if love is not present. And he has not said so much concerning it in
vain; for he knew, he knew clearly, as a husbandman of the commandments of
God, that, when this is rooted securely, the fruit of all goods springs up.
For, You shall not commit adultery, you shall not murder, you shall not steal,
you shall not bear false witness, and if there is any other commandment, it is
summed up in this word, in the, You shall love your neighbor as yourself. And
why must one speak of the small things, being silent about the great?
Through love the beloved Son of God came down to us, and became tent-fellow
and table-fellow with human beings, that having dissolved the polytheistic
error, and having proclaimed the true knowledge of God, he might teach human
beings love toward one another, as John bears witness saying: God so loved the
world, that he gave his only-begotten Son, that everyone who believes in him
should not perish, but have eternal life. Burned by this, Paul sent forth the
heavenly voice, Who shall separate us from the lov=56.281 e of Christ? saying:
tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger,
or sword? And having despised these as cheap, he has also set down the things

far greater than these; for he says, That neither death, nor life, nor things
present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature
shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our
Lord. From this nothing was able to separate that blessed man, burned up by
love, not heaven, not earth, not sea, not the kingdoms in the heavens, not the
punishments in Hades; for he looked beyond all things because of Christ. And
if we should also examine the other saints, we shall find all of them
well-pleasing through love.
2. Love shows you your neighbor as yourself, and teaches you to rejoice at his
good things, as at your own, and to bear his shortcomings, as also your own.
Love makes the many one body, and fashions their souls as vessels of the Holy
Spirit; for not upon those divided from one another, but upon those united in
soul, will the Spirit of peace rest. Love makes the possessions of each common
to all, as the book of the Acts says: And the multitude of those who believed
was of one heart and soul; and no one said that any of the things belonging to
him was his own, but they had all things common; and it was distributed to
each, according as anyone had need. What wall so unbreakable, fortified by the
fitting of great stones, becomes impregnable to the assaults of enemies, as
the company of those who love one another, and are bound together in
like-mindedness? It even beats back the attacks of the devil themselves; and
very reasonably. For those who array themselves with one another against him,
and do not stand with him against one another, become invincible to his
devices, raising the bright trophies of love. And just as the strings of the
lyre, being many, but inclining toward one harmony, sound some most delightful
melody; so those blended toward one mind strike up the well-sounding note of
love. For this reason Paul advises to think the same thing, and to speak it,
and to count one another as surpassing themselves, so that love may not be
dissolved by ambition, but that, yielding the honor to one another, they may
live in concord. And again he says: Through love serve one another.
For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in the, You shall love your
neighbor as yourself. The one who loves does not wish only to command, but
also to be commanded; and he rejoices more being commanded than commanding.
The one who loves wishes rather to grant a favor than to receive a favor; for
he wishes to have the friend as a debtor, rather than himself to owe to him.
The one who loves both wishes to grant a favor to the beloved, and does not
wish to seem to grant a favor, both to begin the benefaction himself, and not
to seem to begin the benefaction. Perhaps some do not understand what has been
said; therefore I will make it clear through an example. The philanthropic
Master was about to grant his Son for us; but that he might not seem to grant
a favor, =56.282= but to render a debt, he commanded Abraham to give his son,
that he himself also, doing this, might be found not as giving a grace, but as
paying a debt, because of the surpassing wealth of his goodness. I know that
what has been said appears strange to the many. And the cause is that I am
discoursing about a thing that now dwells in heaven. For just as if I were
speaking about some plant born in India, of which no one has taken experience,
the word would not have had power to present it, even if I said ten thousand
things concerning it; so also now, however much I may say, I shall speak in
vain; for some do not understand the things said. For in heaven, as I said,
this plant has been planted. But if we wish, it is possible for it to be
planted among us as well. For this reason we have been taught to say to the
Father in the heavens: Your will be done in heaven and on earth.
3. Let us not then think it impossible to acquire such a good.


For it is possible, truly possible, if at least we wish to be sober; and not
this only, but also to accomplish all virtue. For we are governed by
self-determining choice, and we are not subject to a necessity of fate, as
some hold, being defined in the willing and the not willing, and both the good
things and the evil things lying there. For this reason God both promised a
kingdom and threatened punishment. He would not have done thus to those bound
by necessity; for there is a recompense of both of the things that come from
purpose. He would not have laid down laws, he would not have given
exhortations, if we had been held by the bonds of fate. But since we are free,
and masters of our own choice, and from slackness we become base, and from
zeal good, for this reason he prepared these medicines, correcting us by the
fear of punishment and the expectation of the kingdom, and training us to
philosophize. And not from these only, but also from the things we ourselves
do, it is clear that neither fate, nor chance, nor birth, nor the course of
stars administers our affairs.
For if all things that come to pass hang from there, and not from the choice
of human beings, for what reason do you scourge the servant who has stolen?
for what reason do you drag the wife who has committed adultery into court?
for what reason are you ashamed when you do the unseemly things? for what
reason do you not even bear the words when you are reproached, but if someone
calls you an adulterer, or a fornicator, or a drunkard, or something of that
kind, you call the thing an insult? For if the sinning is not of your purpose,
neither is what happens an accusation, nor is what is said an insult. But now
both through not forgiving those who sin, and through being yourself ashamed
when you do the evil things, and being eager to escape notice, and through
counting those who bring these things against you as insolent, through all
things you confess that our affairs are not bound by necessity, but have been
honored with freedom of choice. At any rate we know how to forgive those held
by necessity. And if someone troubled by a demon either tears our tunic, or
inflicts blows, we not only do not punish him, but we even pity him and
forgive. Why then? because not the freedom of choice, but the violence of the
demon worked these things. So that if the other sins also came from the
necessity according to fate, we would have forgiven; but since we know that it
is not of necessity, for this reason we do not forgive, neither masters
servants, nor husbands wives, nor wives husbands, nor =56.283= fathers
children, nor teachers disciples, nor rulers those ruled, but we become bitter
examiners and punishers of what has been dared, and we occupy courts, and we
subject them to blows, and we use rebukes, and we do all things, that we may
release them from the evils. And for our own children we both set pedagogues
over them, and send them to teachers, and add threats, and apply scourges, and
bring many other helps, that they may become good.
What need then of toils and sweats toward accomplishing virtue? For if it is
fated for someone to become good, such a one, even sleeping and snoring, will
be good; or rather it is not even possible to call such a one good, who
becomes this from necessity. What need of toils and sweats toward fleeing
vice? For if it is fated for someone to become evil, even if he suffers ten
thousand hardships, he will be evil; or rather it is not even possible to call
such a one evil, who is pushed toward this under necessity. For just as we
will not address the demoniac as insolent, even if he reviles, even if he
strikes (again I have used the same example), for we do not reckon the
reviling to him, but to the necessity of the demon; so also the evil man, if
he is pushed toward this by fate, let us not call him evil, just as neither
the good man good. For indeed if this should be granted, all our affairs will
be thrown into confusion, and neither will virtue be anything, nor vice, nor
arts, nor laws, nor any other of such things. And for what reason


do we make much zeal when we are sick, and spend money, and call physicians,
and apply medicines, and restrain the belly, and chastise desire? For if both
health and disease hang from fate, superfluous are the expenditures of money,
superfluous the entrance of the physicians, superfluous the exactness of the
diet of the sick. But now, together with the other things, from these also we
show that none of them is superfluous. But the myth of fate is gone; for our
affairs are subject to no necessity; but all things, as I said, have been
honored with freedom of choice.
4. Knowing these things then, beloved, and the things more than these (for
there are many other things also to say concerning these, but for the more
intelligent these suffice), let us flee vice, let us choose virtue, that we
may show through the deeds that we have a self-determining mind toward what
seems good, that we may not be put to shame in the day of the revelation of
works. For we must all be made manifest before the judgment seat of Christ, as
Paul says, that each may receive according to the things he has done, whether
good or evil. Let us take, I beseech you, that court into mind, and let me
suppose it present now, and the judge seated, and all things being uncovered,
and being brought into the midst. For we must not simply stand by, but also be
made manifest. Did you not blush? did you not shudder? do we not often choose
to die, rather than that a hidden fault of ours be made manifest before
honorable friends? How then shall we be disposed then, when our sins are made
manifest before all angels and all human beings, and stand before our eyes?
For I will convict you, he says, and I will set your sins before your face.
And if, the thing not yet being present, but given as a hypothesis, and
sketched in word, we perish under conscience, what shall we do when =56.284=
it arrives, when the whole inhabited world is present, when angels and
archangels, when principalities and authorities, when the trumpets sound, the
continual and successive ones, and the catching up of the righteous upon
clouds, and much weeping of those who have sinned? What fear then will hold
those remaining upon the earth?
For one, he says, is caught up, and one is left; and one is taken, and one is
abandoned. What will be the soul of those, when they see some being led away
with great honor, but themselves being left with much shame? It is not, it is
not possible, believe me, to present the passion in word. Have you ever seen
those being led away to death? What do you think their soul is, as they walk
the road as far as the gate? What would they not have chosen both to do and to
suffer, so as to be released from that gloom? I have heard many of those
saying, who after the leading-away were called back by royal philanthropy,
that they did not even see the human beings as human beings, the soul being
troubled and struck out of itself. And why do I speak of those being led away?
A crowd stood around then, most of them not knowing them. If someone then had
looked around at the soul of each one, no one is so savage, no one so bold, no
one so noble, whom he would not find having it fallen and slackened by fear
and despondency. And if, when others are being put to death, those who have
nothing in common with them are so disposed, when we ourselves fall into the
greater things, what then shall we be, being sent away from that unspeakable
joy, and being forwarded into eternal punishment? For even if there were no
gehenna, to be thrust away from so great a brightness, and to withdraw
dishonored, how great a punishment?
For indeed if now, when a king is making his entrance, many watching, and
considering their own poverty, do not take so much pleasure from the
spectacle, as they endure despondency from sharing nothing of the things about
the king, nor being near the ruler,


what will it be then? Or do you think it a small punishment not to be ranked
in that choir? not to be counted worthy of the unspeakable glory? to have been
cast away somewhere far and distant from that festival and the ineffable
goods? And when there is also darkness, and gnashing of teeth, and bonds that
cannot be loosed, and a worm that does not end, and fire unquenchable, and
tribulation and distress, and tongues being fried, as that of the rich man,
and we wail, and no one hears, and we groan, and we gnash under the pains, and
no one pays heed, and we look everywhere, and nowhere is there anyone who
comforts; where shall we rank those who are in these things? and what is more
wretched than those souls? and what more pitiable?
5. And if, going into a prison, and seeing some squalid, some bound with iron
chains, some shut up in darkness, we are broken, we shudder, we do all things
that we may not fall into such necessity and affliction; when we are led away
bound into the very tortures of gehenna, what shall we be? and what shall we
do? For those bonds are not of iron, but they are of fire that is never
quenched, nor are those set over us of like honor, whom it is often possible
even to appease, but fearful and unpitying angels, whom it is not even
possible to look in the face, being fiercely angry over the things in which we
insulted the Master. It is not, as here, to see some bringing sil=56.285 ver,
some foods, some consoling words, and to obtain comfort, but there all things
are unforgiven. Even if it be Noah, even if Job, even if Daniel, and they see
their own being punished, they do not dare to stand by and stretch out a hand.
For indeed it happens then that the sympathy from nature is taken away. For
since righteous men are found fathers of sinful children, and good children of
wicked parents (for the evils are not of nature, but of purpose), so that the
gladness may be pure for them, and those enjoying those goods may not be
broken by the necessity of sympathy, this too is quenched then, and they
themselves share the Master's indignation against their own bowels. For if
even now some, when they see their own children base, disown them, and cut
them off from the kinship, much more will this happen in the judgment.
Therefore let no one hope, not having worked anything good, for anything good,
even if he have ten thousand righteous ancestors. For each will receive, he
says, the things through the body, according to the things he has done,
whether good or evil. Let us hear, I beseech you, and let us be sober. If you
have a fire of wicked desire, think of the fire of that punishment, and this,
having been quenched, is gone. If you wish to utter something unseemly, think
of the gnashing of teeth, and fear will be a bridle for you. If you wish to
seize, hear the judge saying, Bind his hands and feet, and cast him into the
outer darkness, and you will cast out this desire. If you are savage and
unmerciful, remember those virgins who, their lamps having been quenched
because they did not have oil, fell out of the bridechamber, and you will
quickly be philanthropic. If you desire to get drunk and to live in luxury,
hear the rich man saying, Send Lazarus, that with the tip of his finger he may
cool the tongue being fried, and not obtaining the request, and you will
quickly stand off from the passion. And all the other things too you will
accomplish in this way; for God commanded nothing burdensome. From where then
do the commands appear burdensome? From our slackness. For just as, if we are
zealous, even the things that seem heavy will be light and easy, so, if we are
slack, even the bearable things will appear difficult to us. Reckoning all
these things, let us not count blessed those who live in luxury, but let us
think of their end. For here dung and much flesh, but there worm and fire. Not
those who seize,


but what their end is. Here cares and toils, there bonds that cannot be loosed
and outer darkness. Not those who love glory, but what their end is. Here
slavery and irony, there much loss and being burned continually. If we
discourse with ourselves thus, and continually chant these and such things
over our wicked desires, we shall quickly both flee vice, and accomplish
virtue, and quench the love of the present things, and kindle that of the
things to come. For what do the present things have that is firm, or what
strange and paradoxical, that we spend all our zeal upon them? Do we not see
the same things cycling through everything, such as day and night, night and
day, winter and summer, summer and winter, and nothing more? Let us kindle
then the longing for the goods to come. For a great glory is laid up for the
righteous, and one which it is not possible to present in word. For having
received the bodies incorruptible after the resurrection, they are glorified
together and reign together with Christ.
ʹ. And how great this is, we shall know from here; or rather we shall know it
clearly from nowhere; but so that, having been led up from the goods among us,
we may take at least some small notion, as far as I am able, I will try to
present what has been said. For tell me, if someone promised to make you,
grown old and living in poverty, suddenly young, and to bring you to the very
prime of age, and to make you exceedingly strong and fair beyond all, and to
give a kingdom of the whole earth for a thousand years, a kingdom having a
most deep peace, what would you not have chosen both to do and to suffer for
this proposal? See then, Christ promises not these things, but things much
greater than these. For not so great as the interval of old age and youth is
the difference of corruption and incorruption; nor so great as of kingship and
poverty, so great that of the present glory and that to come; but as of dreams
and truth. Or rather I have not yet said anything; for there is not even a
word sufficient to present the greatness of the difference of the things that
will be toward the present things. And as for time, it is not even possible to
conceive a difference at all. For how could one compare with the present
things a life that has no end? And of the peace so great is the interval, as
the difference of peace and war; and of corruption and incorruption, as much
as the pure pearl is better than a clod of clay. Or rather, however many
things one may say, he will be able to present nothing. For even if I compare
the beauty of the bodies then with the light of a ray, even if with the
brightest lightning, I shall not yet say anything worthy of that brightness.
For these things then how much money is it not worth to spend, and bodies? or
rather how many souls is it not worth to spend? But now, if someone led you
into palaces, and arranged for the king to converse with you with all present,
and made you table-fellow and tent-fellow with him, you would have said
yourself to be more blessed than all; but being about to go up into heaven,
and to stand beside the king of all things himself, and to shine opposite the
angels, and to enjoy that unapproachable glory, you hesitate whether you ought
to spend money, when, even if it were necessary to strip off life itself, you
ought to leap and rejoice and be winged with pleasure? But you, that you may
receive an office that furnishes you occasions of thefts (for I do not call
such a thing gain), both cast out what you have, and having borrowed the
things of others, if it should be necessary, you do not hesitate even to
pledge the wife and the children, but when the kingdom of the heavens is set
forth, the rule that has no successor, you hesitate and draw back and gape
toward money; and you do not consider that if the parts of heaven toward us
are so fair and delightful, how great are the highest things, and the heaven
of heaven? But since it is not possible to see these things with bodily eyes,
go up with the reasoning, and standing above this heaven, look up to that
heaven higher than this, to the


infinite height, to the unapproachable light, to the companies of the angels,
to the ranks of the archangels, to the other incorporeal powers; and again
take hold of the image among us, having come down from above, and sketch for
me the things about the king upon earth, such as men wearing gold, and a pair
of white mules adorned with gold and a jewel-set chariot, and leaves shaken
about it, and dragons fashioned on silk garments, and shields having golden
eyes, and horses wearing gold, and golden bits. But when we see the king, we
see none of these things any longer; =56.287= for that one alone turns us, and
the purple garments, and the diadem, and the seat, and the brooch, and the
sandals, and the radiance of the face. Gathering all these things then
accurately, transfer the reasoning again from these to the things above, and
think of the fearful day, on which Christ arrives. For you will not then see a
pair of mules, nor golden chariots, nor dragons and shields, but what is full
of much shuddering, and produces such amazement, that even the heavenly powers
themselves are struck; For the powers of the heavens, he says, shall be
shaken.
Then the whole heaven is opened, and the only-begotten Son of God comes down,
not twenty, nor a hundred escorting him, but thousands and myriads of angels
and archangels, and all things will be full of fear and trembling, the earth
being torn open, and the human beings who have ever come to be, from when Adam
came to be until that day, being raised from earth, and being caught up, he
appearing with so great glory, as to hide all the light of the sun and the
moon, outshone by that beam. But, alas for the much insensibility, that, such
goods being expected, we still gape toward the present things, and do not
consider the villainy of the devil, who through the small things strips us of
the great, and gives clay, that he may seize heaven, and shows a shadow, that
he may cast out from the truth, and makes a phantom in dreams (for this the
present wealth is), that, day having come, he may show us poorer than all.
Having learned which things, beloved, let us flee his deceit, let us flee the
condemnation with him, that the judge may not say also to us: Depart from me,
you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels.
7. But God is philanthropic; this will not be, he says. Was it written then in
vain? No, he says, but toward a threat only, that we may be sober. If then we
are not sober, but remain evil, will he not bring on the punishment? tell me.
Will he then not render the rewards to the good either? Yes, he says; for this
is fitting to him even to benefit beyond desert. So that those things are true
and will be in every way, but the things of the punishments not in every way?
O the much evil-craft of the devil, O this inhuman philanthropy. For this
reasoning is his, granting a profitless grace and making men slack. For since
he knows that the fear of punishment, like some bridle, throttles our soul,
and checks it from the evils, he does all things and works all things, so as
to pull this up by the root, that thereafter we may be borne fearlessly down
cliffs. How then shall we get the better of him? However many things we say
from the Scriptures, those who contradict will say that it has been written
for the sake of a threat. But concerning the things to come they might have
this to say, even if very impiously; but concerning the things that have come
out, and have had an end, no longer. Let us ask them then: Have you heard
concerning the flood and that utter destruction? was that too said for the
sake of a threat? did it not come to be, and come out into deed? do not even
the =56.288= mountains of Armenia bear witness, where the ark was settled? are
not even its remains still preserved there


unto our reminder? Many said such things then too, and in a hundred years
while the ark was being carpentered, and the timbers prepared, and the
righteous man crying out, there was no one who believed; but since they did
not believe the threat through words, they underwent the punishment through
deeds all at once. Then he who brought such a punishment upon those, will he
not much more bring it upon us? for the things now coming to pass are not less
than the evils then. For then they made lawless mixings; For the sons of God,
it says, went in to the daughters of men; but now there is no kind of sin
unpracticed, which is left. But, if it seems good, let us lead the word also
upon other kinds of punishment, that from the things that have come to be the
things to come also may be believed. Has any of you ever sojourned in
Palestine? I at least think so. Bear witness then yourselves to the truth of
the things said. For above Ascalon and Gaza, at the very end of the river
Jordan, there was a country both large and fertile, and rivaling the paradise
of God; For Lot saw, it says, all the country round about Jordan, and it was
watered as the paradise of God. But now this is more desolate than all the
deserts. For trees stand, and have fruit, but the fruit is a reminder of the
wrath of God. For pomegranates stand having a bright appearance, and offering
good hopes to those who do not know, but when taken into hands and broken
open, they display no fruit, but much dust and ash lying stored within. Such
is the land also, such also the stones, such also the air itself. All things
have been burned, all things have been turned to ash, being reminders of the
wrath that went before, foretokens of the punishment to come.
Are these too threats of words? are these too noises of words? If someone
disbelieves gehenna, let him reckon Sodom, let him think of Gomorrah, the
punishment already come to be and remaining until now. Which also the divine
Scripture narrating concerning wisdom says: She delivered a righteous man
fleeing from the fire coming down upon Pentapolis, when ungodly men were
perishing. She also still even now stands as a witness of wickedness, a
smoking waste, and bearing plants in unfinished seasons. And it is necessary
also to say the cause, through which they have suffered these things. Their
fault was one, harsh indeed and accursed; yet one. They raged after boys, and
because of this they were burned with a fiery rain; but now ten thousand
things both equal and harsher come to be, and such a burning does not come to
be. For what reason? Because another fire has been prepared, that is never
quenched. For how shall he who brought so great wrath for one sin, and neither
received the supplication of Abraham, nor was put to shame by the inhabitant
Lot, spare us when so ma=56.289 ny evils are coming to be? These things are
not so, they are not so. 8. But let us not stop at these; come, let us bring
into the midst others who were punished, that through more proofs the things
said may be confirmed. You all hear of Pharaoh the king of the Egyptians; you
know then also the penalty which he paid, how with chariots and horses, with
the whole camp, he was drowned in the Red Sea. And that you may learn also the
punishments of the Jews, hear Paul saying: Neither let us commit fornication,
as some of them committed fornication, and there fell in one day twenty-three

destroyer; nor let us tempt the Lord, as some of them tempted, and perished by

terrible, and for this reason especially it is necessary to fear. For we are
not being kept so as not to pay a penalty, but so as to pay a worse one, if we
do not change. For those did not know gehenna, and they were given over to the
punishments here; but we, however many things we may sin, even if we suffer
nothing grievous in the


present life, shall suffer all things in the one to come. For how would it
have reason, that those, being more childish, should have suffered such
things, but we, having enjoyed more perfect lessons, and stumbling much worse
than those, should escape the punishments? Do you wish to hear also their
remaining disasters, how many they suffered in Palestine under Babylonians and
Assyrians and Macedonians? how many famines, plagues, wars, captivities they
endured under Titus and Vespasian? Read the book of Josephus, which he wrote
concerning the capture of Jerusalem, and you will learn that bitter tragedy.
For together with the other terrible things, they fell into so great an excess
of famine, as even to eat their belts, and their shoes, and the things more
abominable than these. For necessity brought all things under the teeth, as
somewhere the aforementioned writer says. And they did not even stop at these,
but they even tasted of their own children. How then, those having paid such
penalties, shall we who do the things worse than these escape? For if =56.290=
those were punished then, why are we not punished now? Is it not clear even to
a blind man, that because the punishment is laid up for us in the age to come,
as I have said many times? And it is necessary to reckon besides these also
the things now coming to be in life, and we shall not disbelieve gehenna. For
if God is just and no respecter of persons, as indeed he is, for what reason
do some pay here penalties for murders, but others do not pay? some of the
adulterers are punished, but others die unpunished? how many tomb-robbers have
escaped? how many robbers? how many greedy men? how many seizers? If then
there is no gehenna, where shall they undergo punishment? Do we then persuade
those who contradict, that the word concerning it is not a myth? For it is so
true, that not we only, but also poets and philosophers and story-writers have
philosophized concerning the recompense to come, and have said that the wicked
are punished in Hades. For even if they were not able to speak these things
with truth, as they stand, inasmuch as they were moved from reasonings and
from overheard things from among us, nevertheless they took some image of
judgment. For they speak of certain Cocytuses and Pyriphlegethon rivers, and
water of Styx, and Tartarus so far from the earth, as this is from heaven, and
many other manners of punishment; and again an Elysian plain, and islands of
the blessed, and flowery meadows, and much fragrance, and a thin breeze, and
choirs spending time there, and clothed in a white robe, and singing certain
hymns, and in a word a recompense laid up both for good and for wicked after
the departure from here. Let us not then disbelieve gehenna, that we may not
fall into it; for the one who disbelieves becomes more slack; and the one who
is slack will go away into it in every way; but let us both believe without
doubt, and discourse concerning it continually, and we shall not quickly sin.
For the memory of such words, like some bitter medicine, will be able to wipe
away all vice, settling continually in our soul. Let us use it then, that
having been well purified, we may be counted worthy to see God, as it is
possible for human beings to see, and may enjoy the goods to come, by the
grace and philanthropy of our Lord Jesus Christ. To him be the glory unto the
ages of ages. Amen.
