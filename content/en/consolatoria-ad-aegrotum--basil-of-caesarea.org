* Consolatory Homily to One Who Is Sick

OF THE SAME, A CONSOLATORY HOMILY TO ONE WHO IS SICK.
Father, give the blessing. The life of men is short, and brief is the
brightness of toilsome life, a spider's web; glory is easily dissolved,
the appearance of life is a dream, but most blessed is the rest of the
righteous. Endless is the repose in the heavens, the gifts of the
unfading Benefactor, and reasonably so. For of Him whose nature is
unchangeable, the giving also is unceasing. Therefore their contest here
is temporary; but eternal is the victory there after the prizes. For we
sweat as in a wrestling-school in this life, wrestling with afflictions,
and nature has many antagonists. Pleasures undermine what is firm,
luxuries change =31.1716= manliness, despondencies unnerve vigor,
slanders pour out insults, flatteries attach plots, fears trip us into
despair, and in such a storm nature is tossed without cease. For not
only are the painful things of life burdensome, but even those that seem
pleasant vex by their change, and we travel a life almost full of
afflictions and tears; and if you wish to know, learn the painful things
of life. Man was sown in the womb, but affliction led the sowing. The
seed was cast into the furrow of nature; if we merely call it to mind,
we blush at the preludes of generation. What was cast was changed into
blood, the blood was thickened into flesh, the flesh was formed in time,
what was formed was ensouled beyond speech, the ensouled one was
nourished according to nature, the embryo leaps being cramped, it is
distressed at the prison of nature; but at last the time of birth came,
the bolts of the pangs were loosed, the doors of nature were opened, the
womb released him whom it held; the boxer of affliction slipped out into
life, the tongue of creation drew breath, and what after this? First
voice: he wept. It is enough from the preludes to know life. The infant
fell upon the earth, and did not laugh; but immediately upon falling it
took hold of toils and weeps. For it knew that it had been shipwrecked
into the sea of afflictions. He was reared with tears, weaned
unwillingly, he comes among children, and fears parents or servants. He
became a youth, and was handed over to pedagogues for letters. See a
fear that has no rest. He was idle, he was whipped, he kept vigil, but
he learned thoroughly, he advanced to the height, he was perfected in
letters, he displayed distinction, he flashes through every form of
speech, he is filled with legal experience, he advanced in time among
men, he gave himself to military service. Again a beginning of greater
afflictions. He fears rulers, he suspects plots, he is nailed to gain,
he searches, he rages about profit, he keeps vigil, he lives in
lawsuits, he goes abroad in accountings, not being dragged, for he
follows unwillingly, he toils beyond his strength, by night he is
anxious, by day he stands by, as a slave. For need has sold his freedom.
Then after these things, having often toiled and pleased, he was deemed
worthy of honors, he was raised to the height of office, he
superintends nations, he commands =31.1717= armies, he is ambitious as
consul, he heaped up wealth; but time ran along with the toils, and old
age arrived together with the dignity, and before he could enjoy the
wealth, he departed, snatched from life, and in the very harbor he
suffered shipwreck. For death follows along with vain hopes, mocking
mortals. Such is the life of men: an unstable sea, an uneven air, an
uncertain dream, a stream running past, smoke dispersing, a shadow
leaping aside, a sea vexed by waves; and the storm is fearful, the
voyage unsafe, and we the passengers are drowsy. For fearful and wild is
the sea of life, vain are the hopes, like squalls that dash against the
rocks. Afflictions roar as waves, plots hide as submerged rocks,
enemies gape around as dogs, plunderers lie in wait as pirates, old age
comes on as winter, death stands over us as shipwreck. You have seen the
storm: steer your winter, watch how you sail, lest you make your own
vessel waterlogged, either with wealth from injustice, or with the cargo
of passions; for this reason the blessed Paul lately cries: Godliness is
a great gain. Heavenly wealth, treasury of mysteries, sea of wisdom,
knowledge of truth, worship of the uncreated Trinity, mirror of the
virginal mystery, faith uninquisitive, confession that flees
investigation, holy preaching, offered through the tongue, entering
through hearing, taking root in the soul, and granting the illumination
of the Trinity. Godliness with contentment is a great gain.
For nothing is richer than contentment: a life without superfluity, rest
without care, wealth without plots, need with ease, a burden without
affliction, a life without want, enjoyment without insult. Those who
practice contentment flee the storm of wealth. For the rich man fears
all things: the days, as times of courts; the evenings, as opportunities
of robbers; the nights, as scourges of cares; the dawns, as attacks of
flatterers. And he fears not only time, but also place. He shudders at
the diggings of robbers, the plots of thieves, the slanders of violent
men, the accusations of those who have been wronged, the plunderings of
the more powerful, the crimes of servants, the busybodying of informers,
the ingratitude of those who exact, the thoughts of neighbors, the
rottenness of walls, the collapses of houses, the attacks of barbarians,
the deceits of citizens, the verdicts of those who judge, =31.1720= the
losing after having, the taking away after the possession. O man, if so
great is the winter of possession, where is the spring of enjoyment?
Godliness with contentment is a great gain. It is not shut up with the
present life, he says. For the possession is immortal, for it is not
captured by wealth that is spent. Speak, O Paul: We brought nothing into
the world. The equal honor of the entrance is enough to drive out the
unevenness of the pride of our way of life. We brought nothing into the
world; naked we came out from the maternal womb. You entered having
nothing, O greedy man; not gold, for it is mined from earth; not silver,
for it was not sown with you; not clothing, for it is an invention of
the weaver's art; not possessions, which wealth has labored over, and
hands have built; not dignity, except only that according to the image;
not power, which time corrupts, and death reaps. Naked you entered the
world; would that you might also go out of life naked of sins! We
brought nothing into life, nor can we carry anything out. And do we
carry out nothing of the things of life, O Paul? Nothing of the things
of life, except only virtues, if we have practiced them. We carry out
temperance, if we have blossomed in it. We carry out almsgiving, if we
have grown rich in it. These are advocates of the soul, anchors of life.
Here wealth remains behind, gold is seized, silver is divided,
possessions are sold, glory is forgotten, power ceases, fear is
quenched. For with the stage of life the costume is dissolved. What
then? Having food and coverings, we shall be content with these. I flee
the superfluous as useless, and I seek the necessary as beyond
accusation. There the rich man stands naked; if he has virtues, he is
rich there also; but if he is naked of these, he is a beggar forever.
For nothing is richer than virtuous poverty. Peter was poor; but he
spoiled death. John was poor; but he raised up a lame man's feet. Philip
was poor; but he saw the Father in the Son. Matthew was poor; for he
stripped off wealth together with the seizure. Thomas was poor; but he
dug an inexhaustible treasure, the side of the Master. Paul was poor;
but he became =31.1721= heir of paradise. The Master according to the
flesh was poor; for in divinity He is immeasurably rich. For behold,
today He poured out the wealth of healing, He freed Peter's
mother-in-law from disease, by His touch He put the fever to flight. He
raised the suffering woman without making a circuit; matter did not
appear, and healing was wrought at once. Rich labors were spent, and
there was none who had mercy. But here the physician of nature merely
stood by, and the winter of the body was loosed. And see the wonder with
me. He healed three at once, two men and one woman: Lazarus, the widow's
son, the daughter of Jairus; and hear why, for the word is ineffable. In
order to show that He Himself is Lord of law and grace, therefore He
saves the like image in others. For just as there He set over the people
three leaders, Moses, Aaron, and Miriam. Hear Him crying in the
prophets: My people, what have I done to you, or in what have I wronged
you? Answer me; because I sent before your face Moses and Aaron and
Miriam. Moses in the person of the Law; Aaron in the person of the
prophets, and Miriam in the person of the Church, whom the uncleanness
of idols had maimed, and the loving-kindness of the Incarnate healed.
Therefore the Church, like Miriam, having been freed from the
intelligible Pharaoh, the devil, and from the tyranny of the demons,
seeing the old man drowned under the sea of the font as Egypt, taking
the tambourine of thanksgiving, and fitting to the wood of the Cross the
strings of the virtues, striking it cries: Let us sing to the Lord, for
He has been gloriously glorified. He came down from heaven, and was not
separated from the Father; He was born in a cave, and did not leave the
throne bare; He was laid in a manger, and did not empty the paternal
bosom; having been incarnate of a virgin He was born, and remained, as
God, without a father; He descended, and was not separated from the
things above; He ascended, and made no addition to the Trinity; He
appeared in the form of a servant, and did not lose the equal honor with
the Father; but He is the Word and image, and radiance, and impress.
Word, for He never departs from the Mind; image, not a wax-poured board,
but a seal of equal type; radiance, for the light is coeternal with the
sun; impress, for he who has seen the Son has seen the Father. To Him be
the glory and the power with His only-begotten Son and His life-giving
Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
