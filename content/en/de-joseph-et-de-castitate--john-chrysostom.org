On Joseph and on Chastity.
=56.587=
Helmsmen who are greatly tossed on the sea, for whom neither mainland, nor
mountain, nor hill, nor any reefs mark the dry land, looking toward certain
stars, and steering the vessel by them, remain unshipwrecked; but the
disciples of the Church, who find themselves on the sea of this life, looking
not toward any stars, but stretching the thought of the mind toward the
fathers, and following their footsteps with the desire of the soul, drive on
into the same harbor of the kingdom. For this very reason our Lord also
proclaimed their life in the sacred Books, that we might not only praise them
when we hear, but that, having longed for their way of life, we might also
emulate it.
For knowing that the nature of men is by nature a lover of the beautiful, and
always, through love of honor, reaching after the better things, the Lord has
set forth the contests of the fathers as certain ornaments, so that each of us
may reshape himself toward whichever athletic struggle he wishes. The life of
blessed Job, then, is an excellent teacher of endurance, that of Moses of
forbearance, that of David of meekness, that of Daniel of fasting and prayer,
that of Joseph and of blessed Susanna of chastity. Fitting and desirable also
for the young men is the life of Joseph, and for the young women, especially
those living in marriage, the virtuous contest of blessed Susanna; for the
struggle is of equal weight for both. For he trampled the fear of a mistress
because of chastity, and she set aside the death-bearing threats of rulers
because of chastity. But first let the life of blessed Joseph be drawn for us,
since the father is also more ancient; and then in this way that of blessed
Susanna. The blessed Joseph, then, sprung from noble parents, being still a
youth, the first down of hair just putting forth on his cheeks, like a
blooming rose crowning the flower of his form in the midst of the golden
beard, preserving in the pupils of his eyes the look of a spring violet
turning purple, having teeth white as milk, glowing in the lips, having
eyebrows spread under a lily-colored forehead, joined to one another by the
thickness of the hair. And why do I attempt to describe the youth by beauty,
whom the Scripture praised exceedingly, saying, Fair in form, and very
beautiful in appearance? This one is sold through the envy of brothers, as it
seemed to them, toward slavery, but as it was predetermined by God, toward
kingship; and the boy was led into Egypt as a paltry lad; for those who bought
him did not yet see the wealth of his virtues, and they did not know that he
was about, through the comeliness of chastity, to be master of all Egypt, and
to save the whole of it as it was perishing by famine. He is led then into
Egypt, and is sold a second time, and is not enslaved even once.
For he who is free in purpose, even if he is sold ten thousand times, is not
enslaved even once; just as, again, one who is a slave in his ways, even if he
is set free ten thousand times, is not set free even once; for the man whom
his own manner does not set free, countless papers and black letters cannot
set free. He is led then into Egypt, he is sold into an Egyptian house, he is
loved by the master, he is loved by the mistress; but the master's desire is
good, and the mistress's passion is wicked. For he loved him through
reverence, and she through the frenzy of incontinence; and as far as the time
was prolonged, and the beauty of his form was in bloom, by so much did that
woman's terrible passion for him increase, and the implanted fire of the
love-charm was spreading through her heart. And indeed, planning to deceive
the young man, she went forward through all the things that deceive the eyes
of the young: painting her face like a meadow in bloom, reddening the cheeks
with rouge, whitening the forehead with white lead, and lining the eyes with
black, adorning her neck and her hands with golden ornaments, and perfuming
her soft garment with various scents; and she was simply a death-bearing
snare, thinking to deceive the young man through all the senses, the sight
from the apparent adornment, bewitching the hearing with flatteries, and
enchanting the sense of smell with the perfumes.
But see with me the courage of a noble athlete =56.588=; for I think that God
allowed the temptation to be brought against the boy, that he might both make
manifest hidden virtue in a youth, and leave that man's life to the Church as
a very great school. For there is no good thing done by the fathers which God
has not set up in the Church as a stele bearing a memorial of the life. See
with me, then, the courage of a noble athlete, how many things were fighting
against him. His own youth was at war with him, pleasure was tickling from
within, and the woman from without was wrestling besides, fawning with the
promise, and flattering with the fawning; the women sent in she used as
levers, and the flattery was scratching his hearing and his sense; but none of
these things was able to prevail over him. For neither, being made slack by
the flatteries, did he yield the reins, nor, being puffed up by the promises,
did he despise chastity, nor, being humbled by the threats, did he betray the
wealth of the virtues; but he bridled all the members of his body with chaste
reason. And when she could not deceive the young man through so many
instruments that were in her, she herself, in a certain little chamber,
secretly and violently held him toward a disordered bed. One could see, then,
a very great theater, not earthly, but heavenly. For Joseph was fighting in
the pit of the temptation; the stadium was spread open, the contests were
being accomplished, the trumpet was crying, the president of the games looked
down from above upon the wrestling, and the people of the angels, bending
forward, were watching; the demons below were setting forth the prizes for the
Egyptian woman, and the angels above were weaving the crowns for Joseph; for
demons were running with the Egyptian woman, and angels were breathing with
Joseph. Great was the contest, who would conquer whom; there was much care
among the angels concerning Joseph, lest perhaps the perfumes of the woman
should make the youth slack, lest the soft garments, lest the glance, lest the
posture, lest the gait, lest the hearing, lest the fawning, lest the flattery
should unnerve the youth; much was the care among the angels, much the hope of
the demons. Joseph was contending for a crown, the Egyptian woman was fighting
for shame; he for life, and she for death. And what sort of things were those
said by the Egyptian woman? Some in threats, and some in flatteries; with the
threats frightening the youth, and with the flatteries bewitching him; and
with the threats saying: I am your mistress, you have become my purchased
slave; for this very need you were sold to me; and if you speak against it,
bonds and prisons await you, and after these an inexorable death; but if you
are persuaded, honors, and escorts, and the authority of my whole house will
be given into your hand. But do you fear the exposure? It is possible to
escape the notice both of my husband and of those in the house; only be
persuaded, only consent to the deed, and you shall be master of all. Hearing
these things Joseph said to the Egyptian woman: O woman, I have never become a
slave; for I am a descendant of noble parents; for Abraham and Isaac, who
conversed with God, have become my grandfathers, and Jacob, who wrestled with
an angel, is my father; for this reason I also wrestle with you boldly; but I
have been sold to you through the envy of brothers; yet the name of slavery
will harm in nothing the nobility of my soul.
For an envious mist often coming upon the bright rays of the sun dims them for
a little, but does not darken them to the end; for by the rush of the solar
light it is driven out at once; in the same way the name of slavery also will
not quench the brightness of my nobility; for before long it will be scattered
by the ray of my manners, and the beauty of my nobility will shine out through
all Egypt. I have been sold to you, I too agree, I do not deny it; I have
fulfilled the service of slavery that befits you; I have become to you without
falsehood, harmless, blameless, without guile, inoffensive, not open to
accusation; no charge of disorder has arisen for you against me; and now you
counsel me to do an unholy deed, and secretly, as a serpent, you whisper to
me, thinking to bait me, and with death-bearing threats to bend me down toward
the drama of incontinence that you desire? Do not be deceived, woman, do not
attend to my youth; even a new wineskin can bear old wine, and a new purse
=56.589= can carry ancient pearls. You see my fresh green growth, but look
upon my hoary character; nature furnished me a younger appearance; but the law
has worked my heart gray. Do not be deceived, woman, you will not conquer my
virtue; you will not conquer Joseph the son of Jacob, that Jacob who while
still in the womb also struck the heel, and after these things had strength to
wrestle with an angel; do not be deceived, I wrestle the same things as my
father. But you think to fawn on me with promises also. Tell me, what such
thing can you promise me as chastity is? What kingdom is of equal weight with
it, what treasure is of equal standing with it? Do you not know that chastity
is a heavenly possession, an angelic matter, a gift of God? Keep your
promises; your honors are procurers of dishonor; your honors, which you
promise, are firstfruits of shame; your gold is an offspring of earth, and
food for rust; but my gold is an offspring of God, an everlasting shoot. But
you think the deed will escape notice. Do you not know the unsleeping eye of
God, which oversees all the things that come to pass in the darkness? And how
many angels do you think stand here now? And under how many witnesses do the
things being done take place? Consider, woman, your husband, your brothers,
your friends, your neighbors, your kinsmen, those who boast in your gravity;
consider the angels who are present, and either be ashamed before them, or
have regard for the men who are absent. And with what eyes will you look upon
your husband, against whom you have chosen to plot? And what arms will you
open to him, whom you wished to put to death? With what words will you flatter
him, whose bed, so far as it lay with you, you have defiled? Remember how much
you promised him: how you would keep the pledge of chastity for him, how with
oaths you were affirming that you loved him more than yourself. Imitate,
woman, the turtledove; for she, having been joined to one husband, is no
longer joined to another; and if it happens that her male is caught by a
fowler, or consumed by an eagle, she does not mix with another male, but
remains always awaiting the one she longs for, and she dies in the memory of
him. Let there instruct you, woman, either the fear of God, or the law of the
Hebrews, or the august way of life of the ancients.
Joseph having said these things, and seeing her still holding his garments
shamelessly, and burning more toward the frenzy (for loves are then kindled
more toward the thing longed for, when they cannot attain it), like a noble
athlete, having stripped off the garments, and having emptied her wholly with
the garments onto the ground, leaped out naked of the defilement from the pit
=56.590=, bearing the victory of chastity. And the Egyptian woman was holding
the leaves of disorder, but Joseph was bearing the fruit of righteousness.
There was joy in heaven over the victory of Joseph; angels were rejoicing,
demons were weeping, Joseph was being crowned, the Egyptian woman was being
put to shame; the victory laid hold of her; she turns to slander, and she
accuses Joseph to her own husband, attaching shame to him by her words,
saying, Why did you bring in a Hebrew boy here to mock us? The husband is
indignant, and casts him into prison. For the lamp was necessary for those in
darkness, the giver of grain for those who hunger, the harbor for those
storm-tossed, the physician for those who are sick. Two eunuchs of Pharaoh
also are cast into prison; they see dreams; there was no one to interpret;
they report them to Joseph; he makes clear what was veiled. The eunuchs are
cast out of the prison; it turns out for them according to what Joseph
interpreted. King Pharaoh sees a dream; there was no one to make it clear; the
eunuch announces concerning Joseph; he is cast out of the prison because of
the dream. And see with me a mystery: Through a dream he was sold; through a
dream he was set free. What then? He interprets Pharaoh's dream, the king sees
his shrewdness, and set him free to rule, having entrusted to him all the
authority of Egypt. Joseph, having received the authority, in the time of the
famine was doing good to all, foreigners, natives, friends, enemies,
strangers; his father comes into Egypt; his mother also with the father does
obeisance to him. For even if she died on the way as she was coming, yet in
the husband she did obeisance; for man and wife are one flesh. His eleven
brothers also come, they do obeisance to him; and the vision which Joseph saw
was fulfilled: For I thought, he says, that the sun, and moon, and eleven
stars were doing obeisance to me. Why eleven? Because Judas hanged himself;
for the sold Joseph was a type of Christ sold by Judas. Do you see how through
chastity he was justified, through chastity he reigns in the heavens?
These things have been said by us, so that you also, having emulated the same
way of life, may receive the same glory from God. But we ought also to have
spoken concerning blessed Susanna, especially since we promised it; but since
it is better that they depart still thirsty, and not that those who have been
filled spit out even what they received, keeping the narrative for another
day, by the will of God, let us now run to the mixing-bowl of the mystagogy,
in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory and the might unto the ages of
the ages. Amen.
