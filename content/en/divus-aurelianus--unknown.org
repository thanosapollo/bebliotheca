On the Hilaria, when we know that all festive things both ought to be done and
said, the solemn rites being completed, Junius Tiberianus, prefect of the
city, a man of illustrious rank and to be named with the reverence already
spoken, took me into his own carriage, the official chariot,

and there, when his mind was free and at leisure from lawsuits and public
business, he began a long conversation from the Palace all the way to the
Gardens of Varius, and in it

especially about the lives of princes, and when we had come to the Temple of
the Sun, consecrated by the emperor Aurelian, because he himself drew some
blood from his line, he asked me who had put his life into writing,

and when I answered that I had read none among the Latins, but some among the
Greeks, the holy man poured out the grief

of his groan in these words:

/So Thersites, Sinon, and those other monsters of antiquity we know well, and
posterity will keep visiting them; the deified Aurelian, most famous prince,
most severe emperor, through whom the whole world was restored to the Roman
name, posterity will not know? God

avert this madness. And yet, if I know well, we have the journals of that man
written, and also the wars set out in historical style, which I would have you
take and write in order, adding what belongs to his life.

All this you will learn, according to your diligence, from the linen books in
which he himself had ordered his daily acts to be written. I will see to it
that the linen books are brought out for you from the Ulpian Library.

I would have you send Aurelian into writing as he is, so far as you can./ I
obeyed, my Ulpianus,

the command, I received the Greek books and took into my hands all that was
necessary, and from them I gathered into one little book those things which

were worthy of memory. I would have you take my gift in good part, and if you
are not content with this, read the Greeks, and seek out even the linen books,
which the Ulpian Library will furnish you when you wish.

And since we had talk of Trebellius Pollio, who handed down to memory the
emperors from the two Philips as far as the deified Claudius and his brother
Quintillus, the famous as well as the obscure, in that same carriage,
Tiberianus asserting that Pollio had related many things carelessly, many
things too briefly, I saying on the other side that no writer, so far as
history is concerned, had not lied in some point, and even showing in what
Livy, in what Sallust, in what Cornelius Tacitus, in what last of all Trogus
were convicted by clear witnesses, he came over to my opinion with his feet
and stretching out his hand

joking besides,

/Write, he said, as you please. You will say what you wish in safety, having
as companions in your falsehoods those authors of historical eloquence whom we
admire./

And lest I weave many frivolous things into my prefaces and become tedious,
the deified Aurelian was born, as more say, at Sirmium of a rather obscure
family, as some, in Dacia Ripensis.

I remember, however, that I read an author who declared him born in Moesia.
And it does indeed happen that the native soil of those men is unknown who are
of humbler place and themselves for the most part only chance upon their
native soil, so that

they may give posterity a gleam from the splendour of places, and yet for
great princes in their affairs

the chief thing to know

is not where each was born, but of what kind he was in the state.

Does it commend Plato more that he was an Athenian than that he made the
unique gift of wisdom

shine forth? Or will Aristotle the Stagirite and Zeno of Elea or Anacharsis
the Scythian be found lesser because they were born in the smallest villages,
when all the virtue of philosophy has raised them to the sky?

And, to return to order, Aurelian, born of modest parents, from his first age
most lively in talent, famous in strength, never let a day pass, even a feast
day, even a day of rest, on which he did not exercise himself with the javelin
and arrows and the rest of the duties of arms.

His mother, indeed, Callicrates of Tyre, by far the most learned writer among
the Greeks, says was a priestess of the temple of her Sun

in that village in which his parents lived;

and that she even had some gift of divination, so much so that once,
quarrelling with her husband, she flung at him, when she was rebuking both his
stupidity and his cheapness, /See the father of an emperor./ From which it is
clear that that woman knew the fated things.

The same man says that these were the omens of empire for Aurelian: first that
a serpent often coiled around his washbasin as a boy and could never be
killed, and at last his mother herself, who had seen this, would not kill the
serpent, as if it were a household

creature. To this is added that from a little purple cloak, which an emperor
of her time had offered to the Sun, the priestess is said to have made
playthings for her son.

swaddling-band, from the cradle and set him on an altar

which happened to be beside the shrine without fires. The same author is

But to omit these and such things, he was handsome and with a manly grace
good-looking, rather tall in stature, of most powerful sinews, a little too
fond of wine and food, of rare lust, of immense severity, of singular
discipline, eager to draw

the sword. For when there were in the army two tribunes Aurelianus, this one
and another, who was captured with Valerian, the army had set this man the
mark "hand to the sword," so that if by chance it was asked which Aurelian had
done or performed something, it would be supplied "Aurelian hand to the
sword," and he would be known.

There stand out many distinguished deeds of this man as a private citizen. For
he alone crushed the Sarmatians bursting into Illyricum with three hundred
garrison troops.

Theoclius, a writer of the times of the Caesars, reports that Aurelian with
his own hand in the Sarmatian war killed forty-eight in one day, and on very
many different days more than nine hundred and fifty, so much so that even the
boys made ballads and little dances

on Aurelian of this kind,

with which they used to dance in military fashion on feast days:

/A thousand, a thousand, a thousand we have beheaded. One man, a thousand we
have beheaded. Let him drink a thousand

whoever

has killed a thousand. No one has as much wine as he has shed blood./

I did not think they should be passed over in silence. The same man, as
tribune of the Sixth Gallic Legion at Mogontiacum, so crushed the Franks

This man, however, as we said above,

was so much a terror to the soldiers that under him, after he had once
corrected camp faults with huge severity, no one offended.

He alone of all so punished a soldier who had committed adultery with his
host's wife that he bent down the tops of two trees, bound them to the
soldier's feet, and suddenly let them go, so that the man, torn apart, hung on
both sides. This thing made a huge fear in all.

There is a military letter of his given to his deputy of this kind: /If you

I found recently in the Ulpian Library among the linen books a letter of the
deified Valerian written about the emperor Aurelian, which I have inserted
word for word, as was fitting.

/Valerian Augustus to Antoninus Gallus the consul. You blame me in familiar
letters because I entrusted my son Gallienus to Postumus rather than to
Aurelian, when in any case both the boy and the army ought to have been
entrusted to the more severe man. You will not

judge it so any longer, if you know well

of how great a severity Aurelian is; he is too much, he is abundant, he is
heavy, and he does not now suit our times.

I call all to witness, however, that I even feared that he would think
something too severe toward my son also, if the boy had done anything, since,
as he is by nature prone to sports, he might think too lightly./

This letter shows of how great a severity he was, so that Valerian says he
even feared him.

What shall I say this is, that so few good princes have existed, when already

See, I ask, how few good princes there are, so that it was well said by a

He had determined also to give free wine to the Roman people, so that, as oil
and bread and pork are furnished free, so wine also might be given,

which he had conceived as perpetual by this arrangement. Through Etruria along
the Aurelian Way as far as the Maritime Alps there are huge lands, and those
fertile and wooded. He had determined, then, to give prices to the owners of
uncultivated places, who nevertheless were willing, and to set captive
households there, to plant the mountains with vines, and from that work to
give wine, so that the treasury should receive nothing of the returns, but
should grant the whole to the Roman people. An account had been made of dogae,
of casks, of ships,

and of works, but many say Aurelian was prevented from doing it, others that
he was forbidden by his prefect of the guard, who is said to have said: /If we
give wine also to the Roman people, it remains that we give chickens and geese
too./

It is an argument that Aurelian truly thought this, or rather had arranged to
do it, or had done it in some part, that fiscal wines are placed in the
porticoes of the Temple of the Sun,

not to be issued free to the people but at a price. It should be known,
however, that he gave congiaria three times, that he also gave the Roman
people white tunics with sleeves from diverse provinces and plain African and
Egyptian linens, and that he himself first gave the Roman people oraria, which
the people might use for showing favour.
