* On that saying, I withstood him to the face
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Having gathered at the former assembly in the new church with the bishop, he
spoke this in the old church on the passage of the Apostle: "But when Peter
came to Antioch, I withstood him to the face"; and he shows that what took
place was not opposition, but economy.

=51.371=

αʹ. I was left away from you one day, and as if separated from you for a whole
year, so I continued distressed and restless. And that these things are true,
you know from what you yourselves also suffered. For just as a child still at
the breast, torn from the mother's nipple, wherever he is carried, turns about
continually, looking round for his own mother; so I too, having been carried
farther from the maternal bosom, was continually looking about, seeking
everywhere your holy assembly. Yet I had sufficient consolation for these
things, being persuaded that I suffered them in obedience to a tenderly loving
father, and the wage of obedience kept off the despondency that comes upon
being a guest away. For this is to me both more splendid than every =51.372=
diadem, and more august than a crown, to be led about everywhere with him who
begot me; this is to me both ornament and safety; ornament, because I have so
mastered him and drawn him to my love, that nowhere and never does he endure
to appear without the child; safety, because being present and seeing me
contending, he will in every way also furnish us the alliance from his
prayers. And just as the hands of pilots, and rudders, and blasts of the west
wind send a ship with safety into harbor; so also this man's goodwill, and
love, and the help of his prayers, better than west wind and pilot and
rudders, steer our word. And besides these things that too comforted me, that
you then enjoyed a splen =51.373= did table, and had a liberal and costly
host. And we knew this not from hearing only, but also from the experience
itself. For there were those who conveyed to us the things said, and from the
remnants we guessed the whole feast.

I praised then the one who feasted you, and marveled at the costliness and the
wealth; and I also called you blessed for the goodwill and the exactness,
because you hold the things said with such guard as even to convey them to
another. For this reason we too discourse readily toward your love. For he who
casts the seeds here does not throw them beside the way, nor pours them into
the thorns, nor sows upon the rock; so rich and deep-soiled is your field, and
receiving all things into its own bosom, it multiplies the seeds. But if ever
you have furnished me eagerness and much zeal toward the hearing, as indeed
you have always furnished it, this grace I ask you to give me today also. For
our word is not about chance matters, but about great things. Therefore I need
eyes looking sharp from every side, a mind awakened, a standing-up thought,
stretched reasonings, a soul sleepless and watchful. For you all heard the
apostolic reading; and if anyone attended sharply to the things read, he knows
that great contests and sweats lie before us today. For "When Peter came," he
says, "to Antioch, I withstood him to the face."

βʹ. Does this then not trouble each of the hearers, that Paul withstood Peter,
that the pillars of the Church collide and


fall upon one another? For pillars indeed they are, holding up and bearing the
roof of the faith, both pillars, and breakwaters, and eyes of the body of the
Church, and fountains of good things, and treasuries, and harbors, and
whatever one might say, he will not yet reach their worth; but the greater
their praises are, so much the more is our contest. Rise up then; for our word
is about fathers, so as to beat back the charges brought against them by those
without, and by those alien to the faith. "But when Peter came to Antioch, I
withstood him to the face, because he was condemned." Then also the cause of
the condemnation: "For before certain came from James, he was eating with the
Gentiles; but when they came, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing those
of the circumcision. And the rest of the Jews also dissembled with him; so
that even Barnabas was carried away with their hypocrisy. But when I saw that
they walked not uprightly toward the truth of the Gospel, I said to Peter
before all." And above he says, "To the face"; and here, "Before all." Observe
this, the saying, "Before all." "If you, being a Jew, live as a Gentile, and
not as a Jew, why do you compel the Gentiles to judaize?" Perhaps you praised
Paul =51.374= for the boldness, because he was not ashamed of the dignity of
the person, for the truth of the Gospel he did not blush at those present. But
even if this is a praise of Paul, it becomes our shame. For what if Paul did
well? Yet Peter did ill, if indeed he did not walk uprightly. What then is the
profit to me, when of the pair one horse limps? For my word is not now toward
Paul, but toward those without. Therefore I also exhort you to attend. For I
even increase the accusation, and make it greater, that I may stretch your
zeal. For he who is in agony is sober, and he who fears for a father attends;
he who hears the accusation desires to receive the defense.

If then I begin to increase the accusation, do not think the things said to be
from my own mind. For I deepen your understanding by the word, I dig through
the mind, that having laid the thoughts in the depth, I may make their guard
inviolable. But the things to be said are also a praise of your city. For this
city received the contest, this the battle, or rather not the battle, but that
which seemed to be a battle, yet became more useful than all peace. For our
members are not so bound together toward one another by the wrappings of the
sinews, as the apostles were bound to one another by the bonds of love.

γʹ. Did you praise Paul? Hear then how the things said are an accusation of
Paul, if we do not hunt out the mind hidden in the words. What do you say, O
Paul? Did you rebuke Peter, when you saw him not walking uprightly toward the
truth of the Gospel? Well. For what reason then "To the face"? for what reason
"Before all"? Ought not the reproof to have been without witness? But how do
you make the court public, and make many witnesses of the accusation? and who
would not say that you do this from enmity, and envy, and contentiousness?
Were you not the one who said, "I became to the weak as weak"? And what is,
"To the weak as weak"? Condescending and wrapping their wounds, he says, and
not letting them fall into shamelessness. Then being so careful and humane
toward the disciples, did you become inhuman toward the fellow-apostle? Did
you not hear Christ saying, "When your brother sins, go, reprove him between
you and him alone"? But you both reprove publicly, and think great things of
the deed. For "When Peter came," he says, "to


Antioch, I withstood him to the face." And you do not reprove publicly only,
but also, as on a pillar, having engraved the battle in letters, you make the
memory immortal; that not those then present only, but also all men who
inhabit the world may learn through the letter what took place. Did the
apostles so treat you in Jerusalem, when you went up after fourteen years to
communicate to them the Gospel? Do you not say that "After fourteen years I
went up, and communicated to them the Gospel, but privately to those who
seemed to be something"? What then? When you wished to communicate privately,
did they hinder you and bring you into the midst, and make it plain to all? It
cannot be said. Then you communicate privately, and no one contradicts; but
the apostle you make a show of? Was it then only there that you enjoyed this
goodwill? =51.375= Yet even when there were so many myriads of Jews, did they
not use the same wisdom toward you? taking you privately did they not say:
"You see, brother, how many myriads there are of Jews who have come together,
and these all are zealots of the law, and they have been informed concerning
you, that you teach apostasy from the law. What then is it? Do what we say to
you. There are men among us having a vow on themselves; taking these, shave
yourself with them, and be purified with them, that they may know that of the
things they have been informed concerning you, there is nothing." Did you see
how they spare your reputation? how they hide you with the mask of that
economy, wrapping you with the sacrifice, with the purifications? Why did you
not display so much care also?

δʹ. But if what took place were truly a battle and contentiousness, these
accusations would have had reason; but now it is not a battle, but it seems to
be, yet it displays a great wisdom both of Paul and of Peter and goodwill
toward one another. Yet for the present let us hear the seeming accusation
itself. "But when Peter came to Antioch, I withstood him to the face." Why?
"Because he was condemned." And what is the manner of the condemnation? "For
before certain came from James, he was eating with the Gentiles; but when they
came, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing those of the circumcision."
What do you say? Is Peter cowardly and unmanly? Was he not called Peter for
this, because he was unshaken in the faith? What are you doing, O man?
Reverence the title of the Master, which he set upon the disciple. Peter
cowardly and unmanly? and who will endure you saying these things? Jerusalem
does not know these things of him, nor that first theater, and the Church,
into which he first leaped, and first sent forth that blessed voice, and said:
"This Jesus God raised up, having loosed the pangs of death." And again: "For
David did not go up into heaven. But he himself says," he says, "'The Lord
said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool
of your feet.'" This man then, tell me, cowardly and unmanly, who, when so
much fear and so many dangers were hanging over, with so much boldness went in
to those bloodthirsty dogs, still boiling with wrath, and breathing murder,
and said that the one crucified by them both rose and is in the heavens, and
sits at the right hand of the Father, and surrounds his enemies with myriads
of evils?

That he was able to lift up a mouth, that he was able to open lips, that he
was able to stand, that he was able even only to appear among those who
crucified him, do you not marvel at him and crown him, tell me? For what word,
what mind will be able to set forth his boldness on that day and his
free-speaking? There is none. For if before the cross the Jews agreed, if
anyone should confess him Christ, to make him out of the synagogue, after the
cross and the burial, hearing him not confessing Christ only, but also
proclaiming the whole economy together with all philosophy, how did they not


tear him apart, and limb from limb =51.376= all divide him, who first of all
dared their madness?

εʹ. For this indeed is the great thing, not that he confessed Christ, but that
before all the others, while they were raving and swollen from the murder, he
confessed with boldness. Just as then in war and battle-array, when a phalanx
is locked, we marvel most at that one who leaps before the others and breaks
its front (for not of this only, but also of the successes that come after by
others, this man would be the cause of all, who furnished the beginning and
the entrance), so also one must reckon concerning Peter, that having first
gone in, and broken the front of the Jewish phalanx, and stretched out that
long oration, he thus also gave entrance to the other apostles.

And if John, and if James, and if Paul, and if anyone else after these appears
doing some great thing, this man has the advantage of all, who made a way for
their boldness, and opened the entrance, and gave them, as to a river borne
with much stream, to come in after with much fearlessness, and to sweep away
those who oppose, and continually to water the souls of those who hear with
goodwill. Was he then such after the cross? but before the cross was he not
hotter than all? was he not the mouth of the apostles? when all were silent
did he not himself speak? "Whom do men say that I the Son of man am?" says
Christ; and some said Elijah, and some Jeremiah, and some one of the prophets.
"But you, whom do you say," he says, "that I am?" Then Peter answering said:
"You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." "You," he said, and instead
of all the body he speaks, so Peter was the tongue of the apostles, and
instead of all he himself answered. Was he then such here only, but elsewhere
does he slacken the zeal? By no means; but through all and in all he shows the
same heat. For indeed when Christ said: "They will deliver up the Son of man,
and will scourge, and will crucify," he himself says: "Be it far from you,
Lord; this shall not be to you." Let us not then examine this, that the answer
was inconsiderate, but that it was of genuine and boiling desire. Again he
went up into the mountain, and was transfigured; Elijah was seen there and
Moses conversing. Again there too Peter: "If you will, let us make here three
tents."

ς ʹ. See how he loved the teacher, and consider the exactness, and the
understanding. For since then having answered simply he was muzzled, here he
commits the matter to the authority of the teacher; saying, "If you will." For
it happens, he says, that now too I speak inconsiderately, moved by desire.
That he may not then receive the same rebuke: "If you will," he says. That
holy and fearful supper was there again; and then when Jesus said: "One of you
will betray me," again Peter, because of the rebuke already given, did not
dare to ask the teacher; but because of the desire he had, he could not bear
to be silent; but he was eager both to learn, and not to seem some rash and
inconsiderate person. =51.377= How then did he both fulfill the desire, and
provide safety for himself beforehand? That by wishing to learn he might show
the ungovernable desire, and by not doing this through himself, but putting
forward another, he might display the reverence and all the gentleness. For I
am straitened on every side, he says; there is word of the Master's betrayal;
great is the danger, the precipice on either side. If I am silent, the care
eats my soul; if I


speak, I fear lest I receive a rebuke again. He came then a middle way, and he
who everywhere leaped forward, then needed the boldness of John, so as to
learn what was said. For he breathed nothing else, and had continually in his
soul, but the teacher only. Therefore he dared after these things both prisons
and myriads of deaths, and laughed at all the present life. For him even
having received scourgings, and having the weals on the back, he said to those
who had scourged: "We cannot but speak the things we have seen and heard." Did
you see an unenslaved mind? did you see an unconquered boldness? did you see a
soul full of heavenly desire and love?

How then do you dare to say that fearing those of the circumcision, he
withdrew himself and separated? And many other things too it was possible to
say about Peter, showing his heat, and his courage, and the desire he had
concerning Christ; but that we may not lengthen the word unseasonably, the
things said suffice. For it is not set before us today to speak his praise,
but to loose the seeming inquiry, and bring it to an end.

ζʹ. But do you consider also from another place how the accusation is not
persuasive. For then at the beginning, when he was saying: "This Jesus, whom
you crucified, God raised up, having loosed the pangs of death," then he was
among enemies, still murderous, still boiling with wrath, still wishing to
tear the disciples apart. For the passion was still at its height in them, and
the mind was swollen with wrath. But now, when Paul wrote these things, the
preaching had the seventeenth year. For having said: "After three years I went
up to Jerusalem," he says again, that "After fourteen years I went up to
Jerusalem." He then who then at the preludes of the preaching did not fear,
now after so much time fears? he who did not dread in Jerusalem, in Antioch is
afraid? he who, when enemies had circled him, was not then terrified, now when
not even enemies are present, but believers and disciples, is in agony, and is
afraid, and does not walk uprightly? And how would these things have reason,
to dare when the pyre is kindled and raised on high, but when it has been
quenched and become ash to fear and tremble? If Peter was cowardly and
unmanly, at the beginning of the preaching, in the metropolis of the Jews,
where all the enemies were, then he would have feared, not after so much time
in the most Christian city, nor when friends and genuine ones were present. So
that neither the time, nor the place, nor the quality of the persons lets us
believe the things said as they have been spoken, and condemn Peter of
cowardice.

Did you praise the things said? And yet at the beginning you were marveling at
=51.378= Paul, and you were struck at his boldness; but behold the word has
turned the accusation round. But just as when beginning I said, that there is
no profit to me, if while Paul does well, Peter is shown not doing well (for
the charges and the shame against us remain, whether this one or that one
happens to have erred), so now also I say the same again, that there is no
profit to me, if when Peter has put off the accusation, Paul appears accusing
the fellow-apostle boldly and inconsiderately. Come then, let us release this
one too from the charges. What then? Is Peter such, and Paul not such? and
what could be hotter than Paul, who died each day for Christ? But now our word
is not about courage, (for what is this to the matter at hand?) but whether he
was disposed with enmity toward the apostle, or whether this battle was of
some vainglory and contentiousness. But this too cannot be said; God forbid.
For not only of Peter the chief of those holy ones, but of all the apostles
simply Paul was a slave, and this though having the advantage of all according
to the labors; yet nevertheless he thought himself to be last


of all. "For I am," he says, "the least of the apostles, who am not sufficient
to be called apostle"; and not only of the apostles, but also of all the
saints simply. "For to me," he says, "the least of all saints, this grace was
given."

ηʹ. Did you see a crushed soul? did you see how he sets himself lower than all
the saints, not of the apostles only? And he who was so disposed toward all,
knew also of how much presidency Peter ought to enjoy, and he reverenced this
man most of all men, and as he was worthy, so he was disposed concerning him.

And this is clear from there. The whole world looked toward him, the cares of
the Churches everywhere on earth were hung upon that soul, he was anxious
about myriads of matters each day, from every side cares circled him,
presidencies, corrections, counsels, exhortations, teachings, economies of
myriads of affairs; and leaving all those, he went away to Jerusalem, and
there was no other pretext of the journey, but to see Peter, as he himself
says: "I went up to Jerusalem to visit Peter"; so he honored him, and set him
before all. What then? having seen him did he immediately withdraw? By no
means; but he remained with him fifteen days. If then you should see some
general, tell me, noble and marvelous, when the war is joined, the
battle-array standing, the battle boiling, myriads of affairs calling him from
every side, leaving the array, and going away to visit some friend, do you
seek another greater proof than this, tell me, of goodwill toward that man? I
do not think so. Reckon this then also upon Paul and Peter. For here too a
hard war was standing, and there was an array, and a battle, not toward men
only, but toward the principalities, toward the powers, toward the
world-rulers of the darkness of this age, and a battle about the salvation of
men; but =51.379= nevertheless he so reverenced Peter, that even when so great
a necessity was lying upon him and pressing, he ran out for him to Jerusalem,
and remained with him fifteen days, and then returned. You have known the
courage of Peter, you have learned the courtesy of Paul, that toward all the
apostles, that toward Peter himself; it is necessary thereafter to come to the
solution of the question. For if this man too loved Peter, and that one was
not cowardly and unmanly, and the contentiousness and the opposition did not
come from the soul, what ever is what is said? and for what reason were these
things economized?

θʹ. Here attend, and rise up for me, and stretch yourselves, so as to receive
the defense clearly. For it is absurd that I who dig should endure so much
toil, but you who are about to see the gold from ease, should by sloth run
past this gain. And it is necessary to bring the word a little higher, so as
to make the teaching clearer to you. For when Jesus went up into the heavens,
having fulfilled the economy on our behalf, he left the word of the teaching
to his own apostles, as Paul says: "Having placed in us the word of
reconciliation"; and again: "We are ambassadors on behalf of Christ, as God
entreating through us," that is, instead of Christ. Then therefore, when
these were preaching throughout the whole world, there was no heresy; but
the whole nature of men had these two doctrines, the one sound, the other
corrupted. For
either Greeks, or Jews, were all who inhabit the earth; neither Manichaean,
nor Marcion, nor Valentinus, nor anyone else simply; for why must one list all
the heresies? for after the wheat then the tares were sown, the manifold
corruption of the heresies. The Jews then he


entrusted to Peter, and over the Greeks Christ set Paul. And this I do not say
from myself, but one may hear Paul himself saying: "For he who worked in Peter
unto apostleship of the circumcision, worked also in me," he says, "unto the
Gentiles"; calling circumcision here the nation itself.

And from where is it clear? From what follows. For having said, "He who worked
in Peter unto apostleship of the circumcision, worked also in me," he says,
"unto the Gentiles," it is of one showing that in contradistinction to the
Gentiles he said circumcision. But in contradistinction to the Gentiles, it is
not circumcision, but Jews, whom he hinted at through the circumcision; as if
he said, "He who worked in Peter unto apostleship of the Jews, worked also in
me unto the Gentiles." For just as some wise king, knowing exactly the fit
man, commits to one the presidency of the horsemen, and to another that of the
foot-soldiers; so also Christ, having divided his own camp into these two
parts, entrusted the Jews to Peter, and the Greeks to Paul. And if the camps
are different, yet the king is one.

For just as there the difference of the camps is in the construction of the
weapons, not in the nature of the men; so also here the difference appears in
some small figure of the flesh, not in a change of the substance. =51.380=

ιʹ. As I was saying then, both were entrusted with these camps. And if I do
not lengthen the word, if you have not grown weary, I will tell you also the
cause, for which this one was entrusted with the Jews, and that one with those
from the Gentiles. For it is worthy of inquiry, why ever Paul, who was trained
with exactness in the ancestral law, who spent time at the feet of Gamaliel,
who according to the righteousness in the law became blameless, is not
entrusted with the Jews, but with the Greeks; but the fisherman, and
unlettered, and knowing nothing so great, Peter, was entrusted with the
presidency of the Jews. For what is said also contributes something toward our
solution, if we are able to phrase it well. For one cannot say this, that
seeing Paul shrinking and drawing back and fleeing the presidency of his own,
he did not wish to force and compel.

On the contrary he displayed everything. For not only did he not flee the
superintendence of the Jews, but he even first leaped upon it, and when Christ
commanded to go away to the Gentiles, he himself thinks it worthy to be
entrusted with the economy of the Jews; and suffering myriads of terrible
things from them in many places, and having been entrusted with the teaching
of the Gentiles, he does not cease entreating on behalf of those, and saying,
now: "I could wish to be anathema for my brethren, my kinsmen according to the
flesh"; and now: "Brethren, my heart's desire, and the request toward God, is
on their behalf unto salvation." For what reason then, when he wished and
desired to teach those, did he not allow him to preach to them, but instead of
those sent him as teacher to the Gentiles?

Let us hear Christ himself speaking, and Paul narrating the whole: "And it
came to pass to me as I prayed," he says, "that I became in a trance, and knew
Christ saying to me: Make haste and go out quickly, because they will not
receive your testimony concerning me." And he said the cause of the going
abroad: "They will hate you," he says, "and will turn away; for this they will
not endure you teaching." And yet this very thing was enough to make him a
trustworthy teacher, and to persuade those, that the change did not come about
as human. For never would a man have been able, in the very midst of the
madness, to change one so appearing, and boiling with wrath, and breathing
murder, and not persuaded by Christ working wonders, nor by his apostles who
raised the dead, and to persuade him to display again the excess which he
showed against the preaching, this whole and much more, on behalf of the


confession unto Christ; but this transfer and the change was truly a work of
divine power.

ιαʹ. Which then Paul also, desiring to take their presidency, put forward
toward Jesus saying: "Lord, they themselves know, that I was imprisoning, and
beating those who believe in your name; and when the blood of Stephen, your
martyr, was poured out, I was consenting to his death." And the much
madness guarantees the change that came about all at once, that it is not some
human thing, but from above, and from the heavens it took the beginning. What
then does Christ? "Go, because to Gentiles far off I will send you out." Are
these things then not enough, he says, =51.381= to persuade even those who are
extremely insensible, that this preaching is not human, but all the things
that have come about are above human nature, and it is truly God who
transferred and changed? Enough indeed, O blessed Paul, if you examine the
nature of the things themselves; but Jews are more unfeeling than all; not
examining the nature of things, not looking at the likely and the reasonable
and the necessary, but looking to one thing only, how they may fill their own
contentiousness. And you look toward the sequence of the things; but God knows
the secrets of their mind.

For this he says, "Go, because to Gentiles far off I will send you out," so as
also by the distance to console the hatred. For this, writing to all the
others, he adds his name in the preface of the letters, but writing to
Hebrews, he did no such thing, but simply, not saying who he was, or to whom,
as he was accustomed to do, he began somehow thus: "God, having spoken of old
in many parts and in many ways to our fathers" And this indeed was of the
wisdom of Paul. For that the letters may not share the hatred, as with some
mask, having hidden himself by the taking-away of the name, he thus secretly
applies to them the drug of the exhortation. For when we are disposed
unpleasantly toward someone, even if he says something sound, we do not
receive the things said readily, nor with pleasure; which then, that it may
not happen then also, he took away his own title from the letter, so that this
may become no hindrance to the hearing of the letter. For not only the
unbelieving Jews, but even those who had believed themselves hated him and
turned away. When at any rate he went up to Jerusalem, hear what James says to
him, and all the rest: "You see, brother, how many myriads there are of Jews
who have come together; and these all are zealots of the law, and they have
been informed concerning you, that you teach apostasy from the law." For this
they especially hated him and turned away.

ιβʹ. The cause then, for which he was not entrusted with the Jews, but with
those from the Gentiles, is this. And having been entrusted thereafter with
those, he did not lead them to the faith similarly to Peter, nor through the
same way, but through another. And when you hear "another," do not think there
is a difference in the preaching. For both preached the same things both to
Jews and to Greeks; such as, that Christ is God, that he was crucified and
buried, and rose, and is at the right hand of the Father, that he is about to
judge living and dead, and as many such things as there were, similarly both
Paul and Peter preached. In what then was the difference? In the observation
of foods, in circumcision, in the other Jewish customs. For Peter did not dare
to say to his own disciples openly and expressly, that one must stand away
from these once for all. For he feared, lest perhaps wishing to pull up this
custom before the time


he might pull up also the faith in Christ with those, the soul of the Jews not
yet enduring, because of the long-standing prejudice concerning the law, to
hear these words. For this the blessed Peter endured them judaizing. And just
as some excellent farmer, having set a tender plant near an aged tree
=51.382=, does not dare, nor endure to pull up the aged tree, fearing lest,
those roots being drawn up, the young plant also be drawn up with them, but
waits first for that to be fixed well, and to be rooted down in the very bosom
of the earth, and then with fearlessness draws up what has grown old, fearing
nothing any longer concerning the young plant; so also the blessed Peter did;
he waited for the faith being a young plant to be fixed well in the souls of
the hearers, that when that has been rooted he may thereafter with
fearlessness take away all the Jewish prejudice. But Paul not so; for he was
released from all this necessity, preaching to Greeks, who had never shared in
law, nor heard Jewish observations. For that they did not do these things
opposing one another, but condescending to the weakness of the disciples, it
is possible to see Paul also similarly to Peter conceding these very things,
and not only conceding, but even himself cooperating, and Peter again
legislating the same freedom, which Paul also preached to all the Gentiles.
And where, he says, is it possible to see both these? In Jerusalem itself. For
this one both shaved, and sacrificed, and performed purification, the teacher
of the Gentiles. For this both the time demanded, and the presence of many
Jews. "For you see," he says, "brother, how many myriads there are of Jews who
have come together, and they have been informed concerning you, that you teach
apostasy from the law."

ιγʹ. That one then, being compelled to condescend, was judaizing; but what
took place was not of the mind, but of the economy. Again Peter, the teacher
of the Jews, and everywhere conceding circumcision and Jewish observations,
because of the weakness of the disciples, when he saw a time releasing him
from this necessity, and it was not safe to use the condescension to so great
an extent, but it was a time of doctrines and laws, hear what he says.

For when those about Paul and Barnabas went up from Antioch, to learn the
clear thing about these very matters, much inquiry having come about, Peter
standing up said: "Men brethren, you know from ancient days, how among us God
chose that the Gentiles hear through my mouth the word of the Gospel, and
believe." Then having said some other things in between, he added saying: "Why
then do you tempt God to put a yoke upon the neck of the Gentiles, which
neither our fathers, nor we were able to bear? but through faith of Jesus
Christ we believe to be saved, in the same manner as they also." Do you see
that when it was a time of condescension, Paul also was judaizing; but when it
was not a time of condescension, but one had to dogmatize and legislate, Peter
too, released from that condescension, hands over the doctrines sincere and
pure; and when these things were being said Paul was present, and heard, and
having received the letter himself, conveyed it everywhere, and one cannot say
that he was ignorant of the apostle's mind. For what reason then does he now
bring such charges, saying, that "Fearing those of the circumcision"?

ιδʹ. And that you may see also the history itself of the things said, I will
narrate to you from a little above; but attend, I exhort; for we have come to
the very depth of the solution.


James the brother of the Lord =51.383= was then overseeing the Church at the
beginning, that in Jerusalem, and he presided over all who had believed from
the Jews. And it happened that there were also in Antioch Jews, who having
believed in Christ, because they were far from Jerusalem, and saw many who had
believed from the Gentiles living fearlessly and without Jewish observations,
were quietly and little by little themselves also being led to stand away from
the Jewish custom, and to have the teaching of the faith pure and

of condescension, thereafter lived as a Gentile.

And "to live as a Gentile," Paul says this, the living without Jewish
observation, the keeping none of those legal things, such as circumcision, or
sabbath, or some such thing. Peter then living thus, certain Jews came down
from James, that is, from Jerusalem, who, because they spent time always in
the metropolis, and saw no one living otherwise, still had the Jewish
prejudice, and were dragging many of those observations. Peter seeing these,
who had come down from James and from Jerusalem, still more weakly disposed,
and fearing lest being scandalized they leap away from the faith, changed over
again, and leaving the living as a Gentile, came to the former condescension,
keeping observations of foods. The Jews then who spent time in Antioch, seeing
him doing this, and not knowing his mind, with which he was doing these
things, were themselves also carried away, and were being compelled to judaize
because of the teacher. And this is what Paul charges; and that what is said
may become clearer, I will thereafter read you the apostolic words themselves.
"But when Peter came to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was
condemned. For before certain came from James," that is, from Jerusalem, "he
was eating with the Gentiles," that is, those in Antioch. "But when certain
came from Jerusalem, learned in the law, he withdrew, and Peter separated
himself fearing those of the circumcision." Which? Those who had come down
from James; "And the rest of the Jews also were carried away with him." Which
Jews? Those who before those from Jerusalem came down were spending time in
Antioch, and keeping no Jewish observation; "So that even Barnabas was carried
away with their hypocrisy." And the seeming charge is this.

ιεʹ. And if you wish, having first set also the defenses invented by others,
then I will try to bring in my own word also, making the choice of the things
said rest upon you. How then have some loosed this inquiry? This was not that
Peter, he says, the first of the apostles, the one entrusted by the Lord with
the sheep, but some other cheap and thrown aside, and one of the many. From
where is this clear? Paul having said, that "the rest of the Jews also were
carried away with him," added, he says, "So that even Barnabas was carried
away with their hypocrisy." And the saying, "So that even Barnabas," is of one
showing that this was much =51.384= more marvelous than that Peter was carried
away. For as setting him greater, he so said, that not only Peter, but also
Barnabas; but Barnabas was not greater than that Peter. But these things are
not so, they are not. For it is not, because Barnabas was greater, that for
this he marvels more at this, but for what reason? Because that one was sent
to the circumcision, but Barnabas with Paul preached to the Gentiles, and
everywhere is yoked with Paul; as indeed elsewhere he says, "Or I only and
Barnabas, have we not power not to work?" and again, "I went up to Jerusalem
with Barnabas," and everywhere you see him teaching with Paul. It is not


then because he was greater than Peter, that for this he marvels, that he too
was carried away; but because always preaching with him, and having nothing in
common toward Jews, but teaching among the Gentiles, he too was carried away.
And that it is Peter, about whom he says all these things, is clear both from
the things above, and from the things after. For the saying, that "I withstood
him to the face," and the setting this as a great thing, was of one showing
nothing else, but that he was not ashamed of the dignity of the person; and he
would not, speaking about another, that "I withstood him to the face," have
set this as some great thing. Again, if it were another Peter, his change
would not have been so strong, as to draw even the rest of the Jews; for he
neither exhorted anything, nor advised, but only withdrew, and separated
himself; and the withdrawal and the separation was strong enough to draw all
the disciples because of the dignity of the person.

ις ʹ. That it was Peter then, is clear from these things; and if you wish, we
will also say the other solution. What then is the other? Paul charged Peter
well, he says, because he used the condescension beyond measure. For in the
same way that he himself, having come to be in Jerusalem, condescended to the
Jews, so that one also, he says, having come to Antioch, ought not to have
looked toward Jews, but toward those from the Gentiles. For just as when all
were Jews, Paul too was compelled to judaize; so where those from the Gentiles
were more, and the city furnished no necessity of condescension, he ought not
because of the few Jews to scandalize so many Greeks. But this is not a
solution, but an intensification of the question. For what I said when
beginning the word, this is not what is being pursued by us, to show that Paul
charged well; since thus the question will remain. For Peter will appear
liable to the censures; but what is sought, is to release both this one and
that one from the charges. How then will this be? If we learn the mind, with
which the one rebuked, and the other was rebuked, and unfold the very thought.
What then is the thought? Peter greatly desired even those who had come down
from Jerusalem, those from James, to release from the Jewish observation.

But if he himself had introduced this mind, and coming forward had said,
"Cease using the Jewish customs," as making an oration contrary to himself,
and to all the things done by him in the former ti =51.385= me, he would have
scandalized the disciples. Again, if Paul had stretched this word toward them,
they would not even have attended, nor endured the hearing. For those who even
without this hated him and turned away because of such a report, much more, if
they had also heard him advising these things, would have leaped away. What
then happens? To the Jews from James none of them rebuked, but Peter receives
the rebuke from Paul, that being charged by the fellow-apostle, he might
thereafter have just boldness also to strike the disciples; and Peter is
rebuked, but the disciples are set right. And this happens also upon civic
dealings. For when some owe certain arrears of civic contributions, then those
who are about to demand them of them, being ashamed and blushing to be brought
more vehemently, wishing to take more occasion and authority of vehemence
against them, arrange that others of their fellow-soldiers strip, revile,
arrange myriads of other terrible things to them while those look on, that
when these have happened, they may no longer from themselves, nor from home,
but from the necessity of others seem to attack those more vehemently; and the
insolence of others becomes to them a defense toward their own liable ones.


ιζʹ. This has happened also upon Paul and upon Peter. For the Jews owed
certain arrears. What then are these? The standing away altogether from
Judaism. Peter wished to demand these arrears more vehemently, and to exact
from them the faith pure. Wishing then to take more authority and occasion of
such an exaction, he arranges that Paul rebuke with excess, and strike, that
this feigned rebuke may furnish him a just occasion and pretext of boldness
against those. For this even beginning he says, "I withstood him to the face";
and here again, "I said to Peter before all." For if he had wished to set the
apostle right, he would have done this privately; but since this was not what
was being pursued (for he knew the mind, with which he was doing all these
things), but he was eager to make firm those who had been limping for long,
for this he makes the rebuke before all. And Peter endures, and is silent, and
does not contradict. For he knew the mind, with which Paul was rebuking; and
Peter accomplished the whole by being silent. For this man's silence became a
teaching to the Jews of being no longer held by the legal things. For the
teacher would not have been silent, he says, unless he were conscious that
Paul was rebuking justly. But if it seems good, let us hear also the rebuke
itself. "I said to Peter before all," he says, "If you, being a Jew, live as a
Gentile." Consider understanding: he did not say to him, that "You do ill
living as a Jew"; but he reproves his former manner of life, that the
exhortation and the counsel may seem to have been brought in not from Paul's
mind, but from Peter's judgment already made. For if he had said, "You do ill
keeping the law," Peter's disciples would have rebuked; =51.386= but now
having heard, that this exhortation and this correction was not of Paul's
mind, but Peter himself lived thus, and had these doctrines in his soul, both
willing and unwilling they were quiet. For this neither does Peter introduce
the mind, but endures to be cross-examined by another, I mean Paul, and is
silent, so that the teaching may become acceptable.

ιηʹ. And not from here only is it possible to see the understanding of Paul,
but also from the things said next.

For he did not say, "If you, being a Jew, were living as a Gentile, and not as
a Jew," but that "You live"; so that even now you have the same mind; and that
which is full of much understanding is what is added. For having said, "You
live as a Gentile, being a Jew," he did not add: "Why do you compel the Jews
to judaize," but how? "Why do you compel the Gentiles," he says, "to judaize,"
that by seeming to claim his own disciples, and under pretext of care on
behalf of the Gentiles, he may persuade the Jews to stand away from the old
custom. For that the charges have been feigned, is clear from the things said
themselves. For having said above, "The rest of the Jews also were carried
away with him," here he says: "Why do you compel the Gentiles to judaize?" And
yet he ought to have said, "Why do you compel the Jews to judaize?" For those
carried away were not those from the Gentiles, but Jews. But if he had said
this, the word would have seemed harsh, and belonging to him in nothing; for
he was teacher of the Gentiles; but now under pretext of care of his own
disciples he makes the rebuke unaccountable and free; and that you may learn,
that the things said were not a rebuke against Peter, but an exhortation and
teaching to the Jews in the rank of the rebuke against Peter, hear the things
next: "We by nature Jews, and not sinners from the Gentiles." For these things
thereafter are of one teaching, and he no longer sets the whole around upon
Peter, but makes common the


word. For if he had entered from the beginning as teaching, Jews would not
have endured; but now having taken the beginning from rebuke, and seeming to
make a just rebuke against Peter, as drawing those from the Gentiles toward
the observation of the legal things, he thereafter goes out fearlessly into
exhortation and counsel, as the sequence leading him to this. For that no one,
having heard, that "You compel the Gentiles to judaize," may think that it is
not permitted to those only to judaize, but to the Jews it is allowed, he sets
the word around upon the teachers themselves. What do I say, he says, about
Gentiles, or the rest of the Jews? But even we the teachers, we the apostles.
And he does not say this justification only, that "The teachers and the
apostles," but that "Even we who are Jews from ancestors have stood away from
the law once for all."

What defense then do we have, drawing others into this? Do you see how
secretly he touches the Jews, and composes the teaching complete? For having
said, "We by nature Jews, and not sinners from the Gentiles"; he also sets a
reasonable cause, for which they stood away from Judaism: "Knowing that a man
is not justified from works of law, except through faith of Jesus Christ; and
we believed unto Christ Jesus, that we may be justified from faith of Christ,
and not from works of law; because a man is not justified from works of law,
except through faith of Jesus Christ."

ιθʹ. Do you see how continually he remembers both the weakness of the law, and
the righteousness according to the faith? And densely he turns the names,
which is not of one re =51.387= buking, but of one teaching, and counseling.
But, as I said, if stretching toward Jews he were saying these things, the
whole would have flowed away and been lost, those not enduring this man's
teaching; but since he turned the word toward Peter, those secretly reaped the
benefit, Peter being rebuked and being silent, and all his mind being
uncovered, not from himself, but from the fellow-apostle, and the former
manner of life being brought into the midst. Then, that they may not say among
themselves, "What then if both Peter and Paul did ill?" he sets just and
undisputed causes, for which one must not hold to the Jewish customs. And
these are, that the law cannot justify, but the faith only. And here he uses
the word more mildly; but going forward he makes it more vehement and more
violent. "But if seeking to be justified in Christ we ourselves also were
found sinners, is Christ then a minister of sin?" And what he says is such:
The faith justifies, and commands to stand away from the Jewish customs, as
having ceased thereafter; but if the law still holds and is lord, and he who
has left it is judged of transgression, Christ will be found, who commanded us
to leave it, having become to us cause of the transgression, and not only not
having released us from sin, but even having thrown us into sin.

For if through the faith we left the law, and to leave the law is sin, the
faith, through which we left the law, this became cause of our sin. And since
thereafter he set the word around into an absurdity, he did not even need some
construction toward overthrow, but was satisfied with the "God forbid," as the
absurdity being confessed from itself; "For if," he says, "the things I
destroyed, these I build again, I constitute myself a transgressor"; he set
the word around into the contrary, and showed, that not the transgressing of
law, but the not leaving law, this makes a transgressor; and in the rank of
his own person he again hints at Peter. For Peter destroyed the observation
of foods, having chosen to live as a Gentile. Then again having
returned toward Jews, and living with those,


he will be found building the things he destroyed.

κʹ. Do you see how everywhere he holds to the judgment of Peter, and uncovers
his former manner of life; that not from Paul's tongue, but =51.388= from
Peter's mind, which he displayed through the deeds, the Jews may seem to
receive the exhortation? For these reasons he says, "Fearing those of the
circumcision," and "Because he was condemned," and "That he does not walk
uprightly toward the truth of the Gospel." Not being so; God forbid; for
through many things we have shown this; but just as then Paul was rebuking,
and hearing these things Peter was silent, so as not to overturn Paul's
economy, and he accepted, as not having done rightly, to receive the rebuke,
that this might become a defense for him toward the disciples; so also this
man now with the same mind, with which he rebuked Peter, writes these things,
which he wrote to Galatians. For if then Peter's being rebuked, and being
silent, was useful to the Jews, much more would now also these things being
said about him be useful to those of the Galatians who had been corrupted. For
just as those then spending time in Antioch, seeing Peter rebuked with excess,
and being silent, were set right by the accusation against the teacher, and by
the silence; so also now Galatians being sick about the Jewish things, and
hearing Paul saying these things about him, such as that he was condemned, and
did not walk uprightly toward the truth of the Gospel, and that having been
rebuked upon these things he was silent, were taking a greatest teaching from
this accusation, unto no longer attending to the Jewish customs. For this both
Paul then rebuked, and now remembers the rebuke that then took place; and no
less than this one must marvel at Peter for this, having accepted all the
things said.

For he who accomplished the whole is this one, who endured to be accused, and
was silent. This is the gain of the economy. Thus each of the apostles has
been released for us from charges, and is worthy of myriads of praises, being
eager both to hear and to say all things toward the salvation of the rest.

But let us thereafter entreat the God of Paul and Peter, who bound those to
one another with the bonds of concord, to bind us too more vehemently into the
love toward one another; that having the concord according to God toward one
another, we may be able to be counted worthy to see those holy ones, and to be
found beside their eternal tents, by grace and philanthropy of our Lord Jesus
Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father and to the Holy Spirit glory,
power, honor, and worship, now and always, and unto the ages of the ages.
