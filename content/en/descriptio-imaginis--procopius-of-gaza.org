DESCRIPTION OF A PICTURE LYING IN THE CITY OF THE GAZAEANS
IN THE CITY OF THE GAZAEANS Love and the arrows of Love go everywhere and pass
through all things. And not even Zeus is free when the Loves wish it; but the
august and highest one, whose strength is not to be withstood, both longs for
Semele and busies himself about Hera, and seems a bull to Europa and swims the
sea, steered by Love; and if gold appears, Danae does not appear a maiden. And
Poseidon, when it seems good to them, leaving the sea migrates to the
mainland. And the god Apollo pursues her as she flees, and in winning he was
defeated, having gained Daphne as a plant but not as a maiden. And already the
Loves sport with her who bore them, and from there she was struck with passion
for Adonis, and the rose cries out the love. And these have stretched their
arrows even against Phaedra, as you see. 2 This is Athens, and the height of
summer, and midday. And the season is shown by the facts. For here there is
hunting and the chase, and garments that cool the bodies, and Theseus's sleep,
not, by Zeus, coming on at night; for otherwise a servant would not have sat
beside him to drive off what troubles him. And in the middle of the palace is
the couch, august and very luxurious, yielding, as it seems, to the one who
wishes to sleep, and by a change of colors always weaving the yellow with the
dark blue. 3 And the palace is held up to a height by dense columns and sends
the spectator toward a double stoa. For see, they even sink inward in depth.
For the art imitates entrances, being hollowed by the colors. And the wall
within is divided by denser arches prepared, as is likely, for the dedication
of statues. And the columns are all white, but not all smooth, the extremities
having been carved with upright hollows, and all together gleaming at their
capitals with bright gold. And a brow lies upon them; but the painter did not
leave even this unadorned, but variegated it with the pictures set upon it,
imitating images within images. 4 But if your sight has been trained toward a
finer perception and with a pure light holds fast to the smaller things, grant
me this and look. For in one place Hippolytus is there after hunting, and
having struck with his spear a lion that had not yet risen up, for it was
crouching, still nursing its rage, sitting, stretching some of its feet
forward, and himself sitting upon these that lie under its belly, he raises
the [shepherd] look[ing on] to praise, lifting his hands aloft at the marvel
and thinking he has justice for the lamb, [which he saw snatched by a lion,
being distre]ssed. And the hunter has set one foot, bending the knee aloft,
and [sets the other upon the ground. And when he has stood, having obtained
the prey], turning he stretches out his right hand, [turning] himself from the

lifts the hunting-nets upon his shoulders; for with these the beasts that
hide, having sunk into certain woods or caves, are surrounded. 5 And these

and bull, each incomplete, but filled out by the addition of the other; a man
receiving at the shoulders what the ox lacks. Therefore he also holds it by

Aphrodite, distributed as a pair. For these are female and male, taking
pleasure in one another by love. Of these the one looks about at something and
is turned toward it with her head; and the other looks toward her, held, as it
seems, by a greater charm. 10 Such wonders the painter is ambitious to give
the palace from his art. And Theseus is in the middle among them, not fighting
the Minotaur, not having struck Cercyon, not stopping Sinis from his outrage.
But these things you might see in another painting; and the present one, worn
out toward the length of the day, has turned to a couch and rests his body,
sending away the stifling heat of noon by sleep. And he was conversing with
the woman present, not lying with him; for the bed at night belongs to the
chaste; and she sits beside him upon a certain folding stool. And Theseus,
while speaking, was, as is likely, snatched away toward sleep and, having left
the speech half-finished, was made empty by the passion. 11 Whence the
painter, showing the swift stealth of Sleep and how he steals the mind and
draws down the eyelids, has wrought a certain shadowy and winged thing. And
