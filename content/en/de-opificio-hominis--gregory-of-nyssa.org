OF SAINT GREGORY OF NYSSA ON THE MAKING OF MAN. Gregory bishop of Nyssa to his
brother Peter, servant of God. If it were necessary to honor those who excel
in virtue with honors given through money, the whole world of money, as
Solomon says, would have appeared small toward becoming equal in weight to
your virtue; since the grace owed to your dignity is better than an honor
according to wealth. And holy Easter demands the accustomed gift-bringing of
love, which we offer to your greatness of mind, a gift, O man of God, smaller
indeed than to be worthy of being brought to you, yet not falling short of our
power. And the gift is a discourse, like some poor garment woven not without
toil from our poor mind. And the subject of the discourse will perhaps seem to
many to be audacious, yet it was not judged outside what is fitting. For he
alone worthily considered the creation of God, who was truly created according
to God, and whose soul was formed in the image of Him who created him, Basil,
our common father and teacher, who by his own contemplation made the lofty
ordering of the universe easy to grasp for the many, and made the world
established by God in the true wisdom known to those who are led to
contemplation through his understanding. But we, falling short even of
admiring him according to worth, intended at the same time to add what is
lacking to the things contemplated by the great man, not as interpolating his
labor by insertion (for it is not lawful that that lofty mouth should be
insulted by having our words ascribed to it), but so that the teacher's glory
may not seem deficient among his disciples. For if, the Hexaemeron lacking the
contemplation concerning man, none of those who had been his disciples should
bring any zeal toward filling up what was lacking, the blame would reasonably
have had a handle against his great glory, as if he had not wished to work
some habit of understanding in his hearers. But now, we having ventured
according to our power upon the exposition of what is lacking, if something of
such a kind should be found in our work as not to be unworthy of his teaching,
it will altogether have its reference to the teacher; but if our discourse
should not reach the lofty contemplation, he will be outside such a charge,
having escaped the blame of seeming not to wish that any skill should arise in
his disciples, while we would reasonably seem liable to those who look for
faults, as not having contained in the smallness of our heart the wisdom of
the master. And the aim set before us for 128 contemplation is not small, nor
taking second place to any of the wonders in the world, and perhaps even
greater than each of the things known, because nothing else of the things that
are has been made like to God except the creation according to man, so that
among the well-disposed of the hearers pardon for what is said is ready for
us, even if the discourse should come far behind the worth. For it is
necessary, I think, of all things concerning man, both those believed to have
come about before, and those expected to come about later, and those now
contemplated, to leave nothing unexamined. For otherwise the zeal would be
convicted as more deficient than the promise, if, man being set before the
contemplation, something of the things contributing to the subject should be
passed over. But also the things that seem to be contrary concerning him,
because the same things are not now seen about the nature as those that came
to be from the beginning, it is fitting to join together through a certain
necessary sequence, both from the scriptural guidance and from the conception
found by reasonings, so that the whole subject may agree


in a series and order of the things that seem to be contrary being brought
together toward one and the same end, the divine power thus discovering hope
for things beyond hope, and a way for things without resource. And for the
sake of clearness I thought it well to set the discourse before you in
headings, so that you may have in brief the subject of each of the arguments
of the whole treatise. CHAPTER I. In which there is a partial physiology
concerning the world, and a somewhat richer narration concerning the things
that came before the generation of man. This is the book of the generation of
heaven and earth, says Scripture; when all that is visible was completed, and
each of the things that are, having been separated toward its own place,
withdrew; when the heavenly body encompassed all things in a circle, and the
heavy and downward-tending of the bodies, earth and water, held together in
one another, took the middle region of the whole. And a certain bond and
stability of the things that have come to be, the divine art and power, was
deposited in the nature of the things that are, charioteering all things by
double activities. For by rest and by motion it contrived generation for the
things that are not, and continuance for the things that are, driving around
in a circle, after the manner of a wheel, the swiftest motion of the pole
about the heavy and unchangeable of the unmoved nature, as about some fixed
axle, and preserving for both through one another the undissolved state: the
substance carried in a circle, by the sharp motion, compressing the solid of
the earth in a circle, and the firm and unyielding, by its unchangeable
fixity, unceasingly stretching the whirl of the things circling about it. And
an equal excess was worked into each of the things standing apart by their
activities, both the stationary nature and the unstable revolution. For
neither does the earth change from its own base, nor does the heaven ever give
up the violence and slacken the motion.

And these things also first 129 according to the wisdom of the Maker were
prepared beforehand as a kind of beginning of the whole machine of the things
that are, the great Moses showing, I think, by saying that in the beginning
heaven and earth had come to be from God, that all the things appearing in
creation, those brought into generation according to the divine will, are
offspring of motion and of rest. Heaven and earth, then, standing apart from
one another diametrically according to the opposition of activity, the
creation between the opposites, sharing in part with the things lying beside
it, mediates through itself for the extremes, so that the conjunction of the
opposites with one another through the middle may become evident. For air
imitates in a way the ever-moving and fine of the fiery substance, both in the
lightness of its nature and in being well fitted toward motion. Yet it is not
such as to be alienated from kinship with the fixed things, neither remaining
always unmoved, nor flowing and being scattered continually; but by its
affinity toward the other it becomes a kind of border of the opposition of the
activities, mixing together and dividing in itself the things that stand apart
by nature. According to the same account, the moist substance also is fitted
by double qualities toward each of the opposites. For by being heavy and
downward-tending, it has much kinship toward the earthy. And by sharing in
some flowing and traveling activity, it is not altogether alienated from the
moving nature, but there is through this also a mixture and meeting of the
opposites, the heaviness having been transferred into motion, and the motion
not being fettered in the weight, so that the things standing apart at the
extreme by nature come together toward one another, being united through the
things that mediate. Or rather, according to the exact account, not even the
nature of the opposed things itself has its properties altogether unmixed
toward the other, so that, I think, all the things appearing according to the
world may incline toward one another, and creation may breathe together toward
itself, being found in the properties of the opposed things. For motion being
understood not only according to local change, but also being contemplated in
alteration and change; and again, the unchangeable nature


not admitting the motion according to being changed; the wisdom of God,
exchanging the properties, put the unalterable in the ever-moving, and
alteration in the unmoved, perhaps managing such a thing by a certain
providence, so that the property of the nature, which is the unalterable and
unchangeable, might not, upon some one of the things seen according to
creation, make the creature be thought God. For that which happens to be
moving or being changed would no longer have a supposition of divinity. For
this reason the earth is stationary, and not unalterable; and the heaven, not
having the alterable of the contrary, does not have the stationary either, so
that the divine power, intertwining alteration with the nature that stands,
and motion with that which is not altered, may both make both akin to one
another by the exchange of properties, and alienate 132 them from the
supposition concerning the divine. For neither of these, as has been said,
would be thought of the more divine nature; neither the unstable, nor that
which is changed. Already, then, all things have arrived at their own end. For
heaven and earth and all the things through the middle were completed, as
Moses says, and the several things were ordered with corresponding beauty;
heaven with the rays of the lights, sea and air with the swimming and aerial
of the animals, and earth with the manifold differences of plants and of
cattle, all which, empowered by the divine will, it brought forth together at
the same time. And the earth was full of things in season, putting forth the
fruits together with the flowers; and the meadows were full of whatever things
go over the meadows. And all ridges and mountain-tops, and all that is sloping
and level, and all that is in hollows, were crowned with the newly blooming
grass and the varied season of the trees, things just now rising from the
earth, and at once running up to perfect beauty. And all things reasonably
rejoiced, and the cattle given life by the command of God skipped, running
through the thickets by herds and by kinds. And every place that is roofed and
shady was sounded about everywhere with the songs of the musical birds. And
the sight according to the sea, as is likely, was another such, just now being
set toward quiet and calm in the gatherings of the hollows, according to which
harbors and ports, hollowed of themselves in the shores by divine will, tamed
the sea toward the mainland. And the gentle motions of the waves were made
fair against the beauty of the meadows, rippling smoothly at the very surface
under light and painless breezes. And all the wealth according to creation, on
land and sea, was ready, but he who shares it was not.

CHAPTER II. Why man is last after the creation. For this great and precious
thing, man, was not yet sojourning in the world of the things that are. For it
was not reasonable that the ruler should appear before the ruled, but, the
rule having first been prepared, it was sequential that the one who is king
should be shown, since therefore the Maker of the whole had prepared
beforehand a kind of royal lodging for the one who was going to be king. And
this was earth and islands, and sea, and heaven vaulted over these after the
manner of a roof; and wealth of every kind was deposited in these palaces. And
by wealth I mean all creation, as much as is in plants and shoots, and as much
as is perceptive and breathing and ensouled 133. And if it is necessary to
number also the materials into wealth, as many as through a certain good
coloring were judged precious to human eyes, such as gold and silver, and of
these stones which men love; and having hidden the abundance of all these in
the folds of the earth as in some royal treasuries, thus he shows man in the
world, of the wonders in it, to be spectator of some, and lord of others, so
that through enjoyment he may have the understanding of the One who supplies,
and through the beauty and


greatness of the things seen he may track out the unspeakable and beyond
reason power of the Maker. For these reasons man was brought in last after the
creation, not as something rejected thrown off among the last, but as one who
together with his generation is fitting to be king of those under his hand.
And just as some good host does not house the guest before the preparation of
the foods, but having prepared all things becomingly, and having brightened
with the suitable ornaments the house, the couch, the table, already on things
ready that are fit toward nourishment, he makes the diner at the hearth; in
the same way the rich and costly host of our nature, having adorned the
dwelling with beauties of every kind, and having prepared this great and
manifold banquet, thus brings in man, giving him as work not the acquisition
of things not present, but the enjoyment of things present. And for this
reason he lays down double starting-points of his making, mixing the divine
with the earthy; so that through both he may have kinship and fitness toward
each enjoyment, enjoying God through the more divine nature, and the good
things according to the earth through the kindred sense.

CHAPTER III. That the nature of man is more precious than all the appearing
creation. And it is worthy not to overlook this either uncontemplated, that of
so great a world and of the parts according to it that were laid down
elementally toward the constitution of the whole, the creation is in a way
improvised by the divine power, coming to subsist together with the command.
But of the making of man counsel goes before, and what will be is prefigured
by the Craftsman through the writing of the word, and of what sort it is
fitting to be, and toward what archetype it bears the likeness, and for what
it will come to be, and what it will do when it has come to be, and of what
things it will be leader; the word looks over all things beforehand, so that
it may obtain a dignity older than generation, having acquired the leadership
of the things that are before passing into being. For "God said," it says,
"Let us make man according to our image and likeness, and let them rule the
fishes of the sea, and the beasts of the earth, and the birds of the heaven,
and the cattle, and all the earth." O the wonder; the sun is made, and no
counsel goes before; heaven likewise, of which nothing according to the
creation is equal, 136 such a wonder is established by a word alone, the word
indicating neither from where, nor how, nor anything else of such a kind. Thus
also all the several things, aether, stars, the air through the middle, sea,
earth, animals, plants, all are brought toward generation by a word. But to
the making of man alone the Maker of the whole comes with consideration, so as
even to prepare beforehand matter for him of the constitution, and to liken
the form to some archetypal beauty, and having set forth the aim for the sake
of which he will come to be, to craft for him a nature corresponding and
proper to the activities, being well fitted toward what is set before.

CHAPTER IV. That the making of man indicates through all things the ruling
authority. For just as in this life the instrument is shaped by the craftsmen
correspondingly to the need; so the best Craftsman crafted our nature as a
kind of vessel fit for the activity of kingship, having prepared it by the
advantages according to the soul and by the very shape of the body to be such
as to be well fitted toward kingship. For the soul shows the kingly and
exalted from the very start,


separated far from private lowliness, from its being without a master and
self-determining, administered autocratically by its own wills. For of whom
else is this, and not of a king? And still besides this, to become an image of
the nature that has power over all things is nothing else than that the nature
was crafted a queen at once. For just as according to human custom those who
make the images of the rulers both impress the character of the form, and
write in besides the royal dignity by the wrapping of the purple, and
according to custom the image also is called king; so also the human nature,
since it was made toward rule of others, through the likeness toward the King
of the whole, was set up as a kind of living image, sharing with the archetype
both the dignity and the name; not wearing purple, nor indicating the dignity
with scepter and diadem (for neither is the archetype in these), but instead
of the purple robe clothed with virtue, which is the most kingly of all
garments; and instead of the scepter resting upon the blessedness of
immortality; and instead of the royal diadem adorned with the crown of
righteousness, so that through all things it is shown in the dignity of
kingship, having been likened with accuracy toward the archetypal beauty. 137
CHAPTER V. That man is a likeness of the divine kingship. And the divine
beauty is not made bright by some shape, and a good portion of form, through a
certain good coloring, but is contemplated in unspeakable blessedness
according to virtue. As therefore the painters transfer the human forms
through certain colors onto the tablets, wiping the proper and corresponding
dyes onto the imitation, so that through accuracy the archetypal beauty may be
transferred toward the likeness; so also consider our Fashioner, having
bloomed the image toward his own beauty with the application of the virtues as
with certain dyes, to show in us his own rule. And the colors, as it were, of
the image, through which the true form is painted, are many-formed and
various, not redness and brightness, and the qualitative mixture of these
toward one another, nor a tracing of some black anointing brow and eyes, and
according to a certain blending shading the hollows of the character, and as
many such things as painters' hands have devised; but instead of these,
purity, passionlessness, blessedness, alienation from every evil, and as many
things as are of such a kind, through which the likeness toward the Divine is
formed for men. With such flowers the Craftsman of his own image marked out
our nature. And if you should also examine together the other things through
which the divine beauty is characterized; you will find the likeness preserved
with accuracy toward those also in the image according to us. Mind and word is
the divinity; for in the beginning was the Word. And the prophets according to
Paul have the mind of Christ, the one speaking in them. Not far from these is
also the human. You see in yourself both the word and thought, an imitation of
the mind and word that truly are. Again God is love, and a fountain of love.
For this John the great says, that "Love is from God;" and, "God is love;"
this also the Fashioner of the nature has made our face. For "in this," he
says, "all will know that you are my disciples, if you love one another."
Therefore if this is not present, the whole character of the image has been
recast. The Divine looks upon all things, and hears all things, and searches
through all things. You also have the apprehension of the things that are
through sight and hearing, and the seeking and searching thought of the things
that are. CHAPTER VI. Examination of the kinship of the mind toward the
nature, in which also by the way the dogma of the Anomoeans is refuted. And
let no one think that according to a likeness of the human


activity I say that the Divine lays hold of the things that are in different
powers. For it is not possible in the simplicity of the divinity to understand
the varied and many-formed of the apprehensive activity. For neither are there
for us some many powers apprehensive of the things, even if we lay hold of the
things according to life in many ways through the senses 140. For there is
some one power, the mind itself that lies within, which goes through each of
the sense-organs and lays hold of the things that are. This one sees the
appearing through the eyes; this one understands the spoken through hearing,
both loves what is according to pleasure, and turns away from what is not
according to pleasure, and uses the hand toward whatever it wills, holding
through it and thrusting away whatever it judges to be profitable, using the
cooperation of the organ toward this. If then in man, even if the organs
prepared by nature toward sense happen to be different, the one who acts
through all and is moved and uses each correspondingly toward what is set
before is one and the same, not changing his nature together with the
differences of the activities; how would one, in the case of God, spy out the
many-parted of the substance through the various powers? For "He who fashioned
the eye," as the Prophet says, and "He who planted the ear," marked these
activities upon the nature of men as certain recognizing characters toward the
patterns in himself. For "Let us make," he says, "man according to our image."
But where for me is the heresy of the Anomoeans? What will they say toward
such a voice? How will they preserve in the things said the emptiness of their
dogma? Will they say it is possible for one image to be likened to different
forms? If the Son is unlike the Father according to nature, how does he make
one image of the different natures? For he who said, "Let us make man
according to our image," and through the plural signification indicated the
holy Trinity, would not have mentioned the image monadically, if the
archetypes were unlike toward one another. For it was not possible for a
likeness of the things that do not agree with one another to be shown into
one; but if the natures were different, he would altogether have established
different images of them also, having crafted the one corresponding to each.
But since the image is one, and the archetype of the image is not one; who is
so outside of thought as not to know that the things likened to the one also
altogether have likeness toward one another? For this reason, perhaps, the
word, cutting under this wickedness in the making of human life, says, "Let us
make man according to our image and likeness." CHAPTER VII. Why man is naked
of the weapons from nature and of coverings. But what does the upright of the
shape mean? And why are the powers toward life not grown together with the
body? but naked of the natural coverings, unarmed and poor, man is brought
onto life in need of all things toward the need, worthy according to the
appearing rather to be pitied than to be called blessed, not armed with
projections of horns, not with points of claws, not with hooves, or teeth, or
some sting having a death-bearing poison from nature, such as 141 most of the
animals possess in themselves toward defense of the things that pain; he is
not covered as to the body with the wrapping of hairs; and yet perhaps the one
set forward into rule of the others ought to have been fenced about with his
own weapons by nature, so that he might not need the help from others toward
his own safety. But now lion, and boar, and tiger, and leopard, and if there
is any other such, have sufficient power from nature toward salvation. And the
bull has the horn, and the hare the speed, and the gazelle the leap and the
security according to the eye, and another animal the size, and others the
trunk, and the birds the wing, and the bee the sting, and in all at any rate
some one thing toward


salvation has been implanted by nature; but man alone of all is slower than
those that run swiftly, shorter than those that are much in flesh, more easily
captured than those secured with the weapons grown together. And how, someone
will say, has such a one been allotted the rule according to all? But I think
it is nothing hard to show that what seems deficient of our nature is a
starting-point toward mastering those under the hand. For if man had power in
such a way as to outrun the horse in swiftness, and to have the foot unworn by
firmness, resting upon some hooves or claws, and to carry horns and stings and
nails in himself; first he would have been some beast-like and hard-to-meet
thing, such things having grown together with his body. Then he would have
overlooked the rule of the others, needing nothing of the cooperation of those
under the hand. But now for this reason the needs of life have been portioned
out upon each of the things yoked under us, so as to make the rule according
to them necessary. The slow of the body, hard to move, both brought the horse
to the need and tamed it. And the nakedness of the flesh made necessary the
oversight of the sheep, filling up from the yearly bearing of wools what is
lacking of our nature. And that the starting-points toward life are brought in
for us even from others yoked the burden-bearing of the animals to such
services. But indeed the not being able to eat grass after the likeness of the
cattle worked the ox under the hand for life, making life easy for us by its
own labors. And since there was need also of teeth and of biting toward
overcoming some of the other animals, through the harm of the teeth, the dog
furnished together with speed its own jaw to our need, becoming a kind of
living knife for man.

And stronger and more cutting than a projection of horns and a point of claws,
iron has been devised for men, not always grown together with us, as those
things with the beasts, but having fought with us at the time, remaining for
the rest upon itself. And instead of the hide of the crocodile, it is possible
even to make that one itself a weapon, having put on the skin at the time. And
if not, iron is shaped toward this also by art, which having served toward war
at the time, again left the hoplite free of the burden in peace. And the wing
of the birds also serves life, so that we are not left behind even of the
winged speed through device 144. For some of them become tame, and cooperate
with those who hunt; and others through those are brought under our needs
through device. But art, having also made winged arrows for us through device,
grants the winged speed to our needs through the bow. And that our bases are
easily hurt and easily worn toward travel makes necessary the cooperation from
those under the hand. For from there it is possible to fit sandals about the
feet. CHAPTER VIII. Why the shape of man is upright, and that the hands are
because of the word; in which there is also some philosophy concerning
difference of souls. And the shape is upright for man, and stretches up toward
heaven, and looks above. These too are ruling, and indicate the royal dignity.
For that man alone among the things that are is such, and that the bodies of
all the others have bent toward what is below, clearly shows the difference of
dignity, of those who stoop under the power, and of the authority standing
above them. For for all the others the front limbs of the body are feet,
because what is bent needed altogether the supporting; but in the making of
man the limbs became hands. For of the upright shape one base was sufficient
toward the need, supporting the standing in security with double feet. And
otherwise


also the service of the hands is a fellow-worker for the need of the word. And
someone saying that the service of the hands is a property of the rational
nature will not miss the whole, not only running off in thought toward this
common and ready thing, that we signify the word with letters through the good
nature of the hands (for this too is not without a share of rational grace,
that we speak through letters, and in a way converse through a hand,
preserving the voices by the characters of the elements); but I, looking
toward another thing, say that the hands cooperate with the utterance of the
word; or rather, before examining these things, let us understand the
discourse that was passed over.

For the thing according to the order of the things that have come to be almost
escaped us; for the sake of what the sprouting of the things growing from the
earth goes first, and the irrational of the animals comes on after, and thus
after the making of these, man. For perhaps we learn through this not only
what is understood from the ready, that the grass appeared useful to the
Creator for the sake of the animals, and the cattle because of man; for which
reason before the cattle, food of those, and before man that which was going
to serve human life. But it seems to me that Moses indicates through these
some dogma of the hidden things, and hands over through secrets the philosophy
concerning soul, which the education from outside also imagined, yet did not
understand clearly. For the word teaches us through these that the vital and
psychic power is contemplated in three differences. For there is some that is
only increasing and nourishing, bringing the corresponding toward addition of
those being nourished, which is called natural, and is contemplated about the
plants. For it is possible to understand also in the things that grow some
vital power without a share of sense 145. And there is another form of life
besides this, which both has this, and has taken on in addition the managing
according to sense, which is in the nature of the irrationals. For it not only
is nourished and increases, but also has the perceptive activity and
apprehension. And the perfect life in a body is seen in the rational, I mean
the human, nature, being nourished and perceiving, and sharing in word, and
administered by mind. And some such division of the account would come to be
for us: Of the things that are, some one thing is intelligible, and the other
is altogether bodily. But let the cut of the intelligible toward its own now
be passed over; for the discourse is not of these. And of the bodily, some is
altogether without a share of life, and some shares in vital activity. Again
of the vital body, some lives together with sense, and some is without a share
of sense. Then the perceptive is cut again into rational and irrational. For
this reason first after the soulless matter the lawgiver says that this
natural life was established as a kind of under-base of the form of the
ensouled, preexisting in the sprouting of the plants; then thus he brings on
the generation of those administered according to sense. And since according
to the same sequence, of those who have obtained life through flesh, the
perceptive can be upon themselves even without the intellectual nature, but
the rational would not otherwise come to be in a body, unless it were mixed
with the perceptive; for this reason last after the sprouts and the cattle man
was made, nature going forward sequentially by some path toward the perfect.
For this rational animal, man, is mixed through every form of the souls. For
he is nourished according to the natural form of the soul; and to the
increasing power the perceptive grew on, holding a middle according to its own
nature of the intellectual and of the more material substance; so much more
thick-parted than this, as it is more pure than that. Then there comes to be
toward the fine and light-formed of the perceptive nature an appropriation and
blending of the intellectual substance, so that man has the constitution in
these three; as we also learned such a thing from the Apostle, in the things
in which he said to the Ephesians, praying for them that the complete grace of
the body, and of the soul, and of the spirit be kept in the presence of the
Lord, saying the body instead of the nourishing part, and indicating the
perceptive by the soul, and the intellectual by the spirit.


Likewise also the Lord through the Gospel teaches the scribe to set before
every commandment the love toward God, that which is worked from whole heart
and soul and mind. For here too the word seems to me to interpret the same
difference, having called the more bodily condition heart, and soul the
middle, and mind the higher nature, the 148 intellectual and productive power.
Whence also the Apostle knows three differences of choice, naming the one
fleshly, which is occupied about the belly and the pleasures around it; and
the psychic, which holds a middle toward virtue and vice, standing above the
one, and not sharing purely in the other; and the spiritual, which
contemplates in itself the perfect of the way of life according to God.
Therefore he says to the Corinthians, reproaching their enjoyment-loving and
passionate, that You are fleshly, and unable to contain the more perfect
dogmas; and elsewhere making some comparison of the middle toward the perfect,
he says: "But a psychic man does not receive the things of the spirit; for it
is folly to him; but the spiritual judges all things, and himself is judged by
no one." As therefore the psychic has gone up above the fleshly, according to
the same analogy the spiritual also has stood above this. If then Scripture
says that man has come to be last after every ensouled thing, the lawgiver
