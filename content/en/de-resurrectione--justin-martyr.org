according to authority being carried, it reasonably does not even wish to be
required to give the proofs of the things said, because there are not even
others besides the truth itself, which is God. For every proof happens to be
stronger and more trustworthy than that which is proved, if indeed that which
was formerly disbelieved, before the proof came, when this was brought,
obtained faith, and appeared such as it was said to be. But nothing is
stronger than the truth nor more trustworthy, so that he who asks proof
concerning this is like the one who wishes the things that appear to the
senses to be proved by words why they appear. For of the things taken through
reason the criterion is sense; but of sense itself there is no criterion
except itself. As therefore the things hunted through reason, leading them
away to sense, we judge by this of what sort they happen to be, whether the
things said are true or also false, and we no longer judge, trusting it, so we
send up the human and worldly arguments to the truth, and by this we judge
whether they happen to be worthless or not, but the arguments of the truth we
judge by no other, trusting it. And truth is God, the Father of the universe,
who is perfect mind. The Word, having become his Son, came to us, having worn
flesh, revealing both himself and the Father, giving to us in himself the
resurrection from the dead and the eternal life after these things. And this
is Jesus Christ, our Savior and Lord; this one then is himself both faith and
proof of himself and of all things. Wherefore those who follow this one and
have known him, having the faith toward him as proof, rest upon him. But since
the adversary does not cease warring against many, and uses many and various
methods toward a plot, toward those who have believed, that he may lead these
away from the faith, and toward those still unbelieving, that they may not
believe, it seems to me necessary that we also, having been armed with the
arguments of the faith which are invulnerable, war against him on account of
the weak.

They who say the worse things say that there is no resurrection of the flesh.
For it is impossible, they say, that this which is corrupted and dissolved be
gathered into the same. And besides the impossibility they say that the
salvation of this is also inexpedient, and they abuse it bringing forward the
defects, and they declare it alone the cause of the sins, so that, if the
flesh is going to rise, they say, the defects also will rise with it. And they
weave sophisms of this sort: If the flesh rises, it will rise either complete
and having all the parts or incomplete. But that it is going to rise deficient
shows inability of the one who raises, if he was able to save some things and
not others. But if it will have all the parts and the members, it is clear
that to say these exist after the resurrection from the dead, how is it not
absurd, the Savior having said: They neither marry nor are given in marriage,
but they will be as angels in heaven? And the angels, they say, neither have
flesh nor eat nor have intercourse; so that neither will there be a fleshly
resurrection. These things then and the things like them they say, attempting
to turn aside those of the faith. And there are some who say that Jesus
himself also was present only as spiritual, no longer in flesh, but furnished
an appearance of flesh, themselves also attempting to deprive the flesh of the
promise. First we will solve the things that seem to them to appear insoluble;
then thus in order we will bring on the argument that demonstrates concerning
the flesh that it has salvation.

They say then: If the body will rise complete and will have all its members,
it is necessary that the works of the members also exist; that the womb
conceive, and the member of a man emit seed, and the rest likewise. And let
the whole argument stand upon one point. For this being shown false, their
whole argument will go away. That the members that act act these things which
appear here is clear, but that they act them of necessity according to the
beginning is not necessary. And that what is said may be clear, we will
consider thus. The energy of a womb is to conceive and of a male member to
emit seed. And just as, if these are going to act these energies, it is
nevertheless not necessary for them to act from the beginning (we see at any
rate many women not conceiving, as the barren, even having wombs), so neither
does having a womb immediately also compel conceiving. But also those not
barren from the beginning, but remaining virgins, have abolished even
intercourse; and others also from a time. And the males too we see, some
remaining virgins from the beginning, and some from a time, so that through
them the lawless marriage through desire is destroyed. But indeed we find also
certain animals barren, although having wombs, as also the mule, and the male
mules also do not beget; so that both through men and through the irrational
the intercourse is seen being abolished even before the age to come. And our
Lord Jesus the Christ was born of a virgin for no other reason than that he
might abolish generation of lawless desire and show to the ruler that even
without human intercourse the formation of man is possible to God. And having
been born and having lived the remaining citizenship of the flesh, I mean in
foods and drinks and clothing, this one through intercourse alone he did not
work, but of the desires of the flesh those that exist as necessary he
accepted, and those not necessary he did not admit. For flesh lacking food and
drink and clothing would even be destroyed, but being deprived of lawless
intercourse it suffers no evil whatever. And at the same time he also foretold
the mixing through intercourse that is going to be abolished in the age to
come, as he says: The sons of this age marry and are given in marriage, but
the sons of the age to come neither marry nor are given in marriage, but they
will be as angels in heaven. Let not those outside the faith marvel then, if
the flesh that is from now being abolished in these works he will also abolish
in the age to come.

Yes, they say. If then the flesh rises, it also rises such as it will be laid
down, so that, if it will be laid down one-eyed, it rises one-eyed, if lame,
lame, if there should be some other lack of the body, in this the man will
also rise lesser. Blinded truly as to the eyes of the heart! For did they not
see upon the earth the blind looking up again, the lame walking by his word?
Which things all the Savior did, first that what was spoken concerning him
through the prophets might be fulfilled, that The blind look up again and the
deaf hear and the rest, and further also unto faith that in the resurrection
the flesh will rise complete. For if upon the earth he healed the weaknesses
of the flesh and made the body complete, much more will he do this in the
resurrection, so that the flesh also rise unharmed and complete. The things
then supposed by them to be insoluble will be healed in this manner.

And further of those who say that the flesh does not rise, some say that it is
impossible to rise, others that it is not fitting for God to raise it because
of its cheapness and contemptibility, others that it does not even have a
promise at the beginning. First then it seems to me to go through against
those who say it is impossible for God to raise this, that they themselves are
ignorant, saying in word that they are faithful, and through their works
showing themselves unfaithful and more unbelieving than the unbelievers. For
all the nations having believed upon the idols and having been persuaded that
all things are possible to these, as even Homer, their poet, says:

The gods can do all things
And easily
(but he also added the easily, which is readily, that he might show the
greatness of the power of the gods), they appear then much more unbelieving
than these. For if the nations have believed of the idols, their gods, who
have ears and do not hear, have eyes and will not see, that they can do all
things, being demons, as the scripture says, that The gods of the nations are
demons, much more ought we, who have the excellent and true faith, to believe
our God, having proofs; and first the genesis of the first-formed, that he
came to be from earth by God; for this is a sufficient sample of the power of
God. And further also considering the genesis from one another after these
things it is possible to see, and to marvel more greatly, that from a least
drop of moisture so great a living creature is formed. And yet if this also
was in a promise and was not seen coming to be, it was much more unbelievable
than the others; but the accomplishment makes it more credible. But indeed
concerning the resurrection the Savior showed us accomplishments, which we
will speak a little later. But now we are showing that the resurrection of the
flesh is possible, asking pardon from the children of the truth, if we also
take hold of arguments that seem to be from those outside and worldly; first
because nothing is outside of God, not even the world itself; for it is his
workmanship; second because we make these arguments toward unbelievers. For if
toward the faithful, it would have been sufficient to answer that we have
believed; but now it is necessary to proceed through demonstrations. The
proofs already said are sufficient then to show that the resurrection of the
flesh is possible; but, since they are exceedingly unbelieving, we will also
bring on the argument more compellingly, not from the faith, because they do
not happen to be of it, but from unbelief, their mother, I mean from the
worldly arguments. For if from these we show them that the resurrection of the
flesh is possible, they are surely worthy of much shame, if they can follow
neither the things of the faith nor the things of the world.

The natural philosophers of the world then, called wise, say the universe
exists, some as matter and God, as Plato, others as atoms and void, as
Epicurus, others as the four, earth and water, air, fire, as the Stoics. For
it is enough to mention the opinions that prevail most. And Plato says that
all things have come to be from the matter by God and according to his
providence, but Epicurus and those with him from the atoms and from the void
according to some automatic carrying of the natural motion from the bodies,
and the Stoics from the four, God extending through them. And there being such
disagreement among them, there are certain common doctrines acknowledged among
them by all; one, that neither does anything come to be from what is not nor
is it dissolved and destroyed into what is not, and that the elements exist
incorruptible, from which the genesis of each thing is. These things then
being so, according to all of them the regeneration of the flesh will appear
to be possible. For if according to Plato there is the matter and God, both
these are incorruptible; and God occupies the place of an artisan, as of a
molder, and the matter occupies the place of clay or wax or some such thing.
That which comes to be from the matter then is a corruptible molded thing, the
statue or image, but the matter itself is incorruptible, as clay or wax or
some other such form of matter. Thus the molder from the wax or clay molds and
makes alive the form of a living creature; again, if the molded thing is
dissolved, it is not impossible for him, having kneaded the same matter again
and made it new, to make the same molded thing. Thus according to Plato it is
not impossible even for God, being incorruptible, having also the matter
incorruptible, the molded thing that came to be from it having been dissolved,
to make it new again and to make the same molded thing such as it was also
formerly. But indeed according to the Stoics, the body coming to be from the
mixture of the four elements, and, this being dissolved into the four, these
remaining incorruptible, it is possible again that the four elements, having
received the same mixing and blending from the God who extends through them,
make the body which they had formerly made; just as, if someone from gold and
silver and bronze and tin should make a mixture, then should wish again to
dissolve it, so that each be by itself, and again, if he should wish, having
mixed the same he will make the mixture which he had formerly made from them.
And according to Epicurus also, the atoms being incorruptible and the void, by
the qualitative arrangement and position of the atoms having been put together
both the other compounds and the body come to be, and being dissolved in time
it is dissolved again into the atoms, from which it also came to be. These
being incorruptible, nothing is impossible, they having come together again
and having received the same position and arrangement, to make the body which
had formerly come to be from them and like; just as, if some mosaic-setter
from pebbles should make the form of a living creature, then these having been
dissolved from time or by the maker himself, having the same pebbles,
gathering the scattered, he will not be unable having collected them and
having arranged them likewise to make the same form of the living creature.
But God, the members of the flesh having been dissolved from one another, will
he not be able again having gathered them to make the same as the body
formerly made by him? But the argument concerning the resurrection of the
flesh being possible has been sufficiently demonstrated by me according to the
gentiles. And if according to the unbelievers the resurrection of the flesh is
not found impossible, how much more according to the faithful!

Next it must be said toward those who dishonor the flesh and assert that it is
not worthy of the resurrection nor of the heavenly citizenship; that first its
substance is earth, and afterwards it has also become full of every sin, so as
even to compel the soul to sin with it. And these seem to be ignorant of the
whole business of God, and the genesis and formation of man from the
beginning, and the things in the world for the sake of which it came to be.
For does not the Word say: Let us make man according to our image and
according to likeness? What kind? He means a fleshly man, clearly. For the
Word says: And God took dust from the earth and formed the man. It is clear
then that the man being formed according to the image of God was fleshly. Then
how is it not absurd to assert that the flesh formed by God according to his
own image is dishonorable and worthy of nothing? And that the flesh is a
precious possession with God is clear first from its having been formed by
him, if at least an image also becomes precious to the molder and painter; and
from the rest of the world-making it is possible to learn; for that for the
sake of which the rest came to be, this is most precious of all to the one who
made it. Yes, they say; but the flesh is a sinner, so as even to compel the
soul to sin with it, accusing it in vain and laying the sins of both upon it
alone. For where will the flesh be able to sin by itself, if it does not have
the soul going before and calling it forth? For just as a yoke of oxen, the
oxen having been loosed from one another from the yoke, neither of them can
plow by itself, so neither can soul and body having been loosed from the
pairing do anything by themselves. And if the flesh alone is also a sinner, on
account of this alone the Savior came, as he says: I did not come to call the
righteous but sinners to repentance. Since then the flesh has been shown
precious with God and glorious beyond all the things made, it would justly be
saved by him.

It must be said then toward those who say that, even if it happens most of all
to be a workmanship of God and precious to him beyond all, it does not
immediately also have the promise of the resurrection. And yet how is it not
absurd that that which came to be with so great a counsel and is precious
beyond all the rest the maker should overlook into no longer being? Then the
molder and painter, if they wish the images, which they make, to remain, that
through them they may be glorified, renew them when they are being destroyed;
but God would have overlooked his own possession and molded thing as that
which is not, and no longer also into being? We call such a one a vain
laborer, just as if someone having built a house should then destroy it, or
should overlook it destroyed, being able to raise it; and shall we not accuse
God, that he makes in vain? But the incorruptible is not such, the mind of the
universe is not by nature mindless. Let the unbelievers keep silence, if they
themselves have the not-believing! But indeed he has also called it to the
resurrection and promises the eternal life. For where he evangelizes to save
the man, he evangelizes also to the flesh. For what is the man, but the
rational living creature composed of soul and body? Do not then

by itself is the soul a man? No; but the soul of a man. Would the body then be
called a man? No; but it is called a body of a man. If then neither of these
by itself is a man, but that from the intertwining of both is called a man,
and God has called the man to life and resurrection, he has called not the
part but the whole, which is the soul and the body. Since how is it not
absurd, both being according to the same and in the same, to save the one and
not the other? For it not being impossible, as has been shown, that the flesh
have the regeneration, what is the distinction, so that the soul is saved, but
the flesh not? Or do they make God envious? But he is good and wishes all to
be saved; and indeed not only did our soul hear of him and of his proclamation
and with it the flesh, and they believed in Christ Jesus, but both were washed
and both worked righteousness. Do they then declare God ungrateful or unjust,
if of both believing upon him he wishes to save the one, and not the other?
Yes, they say; but the soul is incorruptible, being a part of God and an
inbreathing, and on this account he wished to save his own and kindred; but
the flesh is corruptible and not from him as the soul is. Then what grace is
there to him? And what showing of his power and goodness, if he was going to
save that which is by nature saved and exists as a part of him? For it had the
salvation from itself, so that in saving the soul he does no great thing; for
to be saved is present to it, because it is a part of him, being his
inbreathing. But neither is it grace to him saving his own; for this is to
save himself. For he who saves his part through himself saves himself, lest
that part become deficient. Such a thing is not of a good man. For neither
would anyone say a man good who grants something to his children and
descendants. For this even the most savage of the beasts do; and if they must
even die for their offspring, they endure this willingly. But if someone
should furnish the same things to slaves, this one would justly be called
good. On this account the Savior also taught us to love the enemies, since
what grace is there to you? he says. So that he has shown us that it is a good
work not only to love those who have come to be from him, but also those
outside. And the things which he commanded us he himself did much earlier. ***

If he needed the flesh for nothing, why did he also heal it? And, the
strongest of all, he raised the dead. For the sake of what? Not that he might
show of what sort the resurrection is going to be? How then did he raise the
dead? Whether the souls or the bodies? But it is clear that both. And if the
resurrection were only spiritual, it was necessary that he, having risen, show
the body lying by itself, and the soul existing by itself. But now he did not
do this, but he raised the body, confirming the promise of life in it. For the
sake of what then did he rise in the flesh that suffered, if not that he might
show the fleshly resurrection? And wishing to make this credible, his
disciples not believing if he truly rose in a body, they seeing and doubting,
he said to them: Do you not yet have faith? he says. See that I am. And he
permitted them to handle him, and he showed the marks of the nails in the
hands. And having perceived him from every side, that it is he and in the
body, they asked him to eat with them, that also through this they might learn
firmly that he truly rose fleshly. And he ate honeycomb and fish. And thus
having shown them that there truly is a resurrection of flesh, wishing to show
this also (as he has said that our dwelling exists in heaven) that it is not
impossible even for flesh to go up into heaven, he was taken up into heaven as
they looked on, as he was in the flesh. Therefore, if anyone after all the
things already said demands demonstrative arguments concerning resurrection,
he differs in nothing from the Sadducees; since the resurrection of the flesh
is a power of God and above every argument, being confirmed by faith, and
being observed in works. ***

Resurrection is of the fallen flesh; for spirit does not fall. Soul is in a
body, and a soulless thing does not live; a body, the soul having left, is
not. For the body is a house of a soul, and the soul a house of spirit. And
these three will be saved for those who have a sincere hope and an undivided
faith in God. Observing at any rate also the worldly arguments, and

according to them not finding it impossible for the flesh that the
regeneration exist, and upon all these things the Savior through the whole
gospel showing the salvation of our flesh, why do we still endure the
arguments of the unbelievers and scandals, and we escape our own notice
turning back, whenever we hear that the soul is immortal, but the body is
corruptible and no longer able to live again? For these things we heard even
before learning the truth from Pythagoras and Plato. If then the Savior said
these things and evangelized the life of the soul alone, what new thing did he
bring us beyond Pythagoras and Plato and their chorus? But now he came
evangelizing the new and strange hope to men. And it was strange then and new
that God promised not to keep incorruption for the incorruption, but to make
the corruption incorruption. But the ruler of wickedness, not being able
otherwise to maltreat the word, sent out his apostles, introducing evil and
pestilent teachings, having chosen them from those who crucified our Savior,
who bore the name of the Savior, but did the works of the one who sent them,
through whom also blasphemy followed the name. And if the flesh does not rise,
why is it also guarded and why do we not rather allow it to use the desires,
and why do we not imitate the physicians, who, whenever they have a man
despaired of not able to be saved, permit him to serve the desires? For they
know that he perishes. Which thing indeed those who hate the flesh do, casting
it out of the inheritance, so far as it is upon them; for on this account they
also dishonor it as being about to be dead. But if our physician Jesus the
Christ, having drawn us away from our desires, regulates our flesh with the
temperate and continent diet according to him, it is clear that having a hope
of salvation he guards it from the sins, just as the physicians do not allow
men who have a hope of salvation to serve the pleasures.
