* On that saying, Let a widow be enrolled
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

On the saying, "Let a widow be enrolled, being not less than sixty years of
age"; and concerning the bringing up of children, and concerning almsgiving.

=51.321=

1. The grace of the Spirit has arranged in season that this passage of the
apostolic letter be read, which you heard today; for it has a certain kinship
with what was said before and much sequence, if not in the words, yet in the
thoughts. For what was read before was this: "But I would not have you
ignorant, brethren, concerning those who have fallen asleep"; and much was
said then about the resurrection, about bearing such sufferings nobly, and
giving thanks to God who takes those belonging to us. Today what was read is
this: "Let a widow be enrolled, being not less than sixty years of age." Since
then widowhood comes from death, and this especially is what stretches the
pain and wakes the mourning, remembering what was said before, which we spoke
comforting those who mourn, receive these things too with all eagerness and
lay them up with those in the treasuries of the mind. For the name of
widowhood seems to be a name of misfortune, yet it is not, but a dignity, and
honor, and greatest glory; not a reproach, but a crown.

For even if the widow has no husband dwelling with her, yet she has Christ
dwelling with her, who drives back all the evils that come upon her. For it is
enough, in the despite that comes upon the widow, to go in and bend the knee,
and groan bitterly, and pour forth tears, and drive back all the plot of those
who despite her; for such are the weapons of the widow: tears, and groanings,
and continual prayers; through these she can drive back not only human
despite, but =51.322= even demonic assaults. The widow has been released from
the affairs of this life, and she journeys toward heaven thereafter; and the
eagerness and service she showed toward her husband, this she will be able to
spend upon spiritual matters. But if you should say that of old the thing was
a misfortune, I would say this, that death too was a curse; but it has become
honor and dignity to those who bear it nobly when it comes. Thus then the
martyrs are crowned; in the same way the widow too passes over to a great
dignity.

2. Do you wish to learn how great a widow is? how she is honorable to God, and
beloved, and a greatest advocate, and appearing she snatches away and
reconciles those condemned, and those given up, and those who have no
boldness, and those who have made war against God and been stripped of all
defense, and she does not bring them pardon only, nor release from punishment,
but also much boldness and splendor, and makes them purer than the sun's rays,
even if they are more defiled than all men? Hear God himself saying to the
Jews: "When you stretch out your hands, I will turn my eyes away from you; if
you multiply your prayer, I will not hear you; for your hands are full of
blood." Yet to these polluted ones, these murderers, these who have no
boldness, these dishonored, he promises to be reconciled, if they should help
widows who are wronged. For after saying, "I will turn my eyes away, and I
will not hear," he says: "Judge for the orphan, and justify the widow; and
come, and let us reason together; and if your sins be as scarlet, I will
whiten them as snow." Did you see how much power the =51.323= widow has? where
she displays her patronage, not before a ruler and king of those on earth, but
before the very King of the heavens? how much wrath she can dissolve,
reconcile the Master to those


who have fallen incurably sick, snatch them from unbearable punishment, wash
from that stain a soul dyed with the filth of sins, and bring it to the
highest purity? Let us not then despise a widow woman, but let us show all
care concerning her. Our patroness is the true widow. And it is worth
stopping to see about what widow he speaks here. For those too are called
widows who have fallen into last poverty, and have been enrolled, and are fed
from the church's funds, as indeed in the time of the apostles. For "there
arose," he says, "a murmuring among the Hellenists, because their widows were
overlooked in the daily ministration." Not these only are called widows, but
those too who need nothing, but enjoy plenty, and preside over houses, having
lost the
husband only. Let us see then about what widow he speaks here, saying: "Let a
widow be enrolled, being not less than sixty years of age": is it about the
one who needs help and has need to be fed from church funds, or about the one
who is without want and lives in plenty? It is clear that it is about this
latter. For when he speaks about that former one, the one perishing by hunger,
he sets no time, he requires no exactness of manners; but simply, "If any man
or woman that believes," he says, "has widows, let them relieve them, and let
not the Church be charged." He did not say, "When she becomes sixty years
old"; he did not say, "If she has lodged strangers, if she has washed the feet
of saints"; and very reasonably. For where poverty must be set right, he does
not wait for time. For what if being fifty years old she should perish by
hunger? And what if in youth she should happen to be maimed in body? Is she to
sleep waiting for the sixtieth year? But this is last inhumanity. Therefore,
when hunger must be comforted, he is exact about neither times nor virtue of
soul; but when it is not to set right poverty, but to grant honor according to
worth, reasonably he makes so great an examination of manners.

3. For just as there are choirs of virgins, so of old there were also choirs
of widows, and it was not permitted them simply to be enrolled among the
widows. He does not then speak about that one living in poverty and needing
help, but about this one who has chosen widowhood. And for what reason does he
require time even in her case? He knows that youth is a certain fire, and a
sea full of waves and having many uprisings. Whenever then they are about to
have immunity from age, and as in a harbor they spend their time in old age,
their desires having been quenched, he brings them into this choir with
security. What then? are there not many, he says, who beginning even after the
twentieth year shone through to the end, and bore the yoke, and displayed an
apostolic life? =51.324= Shall we then hinder those, tell me, and if they wish
to live in widowhood force them to keep company with second marriages? And how
is this worthy of apostolic judgment? What then is what is said? Attend with
exactness, beloved, to the very sense of the word. For he did not say, "Let
her become a widow, being not less than sixty years of age," but "Let a widow
be enrolled"; and again he did not say, "Let younger widows not be enrolled,"
but "But the younger widows refuse"; for he is discoursing these things to
Timothy. For since many of men are easily taken in slander, and have sharpened
their tongues against those who preside over the Church, wishing to snatch the
ruler from the charges, he sets these laws, and says: You refuse, and you do
not enroll. If she herself wishes from her own house and of herself to choose
these things, let her do it; but do you not yet receive her, lest they say
that Being younger, wishing to marry, to preside over a house, so-and-so
compelled her; therefore she fell, and was tripped. Do you not enroll her, so
that, even if she fall, you may be released from the charges; and even if she
does not fall, you may enroll her with more security at the proper time. But
if he says, "I will that younger widows marry, bear children," hear of what
younger ones he speaks: those who after having waxed wanton against Christ
wish to marry, the tattlers, the busybodies, those going about the


houses, those speaking the things they ought not, those who have turned aside
after Satan. For he did not, having said simply, "I will that younger ones
marry," keep silence, but he says also of what sort of younger ones, and he
goes over their falls. What falls? "When they have waxed wanton," he says,
"against Christ, they wish to marry, and they learn to be idle, and
busybodies, going about the houses, speaking the things they ought not, and
they have turned aside." After whom? After Satan. Since then after choosing
widowhood, and enduring all this unseemliness, they wish to marry again, it is
better to come to this before they are held, and trample the covenants toward
Christ; so that if one should not be such, he does not impose a necessity of a
second marriage.

4. And that this is true is clear from there. For if he had set this as a law
for all women, to marry and to be mistress of a house, he was requiring those
things superfluously: "If she has brought up children, if she has washed the
feet of saints, if she has relieved the afflicted, if she has followed every
good work"; superfluously he says that too, "Having been the wife of one man."
For if you command all the younger ones to marry, how will anyone be able to
be the wife of one man? So that his word is toward those. Thus he does also
concerning intercourse according to marriage. For having said, "Defraud you
not one the other, except it be by consent for a time, that you may give
yourselves to fasting and prayer, and come together again"; lest you think the
thing to be a law, he adds the cause afterward, saying: "That Satan tempt you
not." "But this I speak by way of permission, not of commandment, because of
your incontinency." As then he does not discourse those things there to all,
but =51.325= to the more incontinent of men and easily caught; so here too to
the easily taken of women, and those not able to bear the exact life of
widowhood, to these he exhorts and advises to bring in a second bridegroom.
For widowhood is a double thing. What is this double? A display of good works,
and an excellence of greatest honor. Just as office too is a double thing; for
it has both works and dignity; the dignity of office is the authority, and the
service from the many, and the very being a ruler; but the works of office are
to help those who are wronged, to hinder those who wrong, to preside over the
cities, to spend the night in the common cares of affairs, and ten thousand
other things; so widowhood too is both a dignity and a work; it is a dignity,
the very being a widow, which is greatest, as we showed before; it is a work,
not to bring in a second husband, but to be content with the former, to bring
up children, to lodge strangers, to wash the feet of saints, to relieve the
afflicted, to follow every good work. Paul then, discoursing about them, lets
her accomplish all the works of the widow; but into the dignity of the widow,
and the choir, and the order, he does not let her enter, until the sixtieth
year has passed, all but saying: Let her do the works of the widow, but let
her be thought worthy of the honor then, when having displayed all she has
thereafter the security from time, and the proof from the works, and the
testimony from without. Let no one think this word to be fitted for women
only. For it is useful for men too, that they also may love their own even
when they have gone away, and not house lionesses with the little children,
bringing in stepmothers, and overturning all their own security.

5. And we say these things not legislating to turn away from a second
marriage, but exhorting and advising to be content with the former with
self-control. It is one thing to exhort and advise, another to legislate. For
he who exhorts and advises leaves the hearer master of the choice of the
things advised; but he who legislates declines this authority of theirs. But
the Church does not legislate these things, but only exhorts; for Paul too
permitted a second marriage, speaking thus: "A wife is bound by the law as
long as her husband lives; but if the husband be fallen asleep, she is free to
be married to whom she will, only in the Lord; but she is happier if she so
abide." As then


marriage is good, but virginity is better; so the second marriage too is good,
but the first and only is better than it. We do not then cast out second
marriage, nor legislate these things, but we exhort, if anyone should be able
to be continent, to remain upon the former. And we exhort and advise also for
the very security of the house; for often second marriage has become a
beginning and pretext of fight and of daily wars. Often then a husband sitting
at table, having remembered the former wife in the presence of the second,
wept quietly; and she at once grew savage, and like a wild beast sprang upon
him, demanding of him a penalty for his affection =51.326= toward that one;
and if he should wish to praise the one who has gone, the subject of the
praises becomes a pretext of war and fight. And toward enemies who have gone
we make a truce, and with their life we also dissolve the enmity toward them;
but in the case of wives the very contrary. For her whom she did not see, whom
she did not hear, from whom she suffered nothing terrible, this one she hates
and turns from, and not even death quenches the hatred. Who has seen, who has
heard of dust being jealous, and ash being warred against?

6. But the terrible thing is not up to this; but whether children are born
from the second, or are not born, war again and fight. For if they are not
born she is pained more greatly, and therefore she looks on those of the
former as enemies and as having done the greatest wrongs, taking through their
life a clearer sense of her own childlessness. But if they are born, again the
terrible thing is not less. For the husband, often being affectionately
disposed toward the one who has gone, clings to these, both loving and pitying
them for their orphanhood; but she wishes her own to be preferred everywhere,
and wishes these not even in the rank of brothers, but to be in that of
cast-off household slaves; all which things could overturn a house, and make
life unlivable for the one who has married. For these reasons we exhort, if
possible, to be continent, to love the former marriage, and that women not
bring in bridegrooms, nor men wives, so that the whole house not be
overturned. And for what reason, discoursing about widowhood, was he not
content with the former only, with saying, "Wife of one man"? That you may
learn that what makes a widow is not simply not marrying a second time only,
but to abound in good works, in almsgiving and love of mankind, and in the
services of strangers. For if virginity profited the virgins nothing (and yet
virginity is much greater than widowhood), but they went away, the fire of
their lamps having been quenched, dishonored, since they did not have to
display the fruit from love of mankind and almsgiving, much more the widows.
Paul then, having heard that parable, and fearing for these, makes much
exactness about the matter, lest trusting in the one marriage they neglect the
rest of virtue; therefore he says, "Well reported of for good works"; for as
virginity is good, but without the rest it became fruitless, and shut them out
of the bridechamber; so widowhood is good, but without the rest of virtue it
is vain and superfluous. Therefore Paul did not stop the exhortation at not
bringing in a second husband, but he requires of the widow also many other
things far more and greater. And just as those who enroll soldiers seek a good
condition of body, so this man too, enrolling her into the camp of Christ,
sought a good condition of soul and vigor and eagerness in all good works,
speaking thus: "If she has brought up children, if she has lodged strangers,
if she has washed the feet of saints, if she has relieved the afflicted,
=51.327= if she has followed every good work." For each of these seems to be a
bare word, but it contains in itself much life.

7. And if you please, first let us examine what he himself set first: "If she
has brought up children." For by nurture here he does not mean this bare thing
and what is thought among the many, not to overlook the children perishing by
hunger; for not even the necessity of nature itself lets this ever be
neglected; whence there is not even


need of commands and laws about this, that widows feed their offspring; but
the care of righteousness, the bringing up with piety, he means here; so that
those who do not so feed them are child-killers rather than mothers. This I
say not to women only, but also to men. For many of the fathers often do
everything and busy themselves how a horse may become fine for the child, and
how a splendid house, and how a costly field; but how a fine soul and a pious
choice may become his, they have no account. And this is what overturns the
whole world, that we neglect our own children, and take care of their
possessions, but despise their soul, enduring a thing of last folly. For the
possessions, even if they are many and costly, if the one able to manage them
with virtue is not earnest, will all perish and go away with him, and would
bring last harm to the possessor; but if the soul becomes noble and
philosophic, even if nothing is stored within, it will be able to hold the
things of all with security. We must then look not how we may make them rich
in silver and gold and such things, but how in piety and philosophy and the
acquisition of virtue they may become more well-supplied than all; how they
may not need many things, how they may not be fluttered about the things of
this life and youthful desires. And we must examine with exactness their
goings in and their goings out, their pastimes, their companies, knowing that
if these are neglected we will have no pardon with God. For if we are required
to give account of the care of others ("Let no one seek his own," he says,
"but the neighbor's"), how much more of that of the children? "Did I not
settle him with you," he says, "from the beginning? Did I not set you over him
as teacher and patron and guardian and ruler? Did I not put all his authority,
bearing it, into your hands? I commanded you to mold him while he was tender,
and to train him; what pardon would you have, if you should overlook him
having leaped away?" For what would you say? that he is hard to rein and
rough? But from the beginning you ought, seeing these things beforehand, when
he was easy to rein, and quite young, to bridle him with exactness, to
accustom him to the things needed, to train him, to chastise the diseases of
his soul. When the work was easier, then you ought to have cut out the thorns,
when being of a more tender age they were more easily pulled up, and the
passions, if neglected and growing, would not have become hard to work.
Therefore he says, "Bend his neck from youth," when the training would become
easier. And he does not only command =51.328=, but he himself also takes hold
of the work with you. And how, and in what way? "He who speaks evil of father
or mother," he says, "let him die the death." Do you see how much fear he set
over them? how much agony he walled up? how he made your rule strong? What
defense then would we have to say, when he, whenever we are insulted, does not
spare even their life; but we, when God is insulted by them, do not even
endure to be angry with them? "I," he says, "do not decline even to kill the
one who insults you; but you do not even bear to grieve with a word," he says,
"the one who tramples my laws." And how are these things worthy of pardon? Do
you see him insulting the one who made him, and you are not displeased, tell
me, nor do you frighten and rebuke, and this knowing that God himself also
forbade this, not because some harm comes to the one insulted (for the Divine
is indestructible), but for that one's salvation? For he who has become
ungrateful and without feeling toward God will be able much more to outrage
the one who begot him, and his own soul.

8. Let us not then neglect, knowing that if they are well disposed according
to God, they will also be well-reputed and splendid according to the present
life. For all reverence and honor the one who lives with virtue and equity,
even if he be poorer than all, just as all turn from and hate the wicked and
twisted one, even if he has acquired much plenty. And not only to the other


men will he be reverend, but also to you who begot him more longed-for,
having, besides nature, virtue as not a lesser other ground toward being
loved; and not more longed-for only, but he will also be more useful to you,
serving, slaving, tending your old age. For just as those ungrateful toward
God also despise those who begot them; so those who serve the one who made
them hold those who begot them too in much honor. In order then that he may be
well-reputed both with God and with men, and may make your life sweet, and may
release you from the punishment to come, show all eagerness concerning him.
For that those who neglect the children, even if they are otherwise equitable
and moderate, will undergo the last penalty because of this sin, I will tell
you a certain ancient history. There was a priest among the Jews, equitable in
other things and moderate, Eli by name. This Eli then had two children who had
gone to last wickedness; and he did not restrain, nor hinder; or rather he did
restrain and hinder, but not with the fitting exactness and vehemence. For
when he ought to have scourged, to have cast them out of the father's house,
to have displayed every manner of correction, he only exhorted and advised,
speaking thus: "Do not, children, do not so; for the report is not good which
I hear concerning you." What do you say? They insulted the Master, and you
call them children? They have not known the one who made them, and you
acknowledge their kinship? Therefore he says that he did not admonish them;
for admonition is, not if we simply advise, but if more vehemently and more
cut =51.329= tingly and as much rottenness as the wound requires, so great a
blow we bring on. It is not then enough to speak, nor to exhort only, but one
must also wall up much fear, so as to cut off the listlessness of youth. Since
then he exhorted, but did not exhort as he ought, he gave them over to the
enemies, and a battle having taken place, they fell in the array, and not
bearing the message, having fallen he too was crushed and died. Do you see
that I said justly, that fathers too are child-killers, who do not use their
own children vehemently when they are listless, nor require of them piety
according to God? Thus then Eli became a child-killer. For if the enemies
slaughtered his sons, yet he became the cause of the slaughter, having by his
listlessness concerning them driven away the help of God, and shown them naked
and deserted to those who wished to kill them. And not those only, but he
destroyed himself as well.

9. This then many of the fathers now also suffer; not wishing to scourge, nor
to rebuke with words, nor to grieve their own sons living disorderly and
unlawfully, they have often seen them caught in last things, snatched to a
court, beheaded by public executioners. For when you do not train, when you do
not bring to self-control, having mixed yourself with polluted and corrupted
men, and having shared the wickedness with them, they are led under the common
laws and punished with all seeing; and with the misfortune the shame becomes
greater, all pointing with the finger at the father after that one's end, and
making the marketplace impassable for him. For with what eyes will he be able
to look those who meet him in the face after such unseemliness and misfortune
of the child? Therefore I beg and entreat that much care of one's own children
be taken, and that the salvation of their soul be sought everywhere. You are
teacher of the whole house, and God continually sends you both the wife and
the sons. And now Paul says concerning the women: "And if they will learn
anything, let them ask their own husbands at home"; and now concerning the
children, "Bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord." Think
that you have golden statues in the house, the children; each day train them
and look around with exactness, and in every way adorn their soul and mold it;
imitate the blessed Job, who even for the things they erred in according to
thought


fearing, offered sacrifices for them, and made much care of them. Imitate
Abraham; for he too was not eager about money and possessions, but about the
divine laws, how he might deposit the keeping of them with the offspring with
exactness. And God testifies this virtue of his, speaking thus: "For I knew
that Abraham will command his children the judgments and the ordinances." And
David too, when he was dying, having called his own son instead of a great
inheritance, entrusted these things and said continually: that "If you should
wish, child, to live according to the laws of God, nothing unexpected will
fall upon you, but all things will come to you according to stream, and you
will enjoy much security; but if you fall away from that help, no profit to
you of the kingdom and of this much power." These things and such he said,
even if not in these words. =51.330=

10. These things let us too, both living and about to die, discourse toward
our own children, and persuade them that a great wealth, and an inheritance
that does not fall, and a treasure not to be despitefully used, is the fear of
God; and let us be eager not to leave them money that perishes, but piety that
remains and is not spent. For if piety is not, even the money that is perishes
with dangers and last shame; but if this is present, even the things that are
not are added. If you bring up the child well, so will he also his own son,
and this one the son; and like a certain chain and sequence of a best way of
life it will go even to the end, having taken from you the beginning and the
root, and bringing you the fruits of the care of the grandchildren. If fathers
trained their own children with exactness, there would be no need of laws, nor
of courts, nor of punishments and chastisements and the public killings. "For
a law," he says, "is not laid down for a righteous man." But since we neglect
them, therefore we surround them with greater evils, and give them over to the
hands of executioners, and continually push them into the pits; "For he who
chills his own son," he says, "will bind up his wounds." What is "He who
chills"? He who pities, he who flatters, he who serves beyond measure. For
this one needs vehemence and care and fears. I say these things not that we
may be too rough with the children, but that we may not appear easily despised
toward them. For if a wife ought to fear the husband, much more the child the
father. And do not tell me that it is impossible to get the better of youth.
For if Paul requires this care of a widow woman, much more of the men; if it
were impossible, he would not have commanded it. But all the wickedness comes
from our listlessness, and from not leading them from the beginning, nor from
first age, into piety. But how they may share in the education from without,
and may be enrolled into military service, we are eager, and we put down
money, and we ask friends, and we use many runnings about; but how they may be
well-reputed with the King of the angels, we make no account. And we
continually allow them to go up to spectacles, but we never force them to
church; but even if the child comes once or twice, he comes here simply and at
random and in vain and for amusement. But it ought not to be so; but as when
sending to a school we require the accounts of the lessons, so also sending to
church, or rather leading. For we ought not to entrust them to others, but
holding them ourselves to go in here, and we ought to require the memory of
the hearing and teaching here. For thus, thus the correction of the children
would become easier and ready for us; for if also in the house they heard you
continually discoursing about philosophy, and advising them the things needed,
and the things here were added to them with those, they would quickly display
to us a noble fruit of these good seeds. But we do none of these things, but
the necessary things are by-work to us; and if someone exhorts =51.331= about
these, at once laughter;


and because of this the things above have become below, and those whom the
parents do not train, the laws from without train.

11. Are you not ashamed and do you not blush, tell me, when the judge punishes
your son and makes him more self-controlled, and that one needs the correction
from without, having lived with you so long a time from the beginning? Do you
not hide your face and sink down? And do you dare at all, tell me, still to be
called father, having thus betrayed the son, and not having brought in to him
the necessary tribute, but having overlooked him corrupted by every vice? And
if you see some runaway slapping the child, you are angry and rage and are
displeased, having sprung upon the face of the one who struck more terribly
than a wild beast; but seeing the devil slapping him each day, demons leading
him into sins, you sleep and are not angry, nor do you snatch the son from the
most terrible beast? Again if he is worked upon by a demon, you run to all the
saints, and you trouble those on the peaks of the mountains, so as to release
him from that madness; but when sin, which is more terrible than every demon,
troubles him continually, you do nothing more? And to be troubled by a demon
is no terrible thing; for the demon cannot throw into Gehenna at all, but if
we are sober, this trial too will bring us splendid and conspicuous crowns,
when we bear such despite thankfully; but it is impossible that the one living
with sin ever be saved, but there is every necessity both to be reproached
here, and having gone away there to be punished again with immortal things.
Yet knowing these things, for the lesser we make much eagerness, but for the
greater we do not even wish to rise; and seeing one possessed we lament, but
seeing one sinning we do not even perceive; when we ought then to be cut down
and to wail, or rather not only to wail, but also to hold and bridle, to
advise, to exhort, to frighten, to rebuke, in every manner of healing to drive
out that sickness, and to imitate this widow, about whom Paul says: "If she
has brought up children." For he stretches this word not toward her only, but
also toward all, and he exhorts all saying, "Bring up the children in the
admonition of the Lord." For this is first and greatest of the good things;
which then he also required first of the widow; then after this he says, "If
she has lodged strangers." What do you say, tell me? Do you require of a widow
woman hospitality? is it not enough to have brought up children? No, he says;
but this too must be present, and after the patronage of one's own, one must
have care also of those belonging to others, and open the house to the
strangers. The husband has gone away; spend all the eagerness concerning him
upon the strangers. What then, he says, if she should be poor?

She is not poorer than that widow who in a little meal and a cup of oil
received the great prophet Elijah. For children too were present there; but
neither the scarcity of what there was, nor the tyranny of the famine, nor the
expected death, nor the care of the children, =51.332= nor the widowhood, nor
anything else became a hindrance to the hospitable woman.

12. Thus everywhere not measures of substance, but measures of mind are
sought. The magnanimous and rich in mind, even if he be poorer in money than
all men, will be able to surpass all both in hospitality and in almsgiving,
and in all the rest of kindness; the stingy and poor in mind, and creeping on
the ground, even if he be more well-supplied than all, is poorer and more
resourceless than all; therefore he shrinks and draws back from all such
things. And just as in the case of the poor man poverty would be no hindrance
toward almsgiving because of the wealth of mind; so in the case of the one who
is rich the plenty will be able to help nothing toward the kindness because of
the poverty of mind. And the examples are near; for the widow even in a little
meal received the prophet; but Ahab, having acquired so much wealth, desired
even the things of others. Thus it is not wealth of money, but wealth of mind
that furnishes us ease concerning almsgiving; since that widow too by two
obols only ten thousand rich men


outstripped, and poverty did not become a hindrance. This poverty then worked
the greater almsgiving; which then Paul also says, "The deep poverty abounded
unto the wealth of their simplicity." For we must not look at this, that she
put down two obols, but that having only these, she did not spare, but brought
in the whole substance, one must wonder at her and crown her. We need then not
surplus, but eagerness, when we receive strangers. For just as when this is
present no harm would come from poverty, so when it is absent no profit would
come from plenty. What do you say? The widow takes care of children and
therefore would not be able to serve strangers? For this very reason then she
will work this more easily, having the sons as partners of the service, taking
hold with her and joining in this good business. So that the multitude of the
children will be not a hindrance but a help of hospitality, and the many hands
will furnish much ease to the ministration. For do not tell me of a costly
table; if she receives the stranger into the house, if she sets forth what
there is, if she displays much kindness, the whole fruit of hospitality has
been completed. For if a cup of cold water only procures the kingdom of
heaven, to make one also under the same roof, and a partner of the table, and
to give rest, how much fruit will it bring, tell me? Consider then with me
Paul's exactness. For he does not simply require hospitality here, but that
with eagerness and a boiling soul and a warm mind. For having said, "If she
has lodged strangers," he added, "If she has washed the feet of saints." She
must not, sitting with pride, entrust the service of the stranger to
maidservants, but become a worker herself, and snatch the fruit, and yield to
no one this good treasure. And how could this happen, he says? for if she
should be well-born and conspicuous and splendid and notable from ancestors,
will she herself wash the stranger's feet? and how is it not =51.333=
shameful? Shameful then is not to wash, O man. For even if you lift up her
good birth ten thousand times and the conspicuousness, and the splendor, she
shares the same nature with the one being washed, and is a fellow-slave of the
one being served and of equal honor.

13. Think who washed the feet of the disciples, and stop discoursing to me
about good birth. The common Master of the world, the King of the angels, both
washed and girded himself with a towel, and not of the disciples only, but
even of the traitor himself. Did you see how great the interval between the
one washing and those being washed? Yet he came down this whole interval, and
the Master washed the slave, so that the slave-woman may not be ashamed of the
fellow-slave. Therefore also of the traitor, lest you be about to say that the
one about to enjoy the service is cheap and easily despised. For even if cheap
and easily despised, yet he is not yet according to Judas, nor has he treated
you as that one treated the Master, having come to betrayal after the ten
thousand benefits. Yet knowing all these things beforehand he washed, setting
laws for us, so that even if we are higher than all, even if more splendid and
more conspicuous than all, even if those about to lodge with us are worse than
all, we may not because of this flee their service, nor be ashamed of the
cheapness. But you, O woman, if you see someone helping you in the affairs of
this life, or cooperating in a court, or in some other such thing, you both
meet him, and receive him with much goodwill, and kiss hands, and put down
silver, and do the things of the maidservants; but if you see Christ having
come in, you shrink and sink down toward his service? If you do not receive
the stranger as Christ, do not receive him; but if you receive him as Christ,
do not be ashamed to wash the feet of Christ. Do you not see how many of those
despitefully used have fled to the feet of statues? And yet the material is
without feeling, and the bronze without soul; yet because they are images of
kings, they hoped to reap some benefit from those feet. But you, seeing not
feet without feeling, nor material without soul, but an image having the king
within toward you


coming in, do you not run up, tell me, and hold the feet, and serve in every
way? And how are these things worthy of pardon? and of how much shame would
this not be? Think with whom you share when you are puffed up, and lifted
toward boastfulness, and ashamed of the service of the stranger. With the
devil, clearly; for pride is that one's disease. But if you run up, think whom
you imitate. Your Master, and you do the work of Christ. What shame then, or
what reproach, to share with the Master, tell me? Shame then is to be ashamed
of these things, and to think that to be a reproach which Christ did; the feet
of saints entering a house can do great things; they hallow the very floor,
they bring in a treasure of ten thousand good things, they set right a nature
that has been maimed, they loose famine, they bring in much plenty. Thus the
feet of Elijah too having come in =51.334= to the house of the widow displayed
a certain new and paradoxical manner of plenty. He made the house of the widow
a field, and the jar a threshing-floor. A certain new manner of sowing and
harvest came to be then; she sowed into the mouth of the righteous, and she
harvested what was put down with much abundance from the jar; she sowed meal,
and harvested meal; she did not need oxen, and a yoke, and a plow, and a
furrow, nor rain and air and a sickle, nor a threshing-floor and sheaves, nor
winds separating the chaff from the fruit, nor a mill grinding; but in one
turn of time she found the end of all these upon the jar; and two springs, one
of meal, the other of oil, the voice of the prophet continually sent up.

14. Such are the gifts of the saints, and they have much abundance and ease.
For the things reaped from the earth are spent, but those springs being drawn
each day were not emptied, but the fight of the inflow was equal-balanced
toward the emptying. Such things the feet of saints grant, or rather even much
more than these; and if I were not making the word long, I would have
enumerated many such gifts. But just as when honored they bring such gifts, so
when dishonored they bring on great punishment and inescapable fire. Whence is
this clear? Hear Christ himself saying to the disciples: "Into whatever city
or house you enter, inquire who in it is worthy, and there remain, and
entering say: Peace to this house." For lest you say, I spend the money, I
consume the substance, setting a table for the strangers, he first prepares
the one entering to bring you guest-gifts and presents surpassing all surplus.
What then are these? The supply of peace. For nothing is equal to this. Do you
see with how much plenty the saint enters into the house? This word is bare,
but a ground of ten thousand good things. For what could be more secure than a
house enjoying peace? And the saints pray peace for those who receive them,
not that toward one another only, but also that toward ourselves. Often then
we have war in the reasonings, and with no one troubling we are disturbed, and
wicked desires continually rise up against us. This fight too then that word
of the saints settles, and makes much calm within. For at once he spoke, and
every devilish thought and out-of-place reasoning fled from our soul; so that
you receive more than you give. "And if they receive you," he says, "let the
peace come upon them; but if they do not receive, shake off the dust of your
feet. Amen, I say to you, it will be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and
Gomorrah in that day, than for that city." Do you see how much fire the feet
of saints bring on when they are dishonored? Therefore he commands to wash
them, so that being served they may procure us much boldness with God; and at
the same time through this exhortation he also trains us to accomplish all the
works of hospitality through ourselves. Imitate Abraham, become a daughter of
that one, who having three hundred and eighteen born in the house, himself
with the wife divided the fruit of hospitality =51.335=; and he brought a
calf, and she kneaded meal. These also do you


emulate; for not the giving of money only, but also the ministering to those
in need has much reward. Therefore the apostles also set those seven about
Stephen over such a ministration. And yet those men furnished nothing from
their own house to the poor, but they managed well the things given by others;
yet they carried off a great reward, because they managed the things given by
others well and with all exactness.

15. Become then you too a good steward of your own things, so that you may
receive a double fruit, both of the giving, and of the managing well. Do not
be ashamed to serve the poor through your own hand. Christ is not ashamed to
stretch out a hand and to receive through the poor, and are you ashamed to
stretch out a hand and to give silver? And how is this not last folly? One
thing only is shame, wickedness and cruelty and inhumanity; but kindness and
almsgiving and love of mankind and serving those in need make us more
splendid. For as much as you are rich and well-supplied, so much will all
praise you, when you come down toward the poor and cheap, not men only, but
also angels, and the Master of the angels; and he will not only praise, but
also repay with double gifts. For not of the almsgiving only, but also of the
humility he will prepare you many rewards. Let us not then be ashamed of the
services of the poor, nor decline to wash the feet of the strangers; for our
hands are hallowed through such ministration; and if you stretch them up to
prayer from that service, God seeing them is more prevailed upon, and he gives
the request. For to give money would be of many; but to serve those in need
through ourselves and to do this with eagerness and love and brotherly
affection needs a great and large soul and a philosophic one. And this is what
Paul seeks most of all, commanding us so to suffer with those in affliction
and poverty and circumstances, as being in the same terrible things. "For the
bound," he says, "as bound with them." Wherefore he did not stop the word here
only, but he also added another: "If she has relieved the afflicted, if she
has followed every good work." What is: "If she has followed every good work"?
So as also to go into a prison, and to visit the bound, and to look in on
those who are sick, and to comfort the afflicted, and to exhort those in pain,
and in every manner to bring in all the things according to power, and to
decline nothing at all of the things that come to be for the salvation and
rest of our brothers. But if he requires of a widow woman so many
achievements, of what defense would we men be worthy who do not do these
things, which Paul legislated widow women to do? But perhaps someone might
say: And how does he require of a widow woman so much exactness, but when he
wrote about virginity, he discoursed nothing such? He required of them a
philosophy more than these. For when he says: "The woman and the virgin is
divided"; and: "The unmarried cares =51.336= for the things of the Lord, how
she may please the Lord"; and again: "And this I say for the seemly and for
sitting close to the Lord without distraction"; he hints nothing else through
these words but that the virgin, having once for all broken herself off from
all the affairs of this life, must dedicate the whole soul to God and have
nothing in common with the earth, nor be at leisure now for these, now for
those, but having altogether bid these farewell, spend the whole leisure upon
spiritual matters. And the parable of the ten virgins also made this very
thing clear to us. Therefore then they were shut out of the bridechamber,
because they did not have oil; and oil is nothing else but love of mankind and
almsgiving and kindness and patronage of those who are wronged and comfort of
those in pain; which not having, those went away, and fell out of the
bridechamber.

6. Knowing all these things then, both women and men, and virgins, and
married, and widows, much of almsgiving


let us make eagerness; and let us not say that so-and-so is wicked and not
worthy to be well treated, so-and-so is cheap, so-and-so is cast off. Do not
look toward the worth of the one who needs the service, but toward the need
only. For even if he is cheap and cast off, even if easily despised, Christ
reckons the reward to you thus, as himself having been well treated through
that one. For lest we look toward the worth of those benefited, hear what he
says: "You saw me hungry, and you fed me." Then when those said: "When did we
see you hungry, and feed you?" he added saying: "Inasmuch as you did it to one
of these little ones, you did it to me"; so that no pretext has been left us.
For lest we say, "Where now to find one according to Elijah? and where
according to Elisha? and, Give me such men, and I will receive them with all
eagerness, and I will not decline to wash the feet and to serve in every way";
lest we say these things, what is much greater, the Master himself of Elijah
and of Elisha and of all the prophets promised to come in toward us through
the poor, saying: "Inasmuch as you did it to one of these little ones, you did
it to me." But do not run past what was said. For "You saw me hungry and you
fed me" sets four necessities of almsgiving: the trustworthiness of the one
asking, that it is the Master who asks; the necessity of the need, that he
hungers; the ease of the giving, that he seeks to be fed and asks bread only,
not luxury; the greatness of the gift, that he promises a kingdom instead of
these small things. Are you inhuman and cruel and without mercy? Revere, he
says, the dignity of the one asking. But the dignity does not prevail upon
you? Be bent toward the misfortune. But neither do the things of the
misfortune bend you to pity? Give because of the ease of the request. Neither
the dignity, nor the necessity of the need, nor the ease of the giving can
persuade you? Then because of the greatness of the good things promised
furnish =51.337= to the one in need. Do you see four causes able to raise even
the stone itself, and the stingy, and the puffed up, and the merciless,
and the most sluggish of all men? What pardon then will there be for those who
after so great an exhortation and advice overlook those in need? Let me say
then also another thing besides these; let those who have been initiated hear.
He himself, when he must feed you, does not spare even his own flesh; when he
must give you to drink, he does not spare even the blood, nor does he grudge;
and you do not even share bread, nor a cup? And what pardon will you have,
receiving such things, and so honorable, and sparing the cheap? See that
often sparing to give to Christ for gain, you give to the devil for harm. For
when we do not give to the poor, we give to slanderers; thieves often, or even
wicked household servants taking them have gone away, or even other
circumstances of affairs. And when we escape all these too, death having come
led him away
naked. In order then that these things may not happen, let us give beforehand
to Christ who asks, and let us lay it up into the inviolable treasure, so that
we may be confident both about the keeping and about the increase. For he does
not only keep what he received with exactness; but with more =51.338= addition
he gives them back to you again. Let us not then think that our substance is
lessened to us, when we furnish almsgiving. For it is not lessened, but
increased; it is not spent, but it abounds, and what happens is a certain
business and a sowing; or rather more profitable and more secure than both of
these. For commerce is subject both to winds and to waves of the sea and to
many shipwrecks, and the seeds both to droughts and to floods and to other
irregularities of airs; but the money put down into the hand of Christ is
above every plot. No one can snatch from the hand of the one who received the
things once given; but they remain working for us much and unspeakable fruit,
and bringing a rich harvest in season. "For he who sows sparingly," he
says, "will also reap sparingly; and he who sows upon blessings, will also
reap upon blessings." Let us sow then with abundance, so that we may also


thus reap, and enjoy the eternal life; which may it be that we all obtain, by
the grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the
Father together with the Holy Spirit be glory, might, honor, now and always,
and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
