BY THE SAME ON THE LIFE OF SAINT GREGORY THE WONDERWORKER.

The aim of both our discourse and of this present gathering of ours is one.
For Gregory the Great is set before you as the subject of the assembly, and
before me as the subject of the address. And I think that the same power is
needed both to accomplish virtue in deed and to narrate the noble things
worthily in speech. So that this alliance must be invoked, through which he
accomplished virtue in his life. And this, according to my account, is the
grace of the Spirit, which through itself strengthens those who are eager
about each of these, both toward life and toward speech. Since then that
illustrious and notable life was accomplished by the power of the Spirit, it
is a work of prayer that so great a help should come upon the discourse as
came upon his life, so that the praise may not be found falling short of the
worth of the achievements, but that the man may be shown to those present
through memory such as he himself was, seen in his deeds by those of that
time. If then the memory of those who excel in virtue were profitless, and no
contribution toward the good accrued to the hearers, it would perhaps be
superfluous and useless to bring the discourse forward into eulogy for no
useful end, both going through it in vain and wearing out the hearing idly.
But since such a grace of discourse, if it should come about fittingly, will
be a common gain of the hearers, just as the beacon for those sailing in from
the sea, directing to itself those who wander in the gloom over the deep: I
think equal zeal is needed for this from both, from you toward the hearing,
and from me toward the discourse. For it is clear that his life according to
virtue, shining out through memory upon our souls like a beacon, becomes a way
toward the good both for the one who goes through it and for those who hear.
For we human beings are somehow by nature willing to be at home with
everything praiseworthy and honorable, and to come into desire of its
possession. Such then is the subject of the discourses. And for me it is in
every way without danger to dare the subject, whether the discourse should be
able to rise with the greatness of the facts =46.896= or not. For the praise
will be equal to the one being praised in either case. For if the discourse
should reach the wonders, it will altogether astound the hearing with the
achievements; but if it should come falling short of the greatness, even so
the glory of the one being praised is made bright. For the best eulogy of a
man is that he be shown better than the power of those who eulogize him. And
let none of those educated in the divine wisdom seek, according to the custom
of those outside, that the one praised spiritually be praised by the technical
approaches of encomia. For the judgment of the noble is not from the same
things for us and for the others; nor would one find the same opinions about
the same things among those who live according to the world and among those
who have risen above the world. For to the former wealth and birth and glory
and worldly powers seem something great and worthy of zeal, and myths that
settle homelands, and the tales that those who have sense would flee, trophies
and battles and the evils from wars. But by our account one homeland is
honored, paradise, the first hearth of the race of human beings; one city, the
heavenly, settled through living stones, whose craftsman and maker is God; one
nobility of race, the kinship toward God; not coming to anyone automatically,
according to the good birth in the world, which often even upon the base flows
and is carried through this automatic succession, but which it is not possible
otherwise than from


choice to acquire. For as many as received him, says the divine voice, he gave
them authority to become children of God. And what could be more august than
such nobility? And ancestral things for all the others are myths and fictions,
and deceits of demons mixed in with the mythical tales; but our ancestral
things have no need of narrators. For if someone looks into heaven, and the
beauties in it, and considers with the eye of the soul the whole creation, as
many wonders as he is able to grasp in these, these he will find to be the
tales of our homeland; or rather not of the homeland itself, but of the
colony, which, having been colonized away from the higher life, we have
occupied as the present world. And if the colony is such, one must reckon what
the metropolis of the colony is, what the beauty in that, what the palaces in
it, what the blessedness of those who have received that dwelling. For if the
things that appear according to creation are such as to be beyond praise, what
must one reckon that which is beyond this, which it is possible neither to
take with the eye, nor to contain with the hearing, nor to guess with thought?
Through this spiritual eulogy the divine praise of encomia separates those
being praised from the nonsense below, judging it shameful that those known
from such things should be made august =46.897= through the honors according
to the earth. For let some worldly man, looking toward material happiness,
gather praise for men from such things, if a homeland has become fertile in
cattle for someone, if the neighboring sea through itself multiplies materials
for the gluttonous, if successive settings of stones beautify the buildings.
But he who looks toward the life above, for whom beauty is the purity of the
soul, and wealth is possessionlessness, and homeland is virtue, and city is
the very palaces of God, will make ambition in earthly things a reproach.
Therefore we too, standing off from such praises, will neither add the
homeland to the praises of Gregory the Great, nor take ancestors as allies of
the encomia, knowing that no praise is true which is not proper to those being
praised. And we call proper that which remains altogether inalienable into the
continuous. Since then, being separated from all things, wealth, distinction,
glory, honor, luxury, enjoyment, kinsmen, friends, we remain inseparable only
from the disposition according to vice or virtue, we judge only the virtuous
man blessed. And let no one think that, because I have nothing august to
narrate about the homeland or the ancestors of the man, I am stealing away the
reproach in the figure of seeming to overlook such things. For who does not
know the name of the sea that has been assigned by all human beings in an
exceptional way to the nation, through which the virtue of those who from the
beginning obtained the place is witnessed? For this sea alone out of all earth
and sea is named Euxine, whether the name witnesses to them the kindness
toward strangers who sojourn, or also because the place is such as to grant
the provisions for living abundantly not only to the natives and autochthons,
but also to those who come to it from everywhere. For such is the nature of
the country, as to be all-bearing of all things necessary for life, and to
lack none of the goods among others, since even the sea makes the things from
everywhere their own. And while the whole nation is such that whatever part of
it one examines by itself, one would think it takes first place over the
others, no less by the common judgment of the nation, as some summit of the
whole surrounding country over the others, is the city of Gregory the Great,
which a certain notable king of those who were strengthening the rule for the
Romans, Caesar by name, captured by love and longing for the country, thought
it right that the city be called Neocaesarea upon his own name. But these
things are nothing toward our aim, so as to think to show that one great among
the saints more august through these, if the country is heavy with fruits, if
the city is beautified with buildings,


if by the neighboring sea the things from everywhere are brought in at will
toward what seems good. =46.900= But neither shall I make memory in the
discourse of his ancestors, those who led the way of the generation according
to the flesh, narrating their wealth and ambitions and worldly distinctions.
For what would these contribute toward praise, tombs and stelae and
inscriptions, and the dead tales, toward the one who has lifted himself above
the whole world? And otherwise it is not even possible to bring those into
communion of the eulogy, whose kinship he denied for the kinship according to
the soul. For they were made vain in the error of the idols; but he, looking
up toward the truth, adopted himself through faith into the kinship above. But
how, or from whom he came to be, or what city he first inhabited, as
contributing nothing to us toward the present zeal, we leave aside; and we
shall take this beginning of the eulogy, which he made of the life according
to virtue. For when in a quite young body he was deserted of natural care, his
parents having ended their life, at the time when the mind, being incomplete
by age, misses the judgment of the good among most, he showed at once from the
first what he would become in the completeness of age. And just as the
well-born of the shoots, when from first growth they run up into
straight-grown suckers, show beforehand to the husbandmen through the present
season the later beauty: in the same way he too, when for others the soul is
easily tripped by ignorance, youth as a rule readily sliding down toward the
vain and unprofitable, then through the first choice of life showed in himself
that David speaks truly, that The righteous shall flourish like a palm tree.
For this alone of trees grows up from the earth with the thickness of the
crown already complete; and as the growth according to length increases, it
receives no addition upon the breadth from time. So he too blossomed from the
first shoot, at once rising complete and high-crowned by the choice of life.
For standing off from all the things about which the young of age is
fluttered, horsemanship, hunts, adornments, garments, dice, luxury, at once he
was wholly of the acquisition of the virtues, choosing in sequence always what
was suitable to the age present to him. And the first of the acquisition of
the virtues becomes for him the zeal about wisdom. And to this there followed,
as some foal yoked with it, temperance; and toward both self-control was an
ally; and the unpuffed and unangered in him was accomplished together with the
contempt of money. For there is no other genesis of puffing and arrogance,
unless greediness introduces such passions together with itself. And just as
the account holds the patriarch =46.901= Abraham, having come into knowledge
of Chaldean philosophy, and having considered the harmonious and ordered
position and motion of the stars, used the knowledge about these as a step
toward the contemplation of the overlying good, reckoning that if the things
graspable by sense are such, what would the things above sense be? and so
obtained what was sought, as it were stepping upon the outside wisdom, and
becoming higher through it, so as to approach in a way through it the
ungraspable: so also this Great one, having associated through care with the
outside philosophy, through the things by which Hellenism is confirmed for the
many, through these was guided toward the understanding of Christianity, and
leaving the wandering religion of the fathers, sought the truth of the things
that are, taught from the very labors of those outside the inconsistency of
the Hellenic doctrines. For when he saw the Hellenic and likewise the
barbarian philosophy split toward different opinions in the views about the
Divine, and those who stood at the head of the doctrines neither agreeing with
one another, and each striving to strengthen his own by the ingenuity of
words: these he left, as in a civil war, being overturned by one another; and
he takes hold of the standing word of the faith, which is


strengthened by no logical curiosity and technical weavings, but is announced
equally to all through simplicity of words; which in the very fact of being
above faith has the trustworthy. For if what is said were such as to be
grasped by the power of human reasonings, it would have differed nothing from
Hellenic wisdom (for they too, whatever they are able to grasp, that they also
opine to be), but since the grasp of the overlying nature is unapproachable to
human reasonings, for this reason faith comes instead of the reasonings,
stretching itself out to the things above word and grasp. For these reasons,
just as Scripture says about Moses, that he was educated in all wisdom of the
Egyptians: so also this Great one, having gone through all the education of
the Greeks, and having known by experience the weakness and inconsistency of
the doctrines among them, is established a disciple of the Gospel; and before
being brought through the mystical and bodiless birth, he so accomplished the
life that he brought no stain of sin into the bath. And his way of life being
in Egypt at the great city of Alexander, into which the youth from everywhere
of those eager about philosophy and medicine also flowed together, it was a
hard spectacle for his age-mates, a young man adorned with temperance beyond
his age. For the praise of the one who was kept clean was a reproach of those
being defiled. So that there might be some defense for the unrestrained, that
they might not seem to be such alone, a certain device from a plot was
prepared by them, to rub some blame upon the life of the Great one. And there
is put forward by them, for his slander, a little woman of the hetaera sort,
condemned from a house to =46.904= dishonor. And while he, according to
custom, was examining and training together with the select of the men in a
solemn figure about some of the things according to philosophy, the little
woman approaches, affecting coyness and being delicate, and through all things
fashioning that she was in intimacy with him, through what she both uttered
and did; then she said she was being deprived of her wages, adding also from
shamelessness the occasions for which she accused the deprivation of the
wages. And while those who knew his clean life were indignant and moved
together in anger against the little woman, he was neither thrown into
confusion by those who were harsh on his behalf, nor said anything such when
slandered as it is likely for one who is vexed; he did not call witnesses of
the life, he did not push away the blame with an oath, he did not convict the
vice of those who had contrived these things against him; but in a quiet and
settled voice, turning toward one of his familiars, "O you," he said, "pay her
the silver, so that she may not, by troubling further, disturb the present
zeal of the discourse." And when the one commanded this, having learned from
the hetaera how much silver she asked from him, readily counted out all, and
the plot against the temperate man had its end for the unrestrained, and the
gain was already in the hands of the dishonorable one: then from God there
comes both the testimony of the young man's temperance, and the conviction of
the age-mates' slander. For at the same time as she received the silver in her
hand, wrenched by a demonic spirit, and wailing with a beastlike roaring
beyond human voice, she falls prone in the middle of those gathered, having
become all at once a frightful and fearful spectacle to those present, her
hair both thrown about and torn by her own hands, and her eyes turned away,
and her mouth spitting out the foam. And the demon did not first cease choking
her, before that Great one called upon God and made propitiation on her
behalf. Such are the tales of the youth of the great one, preludes truly
worthy of the things lived by him after these. And yet the wonder is so great
that even if there were nothing else to say upon these, he would from


this alone have the second place of the praises among none of those who have
excelled in virtue: rich, young, living in foreign parts, having his way of
life in a populous city, in which, because the young live with pleasures at
will, the purity of the temperate was a reproach to the mindless; with no
mother busy =46.905= about the life, no father tutoring the daily living; he
lifted himself through impassibility so far toward virtue that he made the
overseer of the all a witness of the things lived, who convicted the slander
of the woman by the wicked blow. And what greater thing could one invent for a
subject of encomia? And how could one wonder at him worthily? Who, having
wrestled down nature by reason and having yoked youth to reasoning as some of
the animals under the hand, and having become stronger than all the natural
passions that were being raised, and having moved against himself the envy
that grows upon all noble things, became stronger than this too, neither
raising himself toward defense against the plot of the companions, and
benefiting the woman who had served them toward the blame, delivering her
through prayer from the passion according to the demon. Such we have known
Joseph through the history, for whom the lawlessness concerning the master's
wife was set before him at will, that very woman being mad for the season of
the young man; and no one of human beings was witness of the things dared; but
he too, looking toward the divine eye, chose to seem bad before becoming so,
rather undergoing the things of the evildoers than becoming an evildoer. But
perhaps there is something even more for this one to boast than the history;
for it is not equal for turning away from the defilement, both the pollution
of adultery, and having the blame in what seems a lesser fault. He then who,
in a matter where there was no danger from the laws, judged the pleasure from
sin itself more fearful than the punishment, has either outstripped Joseph by
the magnificence of the wonder, or at any rate will not be judged in second
place. But such is the prelude of the life. And what is the life itself? When,
having run through all education of the outside wisdom, he met with a certain
Firmilian, a Cappadocian of the well-born, of like manner according to
character, as that man showed by the later life, becoming an ornament of the
Church of the Caesareans, and he made both the aim of his mind and of his own
life plain to the friend, that he was looking toward God, and he knew that
one's zeal coincided about the same desire, leaving all the zeal about the
outside philosophy, he goes with that one to the one who at that time was
leading the philosophy of the Christians (and this was Origen, of whom there
is much account upon the writings), showing through this too not only the love
of learning and love of labor, but also the settled and moderate in character;
for being so full of such wisdom he did not think it unworthy to use another
teacher toward the divine of the lessons, and having spent with the teacher a
time commensurate with the lessons, though there were many who were calling
and holding him in the foreign land, and thinking it right that he remain
among them, making the land that bore him more honorable than all, he returns
again to the homeland, bringing the manifold wealth of wisdom and of
knowledge, which like some merchant =46.908= associating in the outside
studies with all the well-reputed he had traded. And yet to one who judges the
facts justly this too will not seem small altogether toward eulogy, to
overlook so great a supplication of a city that came about from the common,
and the zeal of all the select in it on behalf of the man's remaining, and to
receive the eagerness of the rulers sojourning among them toward this very
thing; for all of whom the aim was that that great one remain among them, as
about to be a founder of virtue and a lawgiver of life; but fleeing from every
side the materials toward puffing, knowing that the passion according to
arrogance becomes as a rule the leader of the life according to vice, he goes
over to the quiet life in the homeland as to some harbor.


And while the whole nation was looking toward the man, and all were expecting
him to publish the education in common gatherings, so that he might have as
some fruit of the long labors the good repute upon them: that great one,
knowing from where it is fitting that the true philosophy be published by
those who pursue it accurately, so that the soul might never be wounded by
some ambition (for the praise of the hearers is terrible, to puff out the
intensity of the soul with some puffing and love of glory), for this reason he
makes silence a demonstration, showing the treasure that lies within by deed,
not by words, and having separated himself from the tumults according to the
marketplace, and altogether from the way of life in the city, he was with
himself alone in some remote place, and through himself with God, making
little account of the whole world and of the things in it, not being busy
about a kingdom, not examining offices, not hearing someone going through how
any of the common things is administered; but making the care how the soul
might be perfected through virtue, he stretched the whole life by zeal toward
this, and having let the affairs in it farewell, this man is another Moses in
our times, simply having the contest toward those wonders of his. Both went
out from this turbulent and sounding-about life, Moses and Gregory, each in
his own time having gone apart upon himself, until the gain of the clean life
became manifest to both from a theophany; but a wife also followed along with
Moses according to the philosophy; while Gregory made virtue alone a housemate
for himself. The aim then having the equal in both (for the end of both men's
withdrawal from the many was to consider the divine mysteries with the clean
eye of the soul), it is possible for the one who knows well how to look toward
virtue to judge in which of these the life has more come about through
impassibility, in the one who descended toward the lawful and conceded
participation of the pleasures, or in the one who was lifted above it, and has
given no passage against himself to material attachment. And while Phaedimus
at that time was leading the Church of the Amaseans =46.909=, for whom there
was from God out of the Holy Spirit some prognostic power; and making all
zeal, how, having taken Gregory the Great under his hand, he might bring him
into rule of a Church, so that so great a good might not run by idle and
unprofitable through the life; this one, perceiving the impulse of the priest,
was contriving to escape notice, moving from one wilderness to another. And
when that great Phaedimus, letting down every trial, and using every device
and invention, was not able to bring the man to the priesthood, who was being
guarded by countless eyes so as never to be caught by the hand of the priest,
and there was a rival zeal for both: the one desiring to catch, the other to
flee the pursuer (for the one knew he would bring a holy offering to God, and
the other feared lest some care of the priesthood, laid upon the life as some
burden, become an obstacle for him toward philosophy): for this reason
Phaedimus, raised by some more divine impulse about the present zeal, paying
no attention to the interval between, by which he was separated from Gregory
(that one being a three days' road away), but looking up toward God, and
saying that both himself and that one at that hour were likewise being seen by
God, he brings the word upon Gregory instead of a hand, having consecrated to
God the one not present in body, and having allotted to him that city, which
it happened until those times was so held by the error of the idols, that
though those inhabiting both the city itself and the surrounding country were
boundless, not more than seventeen were found who had received the word of the
faith. Having thus then come under the yoke by necessity, all the lawful
things having been completed upon him after these, and having asked a short
time from the one who had proclaimed the priesthood, toward understanding of
the accuracy according to the mystery, he no longer thought, as the Apostle
says, that he ought to attend to flesh and blood, but he asked that there come
to be for him from God the


revelation of the hidden things; and he did not first take courage for the
preaching of the word, before the truth was unveiled to him through some
manifestation. For once as he was considering in the night about the word of
the faith, and stirring all kinds of reasonings (for there were indeed even
then some who were counterfeiting the pious teaching, making the truth often
doubtful even to the intelligent through the persuasiveness of the arguments):
while he was then keeping vigil over this and taking thought, there appears to
him in a dream someone in human figure, aged in form, priestly in the
arrangement of the garment, signifying much virtue by the grace about the
=46.912= face, and by the settlement of the figure. And he, struck by the
sight, both rose from the bed and asked to learn who he was, and for what he
had come. And when that one had settled the disturbance of his mind in a quiet
voice, and had said that he had appeared to him by a divine command for the
sake of the things being disputed by him, so that the truth of the pious faith
might be unveiled: he both took courage toward the word, and looked toward him
with some joy and amazement. Then when that one stretched out the hand
straight, and as it were was pointing out to him through the straightness of
the fingers what was appearing from the side, he brought his own gaze around
with the straightness of the hand, and saw another spectacle face to face with
the one seen, in a woman's figure, better than according to a human being; and
being struck again, he both inclined the face toward himself, and was at a
loss at the spectacle, not bearing the manifestation with the eyes (for indeed
the paradox of the vision was especially in this, that though it was deep
night, a light shone out together with those seen by him, as if some bright
lamp were being kindled). Since then he was not able to bear the manifestation
with the eyes, he heard through some speech of those seen by him, going
through with one another the word about what was sought; through which he was
not only taught the true knowledge of the faith, but also recognized those who
had appeared through the names, each of them calling the other through his own
address. For he is said to have heard from the one who appeared in a woman's
figure, exhorting the evangelist John to make plain to the young man the
mystery of piety; and that one said he was ready to grant this too to the
Mother of the Lord, since this was dear to her; and having thus spoken the
word, commensurate and well-circumscribed, he again withdrew from the sights.
And he at once marked that divine initiation in letters, and according to it
after these preached the word in the church, and left to those next, as some
inheritance, that God-given teaching; through which that people is initiated
until now, having remained inexperienced of all heretical vice. And the words
of the initiation are these: One God, Father of the living Word, of subsisting
wisdom and power, and of the eternal impress, perfect, begetter of the
perfect; Father of the only-begotten Son. One Lord, only from only, God from
God, impress and image of the Godhead, effective Word, wisdom encompassing the
constitution of all things, and power productive of the whole creation, true
Son of true Father, invisible of invisible, and incorruptible of
incorruptible, and immortal of immortal, and eternal of eternal. And one Holy
Spirit, having existence from God; and manifested through the Son, that is, to
human beings, perfect image of the perfect Son, life =46.913= cause of living
things, holy fountain, holiness, supplier of sanctification; in whom God the
Father is manifested, who is over all and in all; and God the Son, who is
through all: a perfect Trinity, not divided nor alienated in glory and
eternity and kingdom. Therefore there is nothing created or slavish in the
Trinity, nor anything introduced, as if it did not exist before, and later
came in. Therefore the Son never failed the Father, nor the Spirit the Son;
but the same Trinity is always unchangeable and unalterable. And whoever is
pleased to be persuaded about this, let him hear of the Church in which he
preached the word,


among whom the very engravings of that blessed hand are preserved even until
now. Do these things not rival those God-wrought tablets by the magnificence
of the grace? And I mean those tablets, in which the legislation of the divine
will was stamped. For as the account says that Moses, having come to be
outside the apparent, and having been set within the invisible inner shrines
in the soul (for this the gloom hints), both learned the divine mysteries, and
through himself led all the people in the knowledge of God: it is possible to
see the same economy also upon this great one; for whom a mountain was not
something sensible or a mound of earth, but the height of the desire of the
true doctrines; and gloom was the spectacle uncontainable for the others; and
a writing-tablet the soul; and the letters on the tablets, the voice of the
one seen; through all of which the manifestation of the mysteries came about
both for him and for those being initiated by him. Since then he became full
of some frankness and courage through that sight, just as some athlete who has
acquired from a trainer sufficient experience and power toward the contests,
taking courage strips for the stadium, and contends against the antagonists,
in the same way he too, having sufficiently anointed the soul both by his own
practice and by the alliance of the grace that appeared to him, thus takes
hold of the contests. For one must not call anything else, but contests and
prizes, all his life according to priesthood, in which through faith he
contended down all the power of the opposing one. For at once raising himself
from the remote place, and having the impulse toward the city, in which it was
necessary to constitute the Church for God; when he knew that the whole place
was held down by the deceit of the demons, and that a temple of the true God
was constructed nowhere, and that the whole city and the surrounding country
were filled with altars of shrines and with images; the whole nation having
this zeal, how the precincts of the idols and the shrines might be beautified,
and the idol-madness might remain for human beings, being strengthened by
processions and rites and the pollutions upon the altars: just as some noble
soldier, having closed with the one who holds the battle-line, through that
one turns the subject force: so also this great one begins the prowess from
the demons themselves. And what is the manner? For going through the road from
the remote place to the city, evening having overtaken him, and a rather
violent rain holding him, =46.916= he comes within a certain shrine, with
those following him. And that shrine was of the notable ones, in which a
certain manifest visitation of the demons being served came about for the
temple-keepers, some mantic oracle-singing being worked by them. And having
gone under the shrine with those with him, he at once frightened the demons by
the invocation of the name of Christ; and having purified the air polluted
with the savory steam by the type of the cross, he spent, according to his
custom, the whole night, having kept vigil in prayers and hymn-singings, so
that the place abominated both by the gore upon the altar and by the images
was transformed into a house of prayer. And the night having been spent
through for him in this manner, he again at dawn held to the journey onward.
And when the temple-keeper at dawn was bringing the customary service to the
demons, they say that the demons appearing said to him that the shrine was
impassable for them, because of the one who had remained in it; and that he,
using some purifications and sacrifices, attempted through these to house the
demons in the temple again; and when as he brought every trial his zeal was
without effect, the demons nowhere hearkening to his invocation according to
custom; then, raised in wrath and anger, the temple-keeper overtakes that
great one, threatening all the most terrible things, both to report him to the
rulers, and to use a hand against him, to indicate the daring to the king,
