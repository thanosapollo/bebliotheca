It is the custom of those who sail and of those who travel by land to make
vows of whatever each may think of. And a poet already said in jest that one
should vow gold-horned frankincense. But we, gentlemen, along the road here
and the voyage, made this vow, not unmusical nor out of tune nor apart from
the art: if we should be saved, to address the city in public. To vow a speech
equal in measure to the city was not possible, but it truly needed another vow
besides, perhaps even a greater one, to be able to raise such a speech as
would be matched to so great a bulk of a city. Still we promised to address it
as we might be able, since others too make things equal to themselves equal in
turn also to

the gods. But, gentlemen, inhabitants of the great city, if you have any share
in providence that I not falsify the vow, join in the venture, so that as we
begin the encomia first we may also say this: that at once one could meet such
men, by whom a person, even if he were unmusical before, according to
Euripides, at once becomes tuneful and clever and able to speak even of things
greater than himself. Everyone sings of the city and will sing of it, but they
make it so much less than if they were silent, inasmuch as by silence it is
possible to make it neither greater nor less than it is, and it remains intact
in knowledge, whereas speeches do the opposite of what they intend. For in
praising they do not show exactly what they admire, but as if a painter trying
to display through art a beautiful and much-looked-at body then fell short,
everyone would surely say it was better not to paint, but either to let it be
seen itself, or not to show it to them imitated for the worse. So it seems to
me also to be concerning this city. Speeches take away most of the wonders,
and they seem to me to do something like a man wishing to report the multitude
of an army, such as Xerxes', and greatly admiring it, then saying he saw ten
thousand or twenty thousand soldiers, and so much and so much cavalry, not
even stating a tiny fraction of the whole in the things he admires.

For this is the first that has put the power of speech to the proof as not
reaching to everything. About it it is not only impossible to speak according
to its worth, but not even to see it worthily; truly one needs some all-seeing
Argus, or rather the all-seeing god who holds it. For who, seeing so many
peaks occupied, or pasture-lands of plains turned into city, or so much earth
gathered into the name of one city, then

could survey it exactly, from what lookout of that kind? For what Homer said of
the snow, that poured out it covers the peaks of high mountains and the extreme
headlands and the lotus-bearing plains and the rich works of men, and, he
says, it is poured even upon the gray sea, on harbors and shores: this then is
this city too. It covers the extreme headlands, it covers the land in the
middle, and it goes down even as far as the sea, where the common market of
mankind is and the common administration of what grows on earth. And wherever
one might be in it, nothing prevents one from being equally in the middle. And
indeed it is not poured on the surface, but simply far beyond the example it
comes up into the air to the greatest height, so that one should not liken the
height to a covering of snow, but rather to the headlands themselves. And as a
man who far surpasses the others in size and strength is not content unless he
also takes up others upon himself and carries them, so this city too, settled
upon so much land, is not content, but having taken up other cities equal in
measure upon herself she carries them, one upon another. So then the name is
namesake to her, and what is here is nothing else than strength. So that if
someone wished to unfold her purely and, setting the now-elevated cities upon
the ground, to place them one beside another, as much of Italy as is now
vacant would, I think, all be filled, and there would become one continuous
city stretching to the Ionian. Being so great, as great as I perhaps have not
even now sufficiently indicated, and eyes bear better witness, one cannot say
of her as of the others that she stands here, nor what someone said of the
city of the Athenians and of the Lacedaemonians, saying of the one that the
size was double compared with the power, of the other that the size would
appear much less than the power (far be the blasphemy of the example): this
one might say also of this city that is great in all things, that she has not
furnished a power corresponding to so great a

size. But it is possible, looking at the whole empire, no longer to wonder at
the city, thinking it rules a tiny fraction of the whole earth, and looking at
the city itself and the city's bounds, no longer to wonder that the whole
inhabited world is ruled by so great a one. For what one of the historians
said of Asia, saying that as far as the sun travels, of all this one man rules
(not speaking the truth, unless he made all Libya and Europe an exception from
the sun's settings and risings): this has now prevailed as true, that the
equal course of the sun is your possession and that the sun travels through
yours. For sea-rocks do not bound your empire, nor the Chelidoneae and
Cyaneae, nor a horse's daily run to the sea, nor do you reign upon fixed
bounds, nor does another proclaim up to where one must rule, but the sea is
stretched like a girdle in the middle of the inhabited world and equally of
your hegemony. Around it the continents, great, lie greatly, always filling
something for you from there. And from every land and sea is brought whatever
the seasons grow and each country bears, and rivers and lakes and the crafts
of Greeks and barbarians. So that if someone wished to see all these things,
he must either go over the whole inhabited world and so behold them, or come
to be in this city. For whatever grows and is made among each people, it is
not the case that it is not always here and in surplus. And so many merchant
ships arrive here bringing from all, through every season and every turn of
autumn, that the city resembles a common workshop of the earth. Cargoes from
the Indians, and if you like from the blessed Arabians too, one may see in
such quantity as to guess that the trees have been left bare to those there
for the rest, and that those people must come here, if they need anything, to
beg back their own. And garments

again Babylonian, and the adornments from the barbarian beyond, arriving much
more numerous and more easily than if from Naxos or Cythnus one had to put in
at Athens carrying something from there. Your farmlands are Egypt, Sicily, as
much of Libya as is cultivated. Arrivals and departures never fail. So that
one may wonder not only about the harbor but even about the sea, that it
suffices for merchant ships. Simply what Hesiod said about the limits of
Ocean, that there is a place where all things have been bored through into one
beginning and end: into her they have been bored through. And everything meets
here: trades, sailings, farmings, the refining of metals, crafts as many as
are and have been, everything that is born and grows. Whatever one does not
see here is not among things that have come to be or are coming to be. So that
it is not easy to decide whether the city surpasses more among the cities that
exist, or the empire the empires that have ever come to be. I blush, then, if
after so many and so great things have been said, I shall appear, the speech
not having them, to mention some barbarian empire or Greek power, and seem to
do the opposite of the Aeolian poets. For they, whenever they wish to belittle
something of their own time, compared it with something great and with an
ancient that was famous, thinking they would thus most expose it; but I, not
being able to display your things otherwise in how far they surpass, will
compare them with small ancient things. For you have shown even the greatest
things smallest by your excesses. Since I will choose the greatest and speak,
and you will perhaps laugh at them.

Let us then examine the empire of the Persians, which was once much cried up
among the Greeks, and furnished the one who held it to be called Great King as
an epithet (for I will leave aside those before it as being meaner); and let
us see everything in order, both the size and what happened under it. For we
must examine this too together, how they themselves enjoyed what they
possessed and how they arranged those they ruled. First then, what the
Atlantic sea now is for you, that simply was then sea for the King. There his
empire was bounded, so that Ionians and Aeolians were at the ends of his
earth. And having once attempted to cross into Greece, the king from the
rising sun to the setting was admired as much as he greatly failed; and he
made this a display of his own splendor, that he was able to be deprived of
many and great things. He who fell so far short of mastering Greece, and had
Ionia at the edges, has surely not been left behind your empire by a
discus-cast, or as far as a bowshot reaches, but by the whole half of the
inhabited world, and at the sea itself besides. Nor even up to these bounds
was that man always king in the proper sense, but according as the Athenians
had power or the Lacedaemonians fortune: now king as far as Ionians and
Aeolians and the sea, again no longer of the Ionians, nor as far as the sea,
but as far as the Lydians, not seeing the sea from the Cyaneae toward the
west, simply being king as in a children's game, staying up, coming down
again, needing those who would allow him to be king. And the force with
Agesilaus showed it, and before him that of the Ten Thousand with Clearchus:
the one going through as far as Phrygia as through their own land, the other
arriving beyond the Euphrates as through a desert. And what they enjoyed of
the empire was according to the wisdom of Oebares, who is said to have been
first to tell Cyrus, when he was annoyed at the much wandering, that he must
and it was necessary everywhere

to go about the empire willingly and unwillingly, if he were going to be king,
looking at the hide: that the parts of it he stepped on became low and touched
the earth, and the parts he left rose again, and when he trod them they were
lowered again. Some migrant kings, and they differed from the nomad Scythians
only so far as they did not go about on wagons but on carriages. Some nomad
kings and wanderers because of distrust and fear of sitting in the same place,
truly pressing their own country like some hide and by this ruling now
Babylon, again Susa, then Ecbatana, not knowing how to hold them always nor
tending them like shepherds. For such things they truly were, as if they did
not trust the empire to be their own. For they did not care for them as their
own, nor did they increase to beauty and size either the cities or the
countries, but like those who have fallen upon what does not belong to them
they spent shamefully and badly, seeking this, how they would rule the
weakest, and as if competing with one another over murders the second always
tried to outdo the former like pentathletes. And it was a contest to slaughter
as many as possible and to make as many houses and peoples homeless as
possible and to break as many oaths as possible. These things then they
enjoyed of the much-looked-at power, and what nature's law in turn appointed
to attend these: hatreds and plots from those so treated, and revolts and
battles among themselves and continuous strife and unceasing rivalries. They
themselves then enjoyed these things, ruling as from a curse rather than
according to a prayer, and the ruled had everything that those ruled by such
men must, and this has been almost already said. For a child's beauty was
fearful to parents, and a wife's to a husband. And it was necessary

that not the one who had done most injustice perish, but the one who possessed
most. And destructions and razings of cities then were more, I almost say,
than settlements now. And it was easier to be saved while making war than
while obeying: for in battles they were easily defeated, and in their powers
they committed outrage without measure. And those who paid court they despised
as slaves, and those who were free they punished as enemies, from which they
lived hating and hated. And so they had often feared their subjects more than
their enemies, using war for the most part as a reconciler. The cause was that
neither did those men know how to rule nor did the ruled fulfill what follows.
For it is not possible to be ruled well when the rulers rule badly. For rule
and mastery had not yet been distinguished, but king and master were equal. So
reasonably they did not even advance to great things: for this name does not
go beyond a household, and when it arrives at cities and nations it easily
stops. And Alexander in turn, who acquired the great empire as far as yours
and overran the earth, truly seems more like one who acquired a kingdom than
one who reigned. For as if a private man acquired much good land together, but
died before taking the fruits from it, so it seems to me to have happened to
him too. For he advanced over most of the earth and had subdued all who stood
against him and fully enjoyed all the hardships; but he was not able to
establish the empire nor to set a limit to the things labored, but died in the
middle of the course of affairs. So that one might say he succeeded in most
battles, but reigned least, and became a great competitor for kingship, but
enjoyed nothing worthy of his purpose and art, but suffered something like a
man competing in the Olympic contest and defeating his opponents, then dying
upon the victory, before well and finely

to fit a crown to his head. For what laws did he lay down for each? Or what
continuous assessments of money or soldiers or ships did he make? Or by what
customary administration did he lead affairs proceeding of themselves in fixed
periods of times? What constitutions did he constitute among the ruled? And
the one work and memorial worthy of his own nature that he left, the namesake
city by Egypt, this he founded doing you a kindness, so that you might have it
and rule the greatest after yours. So that he dissolved the Persians as
rulers, but did not rule nearest himself. And when he died, at once the
Macedonians were split into countless parts, showing in deed that the empire
was beyond them, and they could no longer even hold their own, but came to
such fortune that they were forced to leave their own so that they might rule
another's, as if more expatriated than able to master, and it was like a
riddle: Macedonians not in Macedonia, but each reigning where they could,
being more like garrisons of the cities and places than rulers, some uprooted
kings, not made so by the Great King but by themselves, and if one may say it,
like satraps empty of a king. And yet shall we say such a state resembles
brigandage more, or kingship?

But now bounds of the empire have been stretched, not to be blamed, nor so
that what is inside them can be taken by measure. But where the Persian's
empire was then bounded, starting from there to walk toward the west, the
remainder is much more than all of his. And nothing escapes you, not a city,
not a nation, not a harbor, not a place, except if you have judged some
useless. The Red Sea and the cataracts of the Nile and Lake Maeotis, which
earlier men heard of as at the ends of the earth, are equal even to a
courtyard's fences for this city. And the one which some of the historians
disbelieved even to exist at all,

Ocean, nor to flow around the earth, and that poets finding the name put it
into poetry for entertainment: this you discovered so well that not even the
island here escaped you. Being so great and so great in size, the empire is
much greater in exactness than in the circuit of the land. For neither Mysians
hold the kingdom, nor Sacae, nor Pisidians, nor other peoples in the middle,
some settled in by force, some in revolt and unable to be taken; nor is it
called the King's land while it belongs to all who can hold it; nor do satraps
fight one another as if they had no king; nor do cities stand apart, some
toward these, some toward those, and garrisons are sent out into some and
driven out of others; but as a courtyard enclosure swept clean, so the whole
inhabited world utters one voice more precisely than a chorus, praying
together that this empire remain for all time. So well is it fitted together
by this chorus-leader. It is ruled equally by all everywhere, and those
settled in the mountains are more humble than those in the lowest plains at
least as regards not resisting, and the allotment-holders and inhabitants of
the blessed plains are your farmers. Mainland and island are in no way
distinguished, but as one continuous country and one tribe all obey in
silence. Everything is accomplished from a command and a nod more easily than
one would pluck a string, and if something must happen, it is enough that it
be resolved, and it is done. And the rulers sent to the cities and the nations
are each rulers of those under them, but toward you and toward one another
they are all equally subjects. And one might say they differ from the ruled in
this too, that they first show how it is proper to be ruled. So great a fear
of the great ruler who administers

poor less, but always helping similarly whoever it may come to.

the barbarians in abundances and powers, and to have outstripped the Greeks in
wisdom and temperance, seems to me great and complete for a reckoning of
virtue, and more splendid than every contest. This then I am going to say, how
those men used even the affairs they had, such as they were; and if they
appear to have been unable to preserve things much smaller, what will be put
together for the vote is clear. For Athenians and Lacedaemonians did
everything for empire and hegemony, and their power was to sail the sea and to
rule the Cyclades and to hold the places in Thrace and Thermopylae and the
Hellespont and Coryphasium; and this was the power. And they suffered
something like a man desiring to become master of a body who would take some
nails and extremities instead of the whole body and think he had what he
wanted. So they too, having desired hegemony, harvested islets and headlands
on the sea and harbors and such things and were worn out about the sea, having
dreamed of hegemony rather than been able to acquire it. And yet having become
at times, as in a cycle of allotment, overseers of the Greeks each of them,
they did not preserve the post even for one generation, so to speak; not
without blame, at any rate. But they won against one another over the hegemony
that Cadmean victory, as the saying is, as if the one pair did not think it
right that the others alone be always hated, but themselves to take a share of
it. For one leader of the Lacedaemonians so disposed the Greeks that, having
willingly got free of them, they sought other rulers for themselves alone. And
having given themselves to the Athenians, when not much time had passed they
changed their mind, neither bearing the excess of the tributes nor those who
cheated them on the pretext of these, having been dragged up each year to

the others in wisdom. But this too is your discovery and came in together with
the rest. Since what was said of the Athenians is in danger of being true if
one should say it also of all the Greeks: they were better than anyone at
standing against those who ruled and defeating Persians and Lydians and
bearing wealth and toils, but they themselves were still uneducated at ruling,

cities, who were surely always no fewer than each of the natives and those to
whom they were sent. And they created suspicion also in those not yet
garrisoned, as bringing everything toward force and violence. So both things
happened: they neither held the cities securely and besides were hated, and
they harvested the evils of empire instead of the goods of empire, having the
greed not secure, but the reputation of greed strong. Then what besides these
things happened: being always pulled apart and divided they became weaker at
home and not sufficient to save their own, because of seeking to have the
others'. So they were then able neither to surpass in number those they
desired to rule by those they sent, nor did they leave themselves remaining
equal, but they were fewer abroad, fewer at home, and

another, but if all revolted in common they would be firmly free; for they

were so much meaner about themselves than the unfaithful

This, however, which had escaped almost all men before, was reserved for you
alone to find and to perfect. And there is nothing wonderful. For as in other
things the arts meet with the materials, so when the greatest empire and a
