O Zeus, what am I to do? Shall I be silent now that Smyrna lies fallen?
Having partaken of what nature of adamant? Or enduring this endurance of
what kind? But shall I lament? Having fitted what harmony, or having
taken what daring so great? For as many voices of Greeks and barbarians
as there are, both of those still above the earth and of those who have
been from all time, all coming together into one in speech, would be too
little even to attempt this matter alone, let alone to preserve its
worth. O I who have seen and heard the longest of the longest things. O
Time that bears all things, what form of a city you found out and
fashioned, and then have consumed. O all things unlike those before. The
ancient things: choruses of Curetes and nurturings and births of gods,
and Pelopes crossing from here, and the Peloponnese a colony, and
Theseus founder of the places under Sipylus, and the births of Homer,
and contests and trophies and victories among the rulers and through all
nations, and descriptions of the learned, recording her the most
beautiful of all. And indeed the things seen were better than narration.
To one approaching

at once a lightning-flash of beauty and numbers of magnitudes, and
measures and positions as of one harmony; feet planted upon shores and
harbors and groves, a middle equally rising above the plain and falling
short of the parts above, and a southern extremity rising little by
little, through the likeness of the whole secretly ending into an
acropolis, having a lookout of both the sea and the city. And that which
is neither speakable in word nor firmly graspable by sight, but escaping
I know not how, and always presenting a desire to take it in: the
brightness standing over the whole city; not destroying the eyes, as
Sappho said, but increasing and nourishing and watering them together
with good cheer; in no way like a hyacinth flower, but such as earth and
sun never yet showed to men. And as a statue accurately wrought in every
part has a spectacle for one going around it, so for you, formerly the
most beautiful of cities, but now untimeliness, there remained from
wherever one might always look. Now then the most beautiful form will be
at hand to appear, now suddenly becoming visible as one arrives, now to
one coming opposite, now from the suburbs, from the straits, from the
open sea, from land, from sea. These were the things before one met with
her. And as many gatherings and conversations as there were, what
satiety did they have for men? And to what other city did they leave
even coming near? O fountains, and theaters, and narrow streets, and
courses both covered and open-air. O form of the exceeding-bright
agora, O streets named for gold and of temples in each square, all of
them instead of an agora, O harbors longing for the arms of the dearest
city, O unspeakable beauties of gymnasia, O graces of temples and
precincts, where on earth have you sunk? O ornaments of the seashore, O
all those things, dreams. What springs of tears are enough for so great
an evil? What concerts and songs of choruses,

of all will suffice to wail over the city of beautiful choruses and
much-hymned and thrice-longed-for by men? O fall of Asia, O remaining
cities, O all the earth, and all the sea both within Gadeira and outside
Gadeira, O circle of stars, O sun that oversees all, what a spectacle
you endured to see. O child's play the sack of Ilium, child's play the
disasters of the Athenians in Sicily and the razings of Thebes, and
destructions of armies and sinkings of cities, and all that fire and
wars and earthquakes have accomplished up to this day. O you who
formerly hid all cities in beauty and Muses, but now have hidden the
fall of Rhodes. You were then to be sung among the Greeks of second
more wretched things. O day of offerings to the dead for all of the
same race. O common unlucky day for the Greeks, what a head of the race
you have carried off. What an eye you have plucked out. O ornament of
the earth, O theater of Greece, O weaving of Nymphs and Graces. O I who
have endured all things, where on earth do I now sing my monody? Where
is my council-chamber? Where the gatherings of young and older and the
clamor of those giving all things? There was once a city on Sipylus,
which the story says sank into the lake. O Smyrna, how far back your
prelude was sung for you. What a fortune you inherited, least of all
belonging to yourself. Now all birds ought to leap into fire, and the
city provides it in abundance; and the whole mainland ought to be
shorn, for in every way her lock of hair is gone; now rivers ought to
flow with tears, now merchant-ships ought to sail out with black sails.
O Meles flowing through a desert. O the present things sounding
opposite to the former songs. O song of swans and chorus of
nightingales, to join in lament. Surely even the Gorgons, if they still
survived, would not have mourned Medusa, nor their own eye, but the eye
of Asia. What Bosporus, or what cataracts, or what Tartessus did your
fame, O divine one, not reach? To what Massalia will the grief

this be bounded? Or at what Borysthenes? Who of the Greeks is so far
outside Greece? Who of the barbarians so unconquered and uncaptured by
the missiles and love-charms of Smyrna, who did not love her by
hearing, and will not be pained by hearing? The Heliades, they say,
mourning their brother and dying, changed their nature to poplars, and
their tear was frozen into amber; but you, O most graceful of cities,
it is the hour to lament even with the trees themselves.
