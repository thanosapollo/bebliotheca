* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Timoleon the Corinthian. Without doubt this man stood forth great in the
judgment of all. For to this man alone it happened, which I know not
whether to any, that he both liberated the fatherland in which he had
been born, oppressed by a tyrant, and from the Syracusans, to whom he
had been sent as aid, drove off an already long-rooted servitude, and
by his arrival restored all Sicily, for many years vexed by war and
oppressed by barbarians, to its former state. But in these things he
was not tossed by simple fortune, and, which is thought more difficult,
he bore prosperous fortune much more wisely than adverse. For when his
brother Timophanes, chosen as leader by the Corinthians, had seized
tyranny through mercenary soldiers and he himself could have been a
partner of the kingdom, he was so far from partnership of the crime
that he preferred the liberty of his own citizens to the safety of his
brother and judged it better to obey the laws than to command the
fatherland. With this mind through a soothsayer and a common kinsman,
to whom a sister born of the same parents had been married, he caused
his brother the tyrant to be killed. He himself not only did not lay on
hands, but did not even wish to look upon fraternal blood. For while
the thing was being accomplished, he was far off in a garrison, lest
any attendant could run to help. This most illustrious deed of his was
not approved in equal measure by all: for some thought piety had been
wounded by him and by envy they crushed the praise of virtue. The
mother indeed after that deed neither admitted her son into the house
to herself nor looked upon him without addressing him, detesting him as
a fratricide and impious. By which things he was so moved that
sometimes he wished to make an end of life and by death to withdraw
from the sight of ungrateful men.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Meanwhile, Dion having been killed at Syracuse, Dionysius again obtained
Syracuse. Whose adversaries sought help from the Corinthians and
demanded a leader whom they might use in war. Timoleon sent hither with
incredible good fortune drove Dionysius from all Sicily. When he could
have killed him, he would not, and he brought it about that he arrived
safely at Corinth, because the Corinthians had often been helped by the
resources of both Dionysii, of which kindness he wished the memory to
stand forth, and he judged that a most illustrious victory in which
there was more clemency than cruelty, lastly that it might not only be
received by the ears but also be seen by the eyes, whom from how great
a kingdom to what fortune he had brought down. After the departure of
Dionysius he waged war with Hicetas, who had been opposed to Dionysius:
that he had not dissented from hatred of tyranny but from desire was
evidence, because he himself, Dionysius having been expelled, was
unwilling to let go of command. This man having been overcome, Timoleon
put to flight the greatest forces of the Carthaginians at the river
Crinissus and compelled them to have enough if they were allowed to
hold Africa, who already for several years were holding possession of
Sicily. He also captured Mamercus, an Italian leader, a warlike and
powerful man, who had come into Sicily to aid the tyrants.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Which things having been finished, when because of the long duration of
war he saw not only regions but even cities deserted, he collected
those he could, first Sicilians, then he summoned colonists from
Corinth, because by them Syracuse had been founded at the beginning. To
the old citizens he restored their own, to the new he divided
possessions emptied by war, he repaired the thrown-down walls of cities
and the unroofed temples, he restored laws and liberty to the states:
from a greatest war he procured so much leisure for the whole island
that this man seemed the founder of those cities, not those who had led
them out at the beginning. The citadel at Syracuse, which Dionysius had
fortified for besieging the city, he threw down from the foundations,
he demolished the other bulwarks of tyranny and gave effort that as few
traces of servitude as possible should remain. Although he was of such
resources that he could command even the unwilling, and moreover had so
much love of all the Sicilians that with no one refusing it was
permitted to obtain the kingdom, he preferred to be loved than to be
feared. Therefore, as soon as he could, he laid down command and lived
as a private man at Syracuse for what remained of life. Nor indeed did
he do that unskillfully: for what other kings were able by command,
this man held by goodwill. No honor was lacking to this man, nor
afterward was any thing done publicly at Syracuse concerning which a
decree was made before Timoleon's opinion was known. No one's counsel
was ever not only preferred, but not even compared. Nor was that done
more by his goodwill than by prudence.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

This man, when he had already been advanced in age, lost the lights of
his eyes without any disease. Which calamity he bore so moderately that
neither did anyone hear him complaining nor did he less take part in
private and public affairs. He used to come into the theater, when a
council of the people was held there, carried because of health by
yoked beasts, and so from the vehicle he said what seemed good. Nor did
anyone attribute this to him as pride: for nothing ever either insolent
or boastful went out from his mouth. Who indeed, when he heard his own
praises being proclaimed, never said anything other than that in that
matter he especially gave and had thanks to the gods, because, when
they had decided to restore Sicily, they had then wished him most of
all to be leader. For he thought nothing of human affairs was done
without the will of the gods; therefore at his own house he had
established a shrine of Automatia and he cultivated it most
religiously.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

To this excellent goodness of the man wonderful chances had been added.
For he fought all his greatest battles on his birthday, by which it was
brought about that all Sicily held his birthday as a festival. A
certain Laphystius, a petulant and ungrateful man, when he wished to
impose a bail-bond on this man, because he said he was going to law
with him, and several had run together who were trying to restrain the
man's insolence with their hands, Timoleon begged the men not to do
that. For that it might be permitted to Laphystius and to anyone, he
had undergone the greatest labors and highest dangers. For this was the
appearance of liberty, if it were permitted to all to try by the laws
what each wished. The same man, when a certain man similar to
Laphystius, named Demaenetus, had begun in an assembly of the people to
detract from his deeds and was inveighing some things against Timoleon,
said that now at last he was condemned of his vow: for he had always
prayed this from the immortal gods, that he might restore such liberty
to the Syracusans, in which it would be permitted to anyone to say with
impunity about whom he wished what he wished. This man, when he had met
his last day, was buried publicly by the Syracusans in the gymnasium
which is called the Timoleonteum, all Sicily celebrating.
