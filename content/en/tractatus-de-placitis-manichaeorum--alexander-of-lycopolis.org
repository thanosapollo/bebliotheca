* Treatise on the Opinions of the Manichaeans
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

1 The philosophy of the Christians is called simple. And this gives the
greatest care to the formation of character, hinting enigmatically at the more
exact accounts concerning God; of which the chief point of the zeal about
these things all would reasonably accept, where they set the productive cause
as most honorable and most ancient and cause of all the things that are. Since
they also leave aside the more laborious matters for the ethical philosophers,
such as what moral and rational virtue is, and all that is said about
characters and passions, they spend their time on the hortatory topic, not
assigning elements toward the taking up of each virtue, but heaping up coarser
precepts as it happened, which the common people, hearing, as it is possible
to learn from experience, make great progress toward equity, and a character
of piety settles in their manners, kindling the disposition taken up from such
habit and leading them little by little into the desire of the beautiful. But
this having been divided into very many parts by those who came after, more
inquiries arose, just as among the eristics, from which it has come about that
some have become more ready and more inquiring than others, as one might say,
and some have already even stood at the head of heresies; from which the
formation according to character withered, these who thought it fit to lead
the heresies not attaining the exactness according to reason, and the great
multitude being disposed more factiously toward itself, and no canon being
present nor laws from which to furnish a solution of the inquiries. And just
as in other things ambition falling out into excesses damages everything, 2 so
also in these, each striving by the novelty of the opinions to surpass the one
before him, they have thrown this simple philosophy into an endless business;
as the so-called Manichaeus, who is a Persian by race, having in my opinion
surpassed all in saying wonders; and his innovation did not overflow long ago:
first a certain Papus by name came to us as an expounder of the man's opinion,
and after him Thomas, and some others after them, and he himself is said to
have lived under Valerian, to have campaigned with Sapor the Persian, and
having offended him in some way to have perished. Some such report of that
man's opinion arrived to us from the acquaintances of the man. He set down as
principles God and matter, and that God is good, and matter evil; and that God
exceeds more in good than matter in evil. And he speaks of matter not as
Plato, that which becomes all things when it receives quality and figure,
wherefore he calls it all-receiving and mother and nurse, and Aristotle, the
element about which are form and privation, but something else besides these;
for the unordered motion in each of the things that are, this he calls matter.
And other powers have been arrayed with God as attendants, all good, and
others likewise with matter, all evil. And the bright and the light and the
above, all these are with God, and the dim and the darkness and the below with
matter. And there are also desires for God, but these too good, and likewise
for matter, but all evil. 3 So at some time matter came into desire to arrive
at the upper place, and having arrived it admired the bright and the light,
how much there was with God, and indeed wished to occupy this principate,
thrusting God aside. And he both wished to punish this, and was at a loss for
an evil with which he might punish; for there is no evil in the house of God.
He sent therefore a certain power, that called by us soul,

upon matter, which would be mixed with it through all of it; for the death of
matter would be the later separation sometime of this power. So then by the
providence of God the soul has been mixed with matter, an unlike thing with an
unlike. And in the mixture the soul suffers with matter; for just as in a base
vessel what is in it is often changed along with it, so also the soul, having
suffered some such thing in matter, has been diminished from the nature that
it was into a sharing of evil. God therefore pitied this and sent some other
power, which we call demiurge; which, having arrived and taken in hand the
making of the world, there was separated from matter that of the power which
from the mixture had suffered nothing untoward, and sun and moon came to be
first, and that which had come to be in moderate evil, stars and the whole
heaven. Of the matter then from which sun and moon were separated, the part
has been driven outside the world, and that is fire, burning but dark and
without light, like night. And in the other elements and plants and animals in
these the divine power is borne mixed unequally. Wherefore also the world has
come to be and in it sun and moon, always separating the divine power from
matter in the generations and the destructions and sending it on to God. 4 For
indeed upon the demiurge another power, having come down to the light-form of
the sun, accomplished these things, and the thing is even manifest and, as one
might say, clear to a blind man. For in the increases the moon receives the
power being separated from matter and becomes full of this during this time,
and having been filled, in the wanings it sends it up to the sun; and he lets
it go toward God, and having done this he waits again for the migration of the
soul to himself from the other full moon, and having received it he likewise
lets it be carried of itself toward God, and he works this through all. And an
image also has been seen in the sun, such as is the form of man, and matter
strove in rivalry to make man from itself according to the mixture of the
power through all of it, he too having something of the soul; yet the form has
contributed much toward man sharing somewhat more of the divine power than the
other mortal animals, for he is an image of divine power. And Christ is
intellect; who indeed, having arrived at some time from the upper place, has
also loosed very much of this power toward God, and finally, having been
crucified, furnished knowledge in some such way, and that the divine power has
been fitted in, has been crucified in matter. Since then it is a dogma of God
that matter perish, they abstain from all ensouled things, and feed on
vegetables and everything that is without sensation, and they abstain from
marriages and sexual acts and the making of children, in order that the power
may not dwell still more in matter according to the succession of the race.
And they do not lead themselves out, while they devise a purification of the
things which the mixture of matter damaged in the power. 5 The more capital of
the things they say are these. And they honor especially sun and moon not as
gods, but as a way through which it is possible to arrive at God. And when the
divine power has been separated exactly, they say the outer fire, having
fallen in, will burn up both itself and all the rest, whatever of matter is
left. And those among them who are more elegant and not inexperienced in
Hellenic accounts remind us from their own, from the rites applying to the
account Dionysus cut in pieces by the Titans, just as they themselves say the
divine power is divided into matter; and from the poems of the gigantomachy,
that not even they were ignorant of the

rising of matter against God. And I would not say that these things are not
sufficient to win over those who receive the account without examination,
seeing that such a deception of accounts has even turned toward itself some of
those who have studied with us in philosophizing; but having now set myself
down to an examination of how it stands, I am at a loss what I should do. For
neither are their hypotheses from certain accounts that have been established,
in order that we may make the inquiry according to these, nor are there
certain principles of demonstrations, in order that we may see what follows
these. And it is truly a godsend, that of those simply said to philosophize,
who, having set up as basis the scriptures among them, both old and new,
supposing them to be god-breathed, accomplish their own opinions from there,
and seem to be refuted only then, if it happens that something not following
these is either said or done by them; and what among those who philosophize
according to the Hellenes the principles of demonstrations are, the immediate
premises, this among them is the voice of the prophets. But here, all these
being thrown out, and the things I mentioned before being said without any
demonstration, and it being necessary to make the answer with reason, but not
to set beside them other things more persuasive and more able to win over, the
approach is harder and still more difficult, because one must also make the
accounts varied. For if they are more exact, they would escape those already
preoccupied by these men without any belief; and if they should come to be for
winning over, they will fall into the same holds, for they will seem to have
come from like things. Wherefore there is need of much and great care and of
God truly, who will lead the argument. 6 He hypothesizes two principles, God
and matter. If, separating that which comes to be from that which is, the
hypothesis is not similarly base, in order that neither matter make itself and
admit the account of contraries, both making and being affected, nor again
such other things be contemplated concerning the productive cause, which
perhaps it is not even lawful to say; although God does not need matter toward
the products, all things being able to come to be in subsistence toward that
intellect. But if, what rather seems to be said by him, the unordered motion
of the things that are is matter, first he escapes his own notice in setting
up another productive cause, an evil-making one, however, and he does not see
also what follows, that if God and matter must altogether be supposed, some
other matter will be supposed for God, in order that for each of the
productive causes the underlying matter may exist. Instead of two, then, he
will be shown making four principles for us. And the opposition is also
wonderful. For if God is with him just what the good is, and he wishes there
to be supposed something contrary to him, why does he not, as some of the
Pythagoreans, set evil against him? At any rate it is more tolerable that it
is said by those that there are two principles, the good and the evil, and
that these are continually at strife, and that the good prevails; for if the
evil should exceed, all things will be destroyed. For in general matter by
itself is neither body nor something exactly incorporeal nor simply this
something, but some indefinite thing which, having taken on the form, becomes
definite, as the pyramid fire, the octahedron air, the icosahedron water, and
cube earth. How then is the unordered motion of the elements matter? By itself
then it is not able to subsist, for motion is in that which is moved; and
matter does not seem to be such, but rather the first underlying and unshaped
and that from which the others are. Since then the unordered motion is matter,
was it always with that which is moved, or has it at some time been separated
from this? For if at some time it was by itself, thus at least it would not
have been, for there is no motion without that which is moved; and if it was
always in that which is moved, again two principles will thus


subsist, that which moves and that which is moved; to which of them then does
he vote, in order that we may suppose that one first along with God? 7 There
is besides, as a trailing extra to the account, the unordered; this is to be
banished altogether; for whenever it is not motion, it would reasonably be
said. And which of the motions is matter? Whether that in a straight line or
the circular, or that according to alteration, or that according to generation
and according to destruction? But the circular is so orderly that it has been
assigned to the order of the all; and not according to these is this to be
blamed, in which are the sun and the moon, whom alone of gods they say they
revere. But that in a straight line? But this too has a limit, the attaining
of its own place. For the earthly has altogether ceased moving whenever it has
laid hold of earth, and every animal and plant growing whenever it has reached
its own bound, so that the standing of these would more reasonably become a
death for matter than the endless ending woven for it by them. And that
according to generation and destruction it is not even possible to conceive as
belonging to this hypothesis; for matter is ungenerated for them. And if they
will assign to it the motion according to alteration (for they seem especially
to say this, since through matter they say there are both changes of
characters and evils in the soul), it is worth examining how they even say
these things. For altering itself it will always begin from a beginning and
going forward will lay hold of the middle and will thus arrive at the end; but
having arrived at the end it will not stand (if at least alteration is its
substance), but will turn back again through the same things to the beginning
and likewise from there to the end, and doing this it will never cease. As if
AG should be altered, and B is the middle, the change from A will arrive at B
and from there at G; then again turning back from the extreme G to B it will
at some time arrive at A, and this will be through all. As the change from
black, and the middle is grey and the extreme white; and again conversely from
this to grey and likewise to black; and again from white the beginning of the
alteration, the same approach. If then 8 according to alteration matter is an
evil-making cause, it is shown thus to be not something more evil-making than
good-making. For let the beginning of the alteration be from the evil, then
thus from this through the indifferents the alteration is toward the good; but
from the good, then again through the indifferents the beginning; whether the
motion is toward this extreme or toward the other, the same account. And these
things are said from abundance; for every motion is about the quantitative,
and the leader of vice and virtue is quality; and that these are distinguished
even by genus, we know. And whether God and matter alone are principles, or he
leaves some other which is in the middle of these? For if there is nothing,
these are left unmixed toward themselves; for it is well said that, in order
that the extremes be mixed, there must be something in the middle so that it
may bind these; and if something else exists, it is necessary again that it be
either incorporeal or body, so that a third principle has come in as an
episode. And whether God and matter are altogether incorporeal, or the one
body and the other incorporeal, or both bodies? For if both are incorporeal,
neither is in neither; except if not as grammar in a soul (and this is absurd
to conceive in the case of God and matter); or if as in a void, as some say
the void has been poured around the all, the other again is without
subsistence, for the substance of the void is nothing; and if as accidents,
first this is impossible, for being without share of substance they can be
nowhere, for substance is as it were a vehicle laid under for the accidents.
And if both are bodies, it is necessary either that both be heavy or both
light or middle, or the one heavy and the other light or the other middle.
Whether then both are heavy, they must thus with all necessity be together,
and the same also in the case of the light and in the case of the middle; or
if they should differ, the one will altogether of the other

be separated. For another is the place for the heavy and another for the
middle and for the light, for the one the above, for the other the below, for
the other the middle. And of every spherical thing the below is the middle,
for from it toward every height as far as the upper surface the distance is
equal from all sides; and again all the heavy things are borne from all sides
upon it; wherefore also laughter came upon me when I heard that matter moving
unorderedly (for this is by nature for it) arrived at the region of God, which
is light and bright and all such things. And if the one is body and the other
incorporeal, first what is body alone is movable; then if they are unmixed,
each is apart according to its own nature, and if the one has been mixed with
the other, it would be either soul or intellect or accident; for only thus are
the incorporeals present to be mixed with bodies. 9 But how was it borne into
the region of God, or from what cause? For by nature the below and the
darkness belong to it, as they say, and contrary to nature the above and the
light; so that then the motion came to be for it contrary to nature and it
suffered some such thing as if someone should throw a stone or a clod of earth
upward; wherefore such things, having been lightened a little by the force of
the one who threw, having become aloft, are carried down into the same place.
Who then lightened matter into the upper place? For through itself and from
itself it would not move with this motion which belongs to it contrary to
nature; and it must undergo force, in order that it be raised aloft into the
upper place, just as the stone and the clod. But they leave nothing else with
it, but God. Clear then is what follows from the account, that according to
them God by force and necessity made this aloft toward himself. And if matter
is evil, there are altogether somewhere also such desires of it; and the
desire of the evil is base, and that of the beautiful is altogether serious.
And if matter desired the bright and the light, the desire is not base; just
as neither if someone being in vice should afterward at some time desire
virtue. For on the contrary it is in accusation that some serious person come
into desire of the base; just as if they should say that God desires the evils
belonging to matter. For the goods of God are not to be supposed such as
wealth is and much land and much gold, of which the transfer from men to
another becomes a lessening for the one who possessed them. But if one must
altogether conceive some image of these, I think one would justly compare
wisdom and the sciences. As then neither wisdom is lessened nor science nor
those who have them, if another shares the same, so it is not reasonable that
God grudged matter the desire of the beautiful, if indeed according to them it
desired this. 10 And they have far surpassed the myth-makers who either cut
the genitals of Ouranos, or compose the plots against Kronos by the son in
order that he himself occupy the principate, or again make Kronos swallow the
sons and then miss through the image of the stone. For how are the things said
by them not such, whenever they relate outright a war of matter toward God and
do not even say these things through a hidden sense, as Homer makes Zeus
rejoice at the war of the gods toward one another in the Iliad, hinting that
the world is composed of unlikes, fitted toward one another and both
conquering and being conquered. And these things have been said by me, since I
know such people, whenever they are at a loss for demonstrations, bringing
together from everywhere from the poems and making these a defense for their
own opinions. And they would not have suffered these things, if they fell in
with whatever it was with some thought. And all evils have been driven from
the divine chorus, and jealousy especially and envy; and these leave them with

God, whenever they say that he composed the plot against matter, because it
desired the beautiful. And with what of the things he had did God wish to
punish matter? For to say that God is simple is I think more exact than
according to these, and the narration of the thought of this is not easy as
about the others; for it is not possible to indicate it simply by an account,
but through much education and labor. But this at least we all know, that
anger and the desire of punishment against matter are passions constituted
about the one so disposed; and these affections would not even come to be
about some serious person, much less about the good itself. 11 The account
then comes round again to other things. For since they say that God sent a
power down into matter, it is worth examining whether this power has been
lessened according to the good beside God or had it similarly. For if it has
been lessened, what is the cause? For it shared nothing of matter of the
things with God, and he introduces one good only, God, and one evil, matter.
And if it had it similarly, for the sake of what did the one, as some king,
give orders, and the other endure this unwilling labor? And in the case of
matter it will also be inquired whether the powers are similar toward the evil
or less. For if less, they are altogether less of vice; therefore these would
become so by a sharing of the good, for of two evils the less such has
altogether somewhere thus stood by a taking of the good. And they leave no
good about matter. Then again another puzzle; for if some other power exceeds
in evil the matter that leads, this is more leading, for the more evil led the
rule according to it. 12 And "God sent down a power upon matter" is said with
no demonstration, and it is plausible not in any way at all, and it must
likewise itself meet with its own accounts. For they say the cause of this is
that: "in order that there be nothing evil and all things good, the power had
to be mixed with matter according to the athletes who in wrestling down
overcome the opponents, in order that, having mastered the evil, it stop it
from being." But I think it far more august and worthy of the superiority of
God to make matter vanish from the things that are together with a thought.
But I think they did not accept this because even now some evil exists, which
they say is matter. And the facts are able no more to cease being such, in
order that one grant that some are not changed into the worse; and there ought
to have come to be some perception of this, that these have been lessened
moderately in some way or other, in order that we may also have better hopes
for the future. For it has been well said toward the account of Zeno of
Citium, who saying "the all will be burned up," "everything that has what
burns, if it will burn, will burn the whole, and the sun is fire and will it
not burn what it has?" from which it was gathered, as he thought, that the all
will be burned up; toward whom one of the more elegant is said to have said:
"but I indeed yesterday and before a year and before more time saw and now
likewise see having suffered nothing by the fire of the sun; and this ought to
have come to be with time little by little, in order that we may also believe
that at some time the whole will be burned up." And toward the account of
Manichaeus, although said with no belief, I think the same answer, that "but
these have been lessened in nothing, but they also were before in the time of
the first man who came to be, where brother destroys brother, and even now
they still are, and the wars are the same and desires more varied; and they
ought, if they have not even ceased, at least to have been lessened, in order
that we may take heart that they will also cease at some time with time; and
if they are the same from there, what is the belief of the things toward the
future?"


13 And what even does he say the evils are? For about the sun and the moon he
leaves nothing; and about the heaven and the stars if he says there is some
such thing, and what this ever is, it is reasonable to inquire next. But
disorder according to them is the evil and the unordered motion; and these
always hold according to the same and in like manner, and one will neither
blame any of the wandering ones as having thought it fit at some time to
remain by a sign beyond the ordered time, nor any of the unwandering as not
remaining upon the same seat and not going around with the revolution of the
all, moving one degree toward the following in a hundred years. And if on
earth he blames the wildness of some regions, or the pilots blame the storms
in the sea, first according to them such things are of the portion of the
good. If at any rate nothing should grow on earth, all the animals would
perish most quickly; and this is able to send on to God much of the power
enclosed in matter, and many moons will need to come to be, in order that they
be able to hold at once so many souls drawing near. And the same also about
the sea; for it is a godsend to perish, in order that those who perish may
most quickly have the road toward God. And the wars upon earth and famines and
everything destructive are in the highest honor according to them; for
everything that is a cause of goods is to be honored, and these are causes of
good from the resulting destruction, sending on to God the power being
separated from those who perish. 14 And we have been ignorant, as it seems,
that the Egyptians honor in justice the crocodile and the lion and the wolf,
since these especially, being strongest beside the other animals, consume
them, feeding on them and destroying them in every way, and the eagle and the
hawk, because these too are destructive of the weaker animals in air and upon
earth. And perhaps according to them man is for this in the highest honor,
because most of all he is accustomed by devices and arts to subdue the most of
the animals, in order that he himself also may not be without share of this
good, he becomes food of others. Again then the generations are absurd
according to them, accomplishing from a small and chance seed something very
great, and it is much more beautiful according to them that these have been
taken away by God, in order that there might have come to be a short release
of the sojourn here of the divine power. But what even toward licentiousness
and injustices and every such thing, someone might say, will Manichaeus say?
Then toward these education and the law have been given as a helper; the
education taking thought that not even some such thing happen about men, and
the law punishing the one caught being in some of the injustices. Then what is
the charge against the earth if a farmer should neglect to tame this, that the
rule of God has been lessened, which is according to the just, whenever some
things are productive of fruits and some not such, or when the winds are borne
according to another cause it happens that some are benefited and some even
undergo some unwished things? And they ought not to have been inexperienced of
the nature of the contingent and of the necessary, for thus they would not
have made such things into marvels. 15 Whence then pleasure and desire? For
these they say especially are evils, and through which they have hated matter,
they are not something else more. That then these are only about the sentient
of the animals and nothing else lays hold of desire and pleasure except the
things that perceive, is manifest. For what sensation of pleasure and pain is
there for a plant, and what for earth or water or air? And the daemons, if
they are sentient animals, perhaps for this take pleasure in the things
established about the sacrifices and are distressed when they are left,
nothing of such a sort being able to be conceived about God. Those then who
say "why do the animals take pleasure and desire?" let them first blame why
these are sentient or why

they need food. For those which were immortal animals and have been released
from destruction and growth, as sun and moon and stars, although being
sentient, are also outside of these and of such a blaming; but man, being able
both to perceive and to judge and wise in potential (for he has power to
become such), having received what is his own, tramples it. 16 For in general
it is worth asking these men whether it is possible for no man to become
serious, or it is possible for some one at least. For if no one is wise, why
ever does Manichaeus himself (I let the others go) not only say he is serious,
but also say he is sufficient to make others such? And if there is someone at
all, what prevents all from becoming serious? For what is about one is
possible also about all, and through the things through which even one,
through these all would become virtuous; unless they say that most of the
power is enclosed in such people. Then again first what need is there of the
labor about education? For we would become serious sleeping. Or why especially
do such men lead their hearers into a hope of the beautiful? For even rolling
about with the courtesans they would have their own good. And if education and
the better turning-around and the seriousness about the taking up of virtue
have worked him such, let all become such, and the unordered motion of matter
chattered by them has been taken away. And it was much more beautiful to say
that wisdom has been given to men by God as a weapon, in order that what is
theirs through being sentient from desire and pleasure, this, leading it round
little by little into the good, may take away the following absurdity from
them; for thus they themselves, professing to teach virtue, would be objects
of emulation for the purpose and the life, and there would be much hope that
at some time the evils will cease, all having become wise. Which it seems to
me Jesus has considered, and in order that farmers and carpenters and builders
and the others from the arts may not be driven from the good, he seated a
common council of all together and through simple and easy conversations both
carried them into a thought of God and made them come into a desire of the
beautiful. 17 But how ever is it said that "God sent the divine power upon
matter"? For if it always was and neither God is conceived prior to it nor
matter, there are thus again three principles according to Manichaeus, and
perhaps even very many will appear a little later. And if it is adventitious,
how is it without share of matter? And if it is a part of God, first they thus
declare God composite and bodily; and this is absurd and impossible. And if he
made it and it is without share of matter, I wonder how neither this man nor
those from him considered that if, what is said according to the true opinion,
that the things next in order, God remaining, are subsistences, God wishing
made this power, how he did not also become cause of all the other things that
came to be, having needed in nothing a matter existing beforehand. The
absurdities appearing from this are such, and the next things follow. Did the
power then have a nature to flow upon matter? Or was this contrary to nature
for it? How then was it mixed? And if this is according to nature for it, it
was altogether somewhere also always with matter; and if this, how do they
speak ill of matter as being from the beginning a mixture toward the divine
power? And how will it also be destroyed, the divine power having been mixed
toward it having withdrawn at some time? For it was reasonable that the
beautiful things preserve and become causes of some other good for those with
whom they are present, rather than bring upon them destruction or some other
evil.


18 For that which is said by them as wise is this, that just as we see the
body itself perishing when the soul has been unyoked from the body, so also
when the power has let go matter, what is left, which is matter, having been
dissolved, will go away. For first they do not see that of the things that are
none is such as to be destroyed into the not-being, for the not-being is not;
and when the bodies have been scattered and have had some change a dissolution
comes to be, so that the one goes away into earth, the other into air, the
other into something else. Then they do not remember that they hypothesize
unordered motion to be matter. And that which is moved through itself and of
which the substance is the motion, and it does not belong to it according to
accident, how is it reasonable that when the power has gone away it cease from
what it was even before the power came down into it? And they do not see the
differing, that every body without share of soul is unmoved (for even the
plants have the vegetative soul), and of matter they say the substance itself
is motion, unordered however. And it was more beautiful, just as upon a lyre
singing out of tune the harmony having come worked the whole fitted, so the
divine power having been mixed with the unordered motion, which according to
