* On the Holy Martyrs
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

When the bishop had gone away to celebrate a martyrs' day in the country, the
homily was delivered in the city on the martyrs, and concerning compunction
and almsgiving.
=50.645=
αʹ. Yesterday was a martyrs' day, but today too is a martyrs' day; would that
we always celebrated a martyrs' day. For if those who are mad about the
theatres, and gape after the contest of horses, never take their fill of those
unseemly spectacles, much more must we be insatiable toward the feasts of the
saints. There a diabolical pomp, here a Christian feast; there demons leap,
here angels dance; there destruction of souls, here salvation of all who are
gathered. But those things have some pleasure? Yet not so much as these. For
what pleasure is it to see horses running at random and for nothing? But here
you do not see a team of irrational beasts, but countless chariots of martyrs,
and God standing over these chariots, and driving the road toward heaven. For
that the souls of the saints are a chariot of God, hear the prophet saying:
The chariot of God is ten thousandfold, thousands of them that thrive. For
what he granted to the powers above, this he has also bestowed on our nature.
He sits upon the Cherubim, as the psalm also says: He mounted upon the
cherubim, and flew; and again, He who sits upon the cherubim, and looks upon
the deeps. This he has given to us again also: he sits upon those, he dwells
in us; For I will dwell
=50.646=
and walk among you. Those have become a chariot; let us become a temple. Did
you see the kinship of the honor? Did you see how he made peace between the
things above and the things below? Therefore we are in nothing distant from
the angels, if we will. But as I said at the beginning, yesterday was a
martyrs' day, and today is a martyrs' day, not of those among us, but of those
in the country; or rather those too are among us. For city and country are
distant from one another in worldly affairs, but according to the account of
piety they share and are united. For do not look at their barbarian speech,
but at their mind that practices philosophy.

For what profit to me is sameness of speech, when the things of the mind are
divided? And what harm to me is difference of speech, when the things of the
faith are joined? According to this account the country is in nothing cheaper
than the city; for in the chief of goods they have equal honor. For this
reason also our Lord Jesus Christ did not spend his time in the cities and
leave the countries empty and deserted, but he went through cities and
villages, preaching the gospel and healing every disease and every infirmity.
This our common shepherd and teacher also imitated, and left us and ran to
them; or rather he did not leave us in going to them; for he went to our
=50.647=
brothers. And just as when the feast of the Maccabees was being celebrated,
the whole country poured into the city; so when the feast of the martyrs there
is being held, the whole city ought now to have transferred itself to them.
For this reason God planted martyrs not in cities only, but also in the
country itself, so that from the occasion of the feasts we might have a
necessary pretext for mixing with one another; and more martyrs in the country
than in the city. For to that which was lacking God gave the more abundant
honor; for that part is weaker; for this reason it has enjoyed more of the
care. For those who inhabit the cities enjoy continual teaching, but those who
live in rustic life do not share in such abundance. God therefore, consoling
the
poverty of teachers with the abundance of martyrs, arranged that more martyrs
be buried among them. Those do not hear a teacher's tongue continually, but a
martyrs' voice conversing with them from the tomb, and having greater power.
And that you may learn that martyrs have greater power even when silent than
we who speak, many often conversing with many about virtue have accomplished
nothing; but others, keeping silence, have achieved the greatest things
through the brightness of their life; so that much more the martyrs have done
this, not sending forth the voice through the tongue, but that through the
deeds themselves, which is far loftier than that from the mouth, through which
they converse with the whole nature of men, saying these words: Look upon us,
how many terrible things we suffered.

For what did we suffer, having been condemned to death, and having found
perpetual life? We were counted worthy to lay aside the bodies for Christ; and
if we had not now yielded them for Christ, a little later we were going, even
unwillingly, to be parted from them, from this temporary life. Even if
martyrdom coming upon them did not take them, still the common death of nature
coming on dissolved them. For these things we do not cease giving thanks to
God, that he counted us worthy to make use of the death owed in any case for
the salvation of our souls, and what was owed by necessity he received from us
as a gift, and with the greatest honor. But the tortures are
burdensome, and grievous. Yet in a brief turn of time, and the relief from
them in the immortal ages; or rather not even in a brief turn of time are the
tortures burdensome to those who look to the things to come, and gape toward
the judge of the contest. Since even the blessed Stephen, when he saw Christ
with the eyes of faith, for this reason did not see the showers of stones, but
instead of them counted the prizes and the crowns. You too then transfer the
eye from the present things to the future, and you will not even have a slight
sense of the terrible things.

βʹ. These things and more than these the martyrs say, and they persuade much
more than we. For if I should say that torture has nothing burdensome, I am
not trustworthy saying these things; for to philosophize such things in word
is nothing hard; but the martyr, sending forth the voice through the deeds,
has no one contradicting. And just as it happens inside
=50.648=
the baths, when the pool of the hot waters boils up, and no one dares to go
down, so long as those sitting on the brim exhort one another with words, they
persuade no one the more; but when one of them either lets down his hand, or
having slackened his foot, with confidence lets his whole body go in, silently
he would more persuade those sitting above to dare the pool than those who
speak many things; so also with the martyrs; for here too instead of the pool
of waters the fire is set forth. Those standing around outside, even if they
exhort with countless words, do not much persuade; but when one of the martyrs
lets down not the foot, nor the hand only, but applies his whole body,
furnishing the trial through the deeds more powerful than all exhortation and
counsel, he casts out the agony of those standing around. Did you see how the
voice of the martyrs is more powerful even when they are silent? For this
reason God left us their bodies; for this reason, having conquered long ago,
they have not even now risen, but they endured the contests so long a time
ago, yet have not yet attained the resurrection, not yet, for your sake and
for your benefit, so that you too, thinking of that athlete, may be stirred
toward the same course.

For to these no harm comes from the delay; but to you the greatest profit
arises from the occasion. For they will receive afterwards, even if they do
not receive now; but if he had snatched them out of the midst now, he would
have
taken from us much consolation and comfort; for truly the greatest consolation
and comfort comes to all men from the tombs of these saints. And you are
witnesses of what is said. For often when we were threatening, flattering,
frightening, exhorting, you did not receive such eagerness for prayer, nor
were you stirred up; but having gone to a martyr's shrine, with no one
counseling, seeing only the tomb, you poured forth many springs of tears for
the saints, and were warmed in your prayers. And yet the martyr lies voiceless
in much silence. What then is it that stings the conscience, and makes the
streams of tears well up as from a spring? This very image of the martyr, and
the memory of all that was accomplished. For just as the poor, when they see
others being rich, being in dignities, escorted with a bodyguard, enjoying
much honor from a king, learning their own poverty more exactly in the
prosperity of others, lament; so also we, when we remember the martyrs'
boldness of speech, which they have toward God the king of all, and the
brightness and the glory, and remember our own sins, seeing our own poverty
more exactly from
=50.649=
their abundance, ache and are in pain, learning how far we fall short of them;
and this is what produces the tears.

For this reason God left us the bodies here, so that when the crowd of affairs
and a multitude of worldly cares scatter much darkness over our mind, whether
from private or from public affairs (and there are many such), leaving the
house, going out of the city, bidding a long farewell to these tumults, we may
withdraw to a martyr's shrine, enjoy that spiritual breeze, forget the much
worldly leisure, revel in the stillness, keep company with the saints, beseech
their judge of the contest for our salvation, pour out many supplications, and
putting off the burden of conscience through all these, return home again with
much refreshment of soul. The tombs of martyrs are nothing else but safe
harbors, and springs of spiritual streams, and inexhaustible treasuries of
abundance, never found wanting. And just as harbors, receiving the ships that
have been washed over by many waves, set them in safety, so also the tombs of
the martyrs, receiving our souls washed over by worldly affairs, set them in
much calm and safety; and as the springs of cold streams restore bodies that
have toiled and been burned, so also these cool the souls that have been
burned by unseemly passions, and an unseemly desire, and a wasting envy, and a
boiling anger, and if any other such thing should trouble, they quench from
the sight alone, and they are better than treasuries of much abundance. For
the treasuries of money furnish many dangers to those who find them, and being
divided into many parts, they become less by the distribution; but here there
is nothing of this sort, but the finding is without danger, and the division
undiminished, contrary to the material treasuries. For those, as I said
before, when broken into small pieces become less; but these, when they are
divided among many, then rather display their own
abundance. For such is the nature of spiritual things: it increases by
distribution, and becomes more by division. Meadows are not so delightful,
furnishing roses and violets to those who look, as the tombs of martyrs,
furnishing to the souls of those who look some unfading and indissoluble
pleasure.

γʹ. Let us then touch those tombs with faith, let us warm the mind, let us
stir lamentations. Many things have been sinned by us, and great sins;
therefore we need much healing, intense confession. The holy martyrs poured
blood; let your eyes pour tears; tears too can quench a fire of sins; those
had their sides torn, they saw executioners standing around; do this also
yourself upon your conscience; seat the reasoning as judge upon the throne of
the unbribed mind, bring all that you have sinned into the midst, set fearful
reasonings over the offenses, punish the unseemly thoughts, from which the
sins have come;
=50.650=
let the mind be torn with much intensity. If we practice judging ourselves
thus, we shall also flee that fearful tribunal. For that he who judges himself
now, and
demands of himself exact accounts of what has been sinned, will not give
penalty in the future, hear Paul saying: For if we judged ourselves, we should
not be judged by the Lord. For accusing those who partake of the mysteries
unworthily, he spoke thus: He who eats and drinks unworthily shall be guilty
of the body and blood of the Lord. And what he says is of this sort: just as
those who crucified Jesus, he says, so also those who partake of the mysteries
unworthily will give penalty. And let not some condemn the word for excess.
The Master's body is a royal garment; and both he who tore the royal purple
and he who stained it with unclean hands alike insulted it; therefore they are
also alike punished; so it is also with the body of Christ. The Jews tore it
with the nails upon the cross, but you, living in sins, stain it with unclean
tongue and mind. For this reason Paul also held the same penalty over you, and
he adds saying: For this reason many among you are weak and sick, and not a
few sleep.

Then, showing that those who demand of themselves the accounts of their sins
here, judging their offenses, and no longer falling into the same, will be
able to snatch themselves from that future fearful and inexorable verdict, he
added saying: For if we judged ourselves, we should not be judged; but now
being judged by the Lord we are chastened, that we may not be condemned with
the world. Let us then tear our mind, severely rebuking the unchaste of our
thoughts; let us wipe off our stains through tears; great is the fruit of
these lamentations, great consolation and comfort. For just as great is the
punishment of laughter and of dissipation, so also continual lamentation
produces consolation. For blessed, he says, are those who mourn, for they
shall be comforted. Woe to those who laugh, for they shall weep. For this
reason Paul also, although conscious of no sin in himself, spent all his time
in tears and dirges. Who says these things? That blessed one himself: For
three years, night and day, he says, I did not cease with tears admonishing
each one of you. That man three years, we at least one month; that man nights
and days, that man for others' sins, we at least for our own offenses; that
man conscious of nothing in himself, we at least on account of our heavy
conscience. And for what reason does he weep? Why does he not teach and exhort
only, but also add tears?

Just as an affectionate father, having an only-begotten child who has fallen
into illness, not admitting the medicines of the physicians but thrusting them
away, sits beside him, flatters, kisses, embraces
=50.651=
him, wanting by the excess of the care to draw him and persuade him to receive
the correction from the treatment; so also Paul, loving the faithful
throughout the world as an only-begotten son, and seeing many who have fallen
into wickedness and incurable illnesses of soul, then not easily receiving the
rebuke, nor the correction from the censure, but leaping away, held them with
tears, so that seeing him lamenting and wailing, and being broken toward that
very sight, they might bear the healing, and putting off the illness return
again to health; for this reason in admonishing he always wept. And if Paul
uses such care concerning others' sins, with how much eagerness must we use it
toward the correction of our own? For great also is the power of grief
according to God toward our benefit, and concerning this Isaiah narrating
spoke thus, or rather God through Isaiah: For a sin I grieved him a little. I
did not, he says, impose a punishment worthy of the offense. For in good deeds
God exceeds the measures with the recompenses, but in sins, through his
loving-kindness convicting in many places, he brings a slight punishment of
what has been committed. Which then, hinting at this here also, he said: For a
sin I grieved him a little, and I saw that he was grieved, and he went gloomy,
and I healed his ways.

δʹ. Do you see how both swiftest and great is the benefit of repentance?
Having punished him a little for sins, he says, when I saw him become downcast
and gloomy, I also remitted that small punishment itself; so ready also is God
for our reconciliations, and he seeks only a slight pretext. Let us then
furnish him the occasions of friendship toward us, and let us try to keep
ourselves clean of sins, and if ever we should be tripped up, let us rise
quickly, mourning what has been committed with much exactness, so that we may
attain the joy according to God. For if, when he was grieved and went gloomy,
he reconciled God, he who has also added tears, and calling him with much
intensity, what will he not accomplish? I know that your mind has now been
warmed, but let us do this: that we may not, going outside, cool this warmth,
but hold it in ourselves. The soil of your mind is fertile, it receives the
seeds, and at once puts forth the ears of grain; it needs no delay, nor time;
but I fear your enemy. Outside the church the devil stands; for he does not
dare to enter
this holy fold; for where there is a flock of Christ, a wolf does not appear,
but he stands outside fearing the shepherd.

If we go out from here, let us not at once give ourselves over to untimely
associations, or to profitless words, and unprofitable pastimes, but as we
have in memory the things said, let us run home, and let each sit with his
wife, with his children, and study with exactness the things said. And if you
do not wish to withdraw home, gathering the friends who share the hearing with
you, sit together privately, and each bringing from himself what he was able
to keep, make the teaching complete a second time, so that
=50.652=
you may not gather here in vain. For the commandments of God are a lamp; For
the commandment of the law is a lamp and light, and life, and rebuke, and
instruction; and he who lights the lamp does not stand in the marketplace, but
runs home, so as not by a blast of winds to quench the fire, nor by a long
lingering to spend the flame; so then let us also do. The Holy Spirit has
kindled for us the teaching from himself; going out then and full of the
hearing, if we see a friend, or a kinsman, or a household member, or anyone
else at all meeting us, let us run past, so that our fire of the teaching may
not be quenched in the meantime while we converse with him of profitless and
superfluous things, but may be at its height in the mind as in a house, and
burning upon the high place of the reasoning as upon some lampstand may light
all the things within. For it is also absurd never to bear to see the house in
the evening without lamp and light, but to see the soul destitute of teaching.
From this most of our sins arise, that we do not quickly light a lamp for the
soul; from this we stumble each day; from this many things simply and as it
happens lie in our mind, since receiving the hearing of the divine words,
before we pass the porch of the church, we immediately throw them away, and
having quenched the light, we walk with much gloom. But if these things
happened before, let them happen no longer, but let us have the lamp
continually burning in the mind, and before the house let us adorn the soul.
For the house remains here, but the soul we take and go from here; therefore
it is necessary to count it worthy of more care. But now there are some so
miserably disposed, who adorn the houses here both with golden ceilings, and
with
flowering mosaics, and with the flowers of paintings, and with the brightness
of columns, and with all the rest; but they overlook the mind lying cheaper
than every deserted inn, full of mire, smoke, and much stench, of unspeakable
desolation. And the cause of all these things is that the lamp of teaching is
not continually burning for us; therefore the necessary things have been
neglected, and the things worth nothing enjoy much zeal. These things I have
said not to the rich only, but also to the poor. For they too, often adorning
their house according to their power, overlook the mind neglected; therefore I
make the teaching common to both, exhorting and counseling them to make little
account of the things in the present life, and to empty all their zeal into
the care of the spiritual and necessary things. Let the poor man look at the
widow who cast in the two obols, and not think poverty to be a hindrance to
the work of almsgiving and loving-kindness; let the rich man consider Job, and
just as that man possessed all that he had not for himself but for the poor,
so also this man. For this reason
=50.653=
he bore their removal nobly, since even before the devil's trial he had
practiced being a stranger to them. You too then despise the money while it is
present, so that when it has withdrawn sometime, you may not ache.

Spend it as needed when you have it, so that when you are deprived of it, you
may have a double gain, both the reward stored up for you from the best
expenditure, and the philosophy that comes from contempt, useful at the time
of their absence. For they are called money for this reason, that we may use
them as needed, not that we may bury them; they are called possessions for
this reason, that we may possess them, and not become their possessions. You
are lord of much money; do not then be a slave to these things, of which God
has made you master;
=50.654=
and you are not a slave when you spend them as needed, rather than burying
them. Nothing is so easily
tossed by the wind as wealth; nothing so easily changed as abundance. Since
then their possession is unstable, and often flies from us faster than any
winged thing, and leaps away more thankless than any runaway, for the time
that we are masters, let us use them as needed, so that having established
firm goods from the unstable money, we may inherit the treasure stored in the
heavens; which may it be that we all attain, by the grace and loving-kindness
of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father be the
glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of
ages. Amen.
