OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA, A FUNERAL ORATION FOR MELETIUS BISHOP OF
ANTIOCH
The young apostle has enlarged for us the number of the apostles, he who was
enrolled with the apostles; for the saints drew to themselves the like-minded,
the athletes the athlete, the crowned the wearer of crowns, the pure in heart
him who was chaste in soul, the ministers of the word the herald of the word.
Blessed indeed is our father in his apostolic company and in his departure to
Christ; but we are pitiable, for the unseasonableness of our orphanhood does
not permit us to pronounce our father's happiness blessed. For him it was
better to be with Christ through his departure, but for us it is hard to be
parted from a father's protection. For behold, it is a time for counsel, and
he who would counsel is silent; war has encircled us, a war =9.442= of
heresy, and there is none to command; the common body of the church languishes
with sicknesses, and we do not find the physician. See in what straits ours
are. I would that, if it were in any way possible, bracing up my own weakness,
I might ascend with the greatness of the calamity and utter some voice worthy
of the affliction, as those noble men have done, bewailing with a loud voice
the misfortune that has befallen them in their father. But what am I to
suffer? How shall I force my tongue into the service of the word, fettered as
it is, as it were, by some heavy shackle of the calamity?

How shall I open a mouth held fast by speechlessness? How shall I let loose a
voice that, from habit, slides down into passions and laments? How shall I
lift up the eyes of the soul, veiled as they are with the darkness of the
calamity? Who, cleaving this deep and dark cloud of my grief, will make the
ray of peace shine forth again out of the clear air? And whence will the ray
flash forth, our luminary having set? O the evil darkness, that hopes for no
sunrise of a star! Even in the opposite sense are the words spoken now in this
place from those spoken the day before. Then we danced as at a wedding, now we
groan piteously over the mourning. Then we sang an epithalamium, =9.443= now
a funeral ode. For you remember when we entertained you at the spiritual
marriage, bringing the virgin to dwell with the fair Bridegroom, you, and
brought in the gifts of our words according to our power, rejoicing in our
part and being made glad. But now our joy has been changed for us into
lamentation, and the robe of our gladness has become sackcloth. Or perhaps the
affliction ought to have kept silence and shut the pain up within with
silence, so as not to trouble the sons of the bride-chamber, who, not having
the bright garment of the marriage but clothing themselves in black, are of
the word. For since the fair Bridegroom has been taken from us, we have been
blackened all at once with mourning, and it is not possible, as is customary,
to brighten the discourse with the robe that adorns us, since Envy has
stripped it away. Full of good things we came to you; naked and poor we return
from you. We had the lamp upright above our head, shining with rich light;
quenched we carry it back, its brightness dissolved into smoke and dust.

We held the great treasure in an earthen vessel; but the treasure has
vanished, and the earthen vessel, emptied of its wealth, is restored to those
who gave it. What will those who sent it say? What answer will they make who
are called to account =9.444= for it? O wicked shipwreck. How have we been
wrecked in the midst of the harbor of our hopes? How has the freight-bearing
hull, sinking with all its cargo, left us naked who were once rich? Where is
that bright sail steered through all by the Holy Spirit? Where is the sure
rudder of our souls, by which we passed untouched over the heretical billows?
Where the immovable anchor of resolve, resting on which in all safety we took
our ease? Where the fair pilot who steered the vessel toward the haven above?
Are the things that have happened small, and do I grieve unreasoningly? Or
rather, even should I cry aloud in speech, I do not attain to the calamity.
Grant us, brothers, grant us the tear of sympathy. For when you rejoiced, we
shared your gladness with you. Repay us then this wretched return. To rejoice
with those who rejoice, that we did; to weep with those who weep, that do you
render back. =9.445= Once a strange people wept over the patriarch Jacob,
and made the calamity of another people its own, when those who carried their
father up out of Egypt mourned with one accord, in a foreign land, the
affliction that had befallen him, thirty days and as many nights, prolonging
the lament over him. Do you, brothers and kinsmen, imitate those of another
race. Then the tear of strangers and of the people of the country was common;
let it be common now also, since the affliction is common. Behold these
patriarchs; all these are children of our Jacob; all are of the free. None is
base-born or supposititious; for it was not lawful for him to introduce into
the nobility of the faith a servile, low-born stock. Therefore he is your
father there also, because he was the father of your father. You have just now
heard Ephraim and Manasseh, what things and how many they related of their
father, wonders surpassing speech. Grant me also to say something of these;
for it is henceforth without peril to pronounce him blessed; I no longer fear
Envy, for what worse can it work upon me? Know then who the man was. Noble, of
them from the sunrising of the sun, blameless, just, true, devout, abstaining
from every evil thing (for surely =9.446= the great Job will not be jealous,
should even his imitator be adorned with the testimonies concerning him). But
Envy, which sees all things fair, saw also our good with a bitter eye, and he
who walks to and fro in the world walked through us also, planting broadly the
footprint of affliction upon our prosperity. It is not herds of oxen and sheep
that he maimed, unless perhaps in a mystical sense someone should transfer the
flock to the church; but not in these has Envy wrought us the harm, nor in
asses and camels has he worked the loss, nor has he wrung our senses with a
wound of the flesh; but he has stripped us of the very head. And with the head
our precious organs of sense departed. No longer is there the eye that beheld
the things of heaven, nor the ear that was taught of the divine voice, nor
that tongue, the pure offering of the truth.

Where is the sweet calm of those eyes? Where the bright smile upon the lips?
Where the courteous right hand, waving the fingers to accompany the blessing
of the mouth? I am led forward, as it were, to cry out the calamity upon a
stage. I pity you, O church; to you I speak, city of Antioch; =9.447= I pity
you for this sudden change. How has the beauty been unadorned? How has the
adornment been stripped away? How suddenly has the flower flowed away? Truly
the grass has withered, and the flower has fallen. What evil eye, what evil
witchery, has raged madly against that church? Such things in return for such.
The fountain has failed; the river has dried up; again the water has been
turned into blood. O that miserable tidings, announcing to the church the
affliction. Who will say to the children that they are orphaned? Who will
announce to the bride that she is widowed? O the evils. What did they send
forth, and what do they receive? They sent forth an ark, and they receive a
coffin. For the man of God was an ark, brothers, an ark containing within
itself the divine mysteries. In it the golden jar, full of the divine manna,
full of the heavenly nourishment. In it the tables of the covenant, written
upon the tablets of the heart by the Spirit of the living God, not with ink;
for nothing dark and black of thought had been burned into the purity =9.448=
of the heart. In it the rod of the priesthood, which blossomed in those hands.
And whatever else we hear the ark to have held, all was embraced in the soul
of the man. But in place of these, what? Let the word be silent. Clean linen
cloths and woven stuffs of silk, abundance of myrrhs and perfumes, the loving
care of a modest and seemly woman; for it shall be said, that these also may
become a testimony for her, what she did lavishly for the priest, pouring out
the alabaster jar of myrrh upon the head of the priest. But what is preserved
in these? Dead bones, which meditated their deadness even before death, the
grievous memorials of our calamities.

O what a voice shall again be heard in Rama; Rachel weeping, not for her
children but for her husband, and refusing consolation. Let be, you who
console; let be. Do not prevail =9.449= to console. Heavy be the widow's
mourning. Let her feel the loss she has suffered; and yet she is not
unpractised in separation, having been trained beforehand, in the contests of
the athlete, to bear her solitude. You remember surely how the discourse
before ours related to you the contests of the man, how through all honors he
preserved the honor of the holy Trinity, even in the number of the contests,
having contended against the assaults of three temptations. You have heard the
order of his labors, what he was in the first, what in the midst, what in the
last. I judge a repetition of what has been well said superfluous, but perhaps
it is not unseasonable to say only this much: when that sober-minded church
first saw the man, she saw a face truly fashioned after the image of God, she
saw love welling forth, she saw grace poured round the lips, the utmost term
of humility, beyond which it is not possible to conceive more; according to
David meekness, according to Solomon understanding, according to Moses
goodness, according to Samuel exactness, according to Joseph temperance,
according to Daniel wisdom, according to the great Elijah zeal for the faith,
according to the lofty =9.450= John incorruption of the body. She saw the
concourse of so many goods in one soul; she was wounded with the blessed
longing, loving her own Bridegroom in the pure and good affection. But before
she could fill her desire, before she could give her longing rest, still
glowing with the love-philtre, she was left alone, the temptations calling the
athlete to his contests. And he toiled in the sweat for the truth, and she
endured in temperance, keeping the marriage. Long was the time between, and
one kept coming down upon the undefiled bridal-chamber with adulterous intent.
But the bride was not defiled. And again a return, and again a flight, and a
third time likewise, until the Lord, cleaving the heretical gloom and casting
upon it the ray of peace, gave some respite to hope from the long labors. But
since they saw each other again and the spiritual joys and meetings of the
mind were renewed, and the longing again was kindled, straightway this last
departure cuts off the enjoyment. He came to deck you as a bride, and he did
not miss his aim. He laid upon the fair union the crowns of blessing;
=9.451= he imitated his own Master. As the Lord in Cana of Galilee, so here
the imitator of the Lord. For the Jewish waterpots, filled with heretical
water, he filled full of the unmixed wine, transmuting the nature in the power
of the faith. Often he set among you the sober mixing-bowl, lavishly pouring
out grace with his sweet voice. Often he set before you the rational banquet;
he led the way, blessing, and those fair disciples served the multitudes,
refining the word. And we rejoiced, making our own the glory of your race.

How fair until now the narratives; how blessed a thing it were to end the
discourse in these. But after these things what? Call for the mourning women,
Jeremiah says. For it is not otherwise possible for a burning heart, swollen
with the affliction, to be cooled, unless it be lightened by groans and tears.
Then the hope of his return soothed the separation; but now he has been torn
away by our final separation. A great chasm has been fixed in the midst
between him and the church. =9.452= He rests in the bosom of Abraham, but
there is no one carrying the drop of water to cool the tongue of them that are
in torment. Perished is that beauty; the voice is silent; the lips are mute;
the grace has fled; the fair report has become a tale. Once Elijah, flying
from the earth to God, grieved also the people of Israel; but Elisha soothed
the separation, being adorned with the mantle of his master. But now the wound
is past healing, since Elijah has been taken up and Elisha has not been left
behind. You have heard certain gloomy and plaintive voices of Jeremiah, with
which he bewailed the city of the Jerusalemites laid waste, and he set forth
other things also pathetically, saying: The ways of Zion mourn. These things
were said then, but now are fulfilled. For when the report of the affliction
goes round, then the ways will be full of those mourning, and those shepherded
by him will pour themselves forth, imitating upon the affliction the voice of
the Ninevites, rather suffering pain more bitter than theirs. For them the
lament loosed the fear, but for these no release of the evils is hoped for
from the laments. I know another voice =9.453= of Jeremiah also, reckoned
among the books of the Psalms, which he made over the captivity of Israel,
when, sitting upon the rivers of Babylon, they bewailed the memory of their
own goods. And the word says: We hung our instruments upon the willows,
condemning ourselves and our instruments to silence. I make this song my own.
For when I see the heretical confusion (for confusion is Babylon), and when I
see the temptations flowing through the confusion, these I say are those
Babylonian streams, beside which we sit and weep, because we have not him who
led us through them. And even if you speak of willows and of the instruments
upon them, this riddle also is mine. For truly our life is among willows; for
the willow is an unfruitful tree, and from us has flowed away the sweet fruit
of life. Therefore we have become unfruitful willows, having hung upon the
trees the instruments of love, idle and unmoving.

If I forget you, he says, O Jerusalem, let my right hand be forgotten. Grant
me to alter the written word a little: that it is not we who have forgotten
the right hand, but our right hand has forgotten, and the tongue, cleaving to
its own throat, =9.454= has barred the passages of the voice, so that we
should no more hear again that sweet voice. But wipe away for me the tears.
For I feel that I am made womanish beyond what is right over the affliction.
The Bridegroom has not been taken from us; he stands in our midst, even if we
do not see him. The priest is within the inmost places; he has entered into
the places within the veil, where the Forerunner entered for us, Christ. He
has left the veil of the flesh. No longer does he worship by type and shadow
of the things in heaven, but he looks upon the very image of the realities. No
longer through a mirror and in a riddle, but face to face he makes
intercession to God; and he intercedes for us and for the ignorances of the
people. He has laid aside the coats of skins; for those dwelling in paradise
have no need of such coats; but he has garments =9.455= which he wove for
himself in the purity of his life and carried off. Precious before the Lord is
the death of the holy, or rather it is not death but a loosing of bonds. For
You have broken my bonds, he says. Simeon has been let go; he has been freed
from the bonds of the body. The snare is broken, and the bird has flown away.
He has left Egypt, the muddy life. He has crossed, not that Red Sea, but this
black and gloomy sea of life. He has entered into the land of promise; upon
the mountain he philosophizes with God. He has loosed the sandal of the soul,
that with the pure sole of his understanding he may tread that holy land in
which God =9.456= is beheld.

Having this consolation, brothers, hear Paul charging you, you who are
conveying the bones of our Joseph into the land of blessing: Sorrow not as the
rest who have no hope. Say to the people there; tell the fair tales. Tell the
incredible wonder, how the ten-thousand-man people, packed dense at the sight
of the sea, all became one continuous body, like some water surging around the
procession of the tabernacle; how the fair David, dividing himself in many
parts and many ways into myriads of ranks, danced before the tent among men of
other tongues and of the same tongue; how the rivers of fire on either side,
flowing like some stream of waters unbroken in the continuity of the lamps,
were stretched out as far as the eye could take in. Tell of the eagerness of
the whole people, the assembly of the apostles; how the napkins of his body
were plucked apart for keepsakes of the faithful. Let there be added to the
tale the king, gloomy over the affliction, rising from his throne, and the
whole city passing over in the procession =9.457= of the saint; and console
one another with these words. Fairly does Solomon heal grief; for he bids wine
be given to those in grief, saying this to you, the laborers of the vineyard.
Give then your own wine to those who grieve, not the workman of drunkenness
but that which gladdens the heart. With a stronger draught and fuller cups of
the word receive it, so that our mourning be turned again to gladness in
Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be glory unto the ages. Amen.
