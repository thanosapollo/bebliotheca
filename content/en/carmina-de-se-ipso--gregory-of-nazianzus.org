BOOK II. HISTORICAL POEMS. SECTION I. CONCERNING HIMSELF. Αʹ. Concerning the
things about himself. Christ the King, who once with holy hands lifted up in
the form of a cross of Moses on the mountain of your servant, you bent the
deadly strength of Amalek; who also with hands stretched out under Daniel in
the pit bound the terrible gapings of lions and the dreadful points of claws;
970 through whom also Jonah sprang out from the great sea-monster, having
prayed and stretched his hands in the bowels; and in the Assyrian flame a dewy
cloud covered round the three bold children, when they spread out their hands;
who once walking on foot over the whole seething sea stilled the waves and the
might of winds, so that you might 971 draw the disciples from the deep, tossed
by the gales; and for many you loosed both souls and limbs from diseases, as
God, and being mixed as a mortal you were mingled with mortals; of which the
one you were from above, and the other you appeared to us last, so that you
might make me a god, since you yourself became mortal; thus, blessed one, come
also to me as a gracious God when I call; come bringing a hand, God gracious,
that you may save me worn in war, and among beasts, and in flame, and in
winds, and bearing my eye to heaven alone. For indeed beasts and the wild
swell of the sea, and groaning strife, and the rush of burning fire, all these
wicked men become, destroyers of life, who hate most those who love God, 972
neither fearing the justice that meets them afterwards, nor regarding mortals
who abhor the sinner. From these keep me away, Christ, and guard me kindly,
holding me around with your wings, and cast far the mournful cares of your
servant, O King, and let not heavy anxieties shake my mind, which this world,
and the ruler of the world, raises for wretched mortals, gnawing, as rust
iron, the godlike form within, and making the better lot connate with earth,
so that the soul may not draw the dust upward when it weighs toward the earth;
and dust may cast the winged soul to the ground, ill-fated, made flesh in
muddy works. For there are two gates of hateful death for mortals. 973 And
some beget within their minds a turbid spring of wickedness, who always care
for lawless works, and the body, and insolent satiety, and mournful devices;
who, urging them to every transgression, take pleasure in sin, embracing their
own doom; but others, looking upon God with pure eyes of mind, and hating the
offspring of the shameless world, insolence, live far from wretched life with
shadow-like flesh, and tread the earth with lighter feet, following God who
calls, light in spirit, initiates of the hidden life of Christ the King, so
that they may later flash when it shines. But even so they are pierced by the
evil thorns of life 974 by necessary need, and from without the raging,
evil-working daemon has devised countless goads of doom, ah, for wretched
mortals; and often hiding mournful destruction in a fair form, when fighting
against them he gives way. For such a plague he weaves upon men, as bronze
under bait, when it brings doom to fish, who, longing for life, have drawn
unforeseen destruction into their bowels, gaping around their own doom. So
also to me the crafty one, when I knew him to be darkness, putting on a fair
skin, came like light, if somehow longing for virtue I might draw near to
wickedness, my lighter mind being stolen toward destruction. Marriage did not
bind me, the stream of life, which matter 975 cast as the greatest bond upon
men, the beginning of a burden; nor did the fair threads of silkworms take me,
nor did I admire a rich table, feeding a wide-gaping belly, the lawless mother
of lewdness; nor did I love to dwell in great and splendid houses; I did not
warm my heart in the tender strikings of the muse; nor did a soft feminine
breath of unguents run through me. But of others gold and silver, who love
sitting upon countless possessions

to tend them, they have little delight, much toil. But for me barley-cake is
dear, and a sweet relish, salt, and a makeshift table; and upon these sober
water as drink; this is the best wealth for me, and Christ always lifting my
mind. 976 Not the acres of wheat-bearing earth, and fair groves, nor herds of
oxen, and fat flocks of sheep, nor dear servants, my race, whom ancient
tyranny split, and set a double name, nobles and slaves, born of one earth,
either of earth, or of God. And a sinful law followed. Nor have I any need of
a breath flowing most swiftly, of human honor, and perishing glory. It is no
great thing to have a gift from the king within the court, nor did desire of
the thrones of justice ever take me, on which I was going to sit famous and
raise height above the brows; nor to have great strength among many, or among
citizens, delighting in lying and feeble dreams 977 going now upon one now
upon another, and flying away alike, either catching with palms the streams
rushing by, or having a shadow in the hands, or grasping mist. For such is the
race of mortals, and such also is prosperity, prosperity like the faintest
tracks of a ship, scored in front and destroyed behind. Only the glory of
words was dear to me, which East and West and Athens, the boast of Hellas,
gathered. Upon these I toiled much for a long time, but even these I set prone
on the floor before Christ, yielding to the Word of the great God, who covers
all the turning, many-shaped speech of mortal mind. But these I escaped, yet I
did not flee the faithless hatred 978 of an enemy lying in wait in a kindly
face. I will tell openly my misery to all; so that someone may escape the
thoughts of the crooked beast. I thought, sitting beside my parents, O King
Christ, worn with hateful old age and grief, of whom I alone of children am
left, a dim hope, a little gleam from a great torch that is no more, to do
things pleasing to you, blessed one, and to your laws, you who gave sons to
mortals to have as helping strength, and for the trembling to lean as a staff
under their limbs. For indeed of all who honor you, they care for piety, and
avoiding the woes of hard life they have fastened their stern-cables to your
undefiled ordinances. And you are to them end and beginning. 979 She, then,
bringing from her fathers a faith pleasing to God, threw a golden chain about
her children, having a manly spirit in a female form, touching earth and
regarding the world only so much as to push all this life toward heavenly
life, and to lift a light foot toward the aether. But he was formerly a wild
olive living under idols, but was grafted under the stem of a fair olive, and
received so much of the noble root that he covered trees, and satisfied many
with honey-sweet fruit; both the mind of the city and the head of the city,
gentle, sweet-speaking, a new Moses, or some Aaron, standing between mortals
and the heavenly God, with our pure rites and sacrifices, such as a mind holy
within offers, 980 gathering mortals and the great immortal God into one. Of
such a father and mother am I, with whom it is not lawful to contend, yet I do
not grudge that they contend with one another. These I, tending and attending
in their toil, warmed my mind with hopes, as accomplishing something best and
bearing the debt of nature. But that was true, that for sinners the road of
pits is full, so that once even to me evil came from good. For indeed dense
and painful cares, feeding on soul and limbs night and day, bring me down from
heaven to earth my mother. First, to rule over servants, what a net of
destruction! who always hate bitter masters, but shamelessly trample the holy,
neither 981 gentle to the bad, nor obedient to the good; but toward both
breathing goads of wrath beyond reason. Then to tend property, and always to
have Caesar's burden on the shoulders, and to bear the strong cry of the
tax-collector (for tribute dishonors the free day for mortals, following upon
possessions, and a fetter lies upon the lips), and about the roar of the
crowded marketplace, and the high seats that care for mortal judgments

to turn about, and to bear the tumult of pleadings against opponents, and to
suffer pains in the twisting snares of laws, where there is both battle and
toil, and the wicked have more than the good, and those who are helpers of the
laws are 982 for sale to both; and if some wicked man has more, he is the
best. Who could avoid many lies and craft-weavings apart from God, among such
men? For necessity shakes off everything from one fleeing headlong from evils,
or even blackens the dear heart with sins; as those drawing near the evil
breath of raging fire, bearing the mournful signs either of fire or of smoke.
But these things might be bearable. The more painful wound for me is all that
I endured when my brother left this life, and I still expect to suffer. For
those who have come upon unexpected woes do not think there are greater. 983
While he lived, I had glory as from others. For wealth never took my mind, nor
any strength. But pains and groanings, when he died, I alone received. And of
the goods he had acquired, some the earth took, gaping, when Nicaea fell with
earthquakes; others the daemon made a prey for the grasping hands of sinners.
But he himself, hidden, escaped death, the hand of the heavenly God stretching
over the roof where he was. Woe is me for Caesarius! Formerly in the palaces
you shone as some morning star, a reverend name, bearing the heights of wisdom
and of lovely character, and plumed with many strong and dear companions. 984
For many you found a cure of diseases for toiling bodies; and to many you gave
a release from poverty, doing what was due. But now having died, you have
satisfied many dogs, who bark at me standing from all sides; and none of my
kinsmen helps me. And few love in the same way as enemies. Before receiving
they honor, but having received they hate. As of a high-leaved oak fallen by
the violence of winds, they snatch the branches standing around, one from one
side another from another, or a great vineyard, when the fence is broken,
travelers running by reap without pity, and from the thicket a solitary feeder
has harmed it with his own tooth; but for me the toil is full of groaning.
There is no longer strength of my hand either to satisfy all, or to keep them
off. For from the time when first, cutting the soul from life, 985 I mixed it
with bright heavenly thoughts, and a steep mind bearing me set me far from
flesh, raising me from there, and hid me in the recesses of the heavenly tent,
and the light of the Trinity shone around our eyes, than which I have
perceived nothing brighter, high-throned, sending forth a common and
unspeakable gleam, which is the beginning for all that time shuts off from
above; from that time I have died to the world, and the world to me; and I am
a breathing corpse, and my strength is as of dreams; and my life is elsewhere,
and I groan under thick flesh, which the wise call the darkness of the mind.
And I long, loosed from this life and from the misty sight, and from those
going on the ground, wandering and being wandered, to look more purely upon
Him who stands, no longer 986 mixed together with dim images, as before, by
which even the eyes of the sharpest mind wander, but looking upon truth itself
with the eye of a pure mind. But these things are behind; and the things here
are worthless smoke, or dust, who have exchanged the great life of life, the
high for the earthly, and the steadfast for the perishing. Therefore all have
also set upon me, and they do not wish to give way, hastening upon a most
ready hunt. Ah ah, and Caesarius is mournful dust, who kept all the crowd from
me, giving me to avoid every burden? Honoring as never another honored a
brother, and respecting as if someone were embracing his own father. But I do
not so much lament, not the 987 scattering of life, which I longed to have in
common with the poor, as being myself also a sojourner here, a wanderer,
looking to the hand of the Most High, the giver of good things; not because of
the hateful outrage against all mortals, which fills even a gentle man most
quickly with wrath; nor of brothers, whom the tomb covers round, short-lived,
admirable to all upon the earth, so much as I lament my soul, as someone
seeing a queen, fair and great and of noble kings, toiling in strong fetters,
whom enemies took with the spear, and bound in hard slavery, and she fixes
grim eyes upon the ground. Such things I have suffered;

such a wound then I have in my heart. There is an ancient saying, that when a
bitter viper 988 strikes its tooth upon a man, a deadly wound, the
evil-working one wishes this to be told only to those on whom the mournful
viper has wiped destruction with fiery wrath; for he alone also knows the evil
pain, so I too will declare my toil to those who have a common love, a common
evil, a like pain. For they alone would receive my speech kindly, and would
know best the mysteries of a wailing heart, who longing to lift the burden of
the cross upon the shoulder, and having a lot in the precincts of the great
King, love the good path, and pity those who have fallen, but to the others I
would be a laugh telling my cares, whose light faith has scored only the
surface of the heart, 989 nor has a sharp longing of the King gone through the
bowels; and they live here only, thinking of things of the day. But let them
waste away, since they arm the tongue equally against all, a most ready
weapon, against other good men, and against the wicked. But I will not cease
from lament until I escape groaning wickedness, and set a bar upon the
ravenous passions of the mind, for which now bitter Satan has thrown open all
the doors, that were shut before, when the hand of God covered me, nor did
wickedness have a nearby occasion, which most quickly catches, as the might of
fire near standing stubble, until even from wind the torch is lifted to the
air. Would that I had hidden my body before these things in cliffs, and
mountains, and rocks; surely I would have borne all this life, having fled the
cares of life and of flesh, 990 wholly carrying Christ in my breast, dwelling
alone apart from others, and lifting a pure mind to God alone; until even with
light hopes I reached the goal. Would that I had; but longing for dear parents
held me back, dragging, as a balance, toward the earth; not so much longing,
as pity, cutting through marrow and all inwards, which is the gentlest of all
sufferings, pity of godlike grey hair, pity of distress, pity of
childlessness, and pity also concerning a child, for whom they tremble around,
always having a sweet toil, the eye of life, and distressed at pains. O books
that formerly were my care, which the Spirit scored with the tongues of the
holy, and within the letter 991 the flashing grace of the good Spirit, and the
hidden benefit appearing to pure mortals alone; and dear prayers and
groanings, and sleepless nights, and angelic choirs, who provoke God with
psalms, standing, and sending souls to God in hymns, uttering a common voice
from many mouths; and the toil of the belly that is first of evils, and
measures of laughter, and stillness of tongue and of eye, and the bridle of
raging wrath. And the Word bound the wanderer looking every way, and turned
the mind to Christ with heavenly hopes. And the Word lay upon all, leading the
image toward the King. And God delighted in the impulse. 992 And instead of
possessions and painful tumults, which provoke me even with nightly evil
dreams (for the phantoms of night are like daily cares), there was in my eyes
the gleam of God, and the all-shining choir-standing and glory of pious souls.
But now all the treasures have perished from my soul, which formerly consorted
with all the best, and only longing remained within, and unexpected pain.
Therefore I wail. And tomorrow I do not clearly know, whether God will lead me
back to the former ways, loosing me from woes, and shaking off all burdens, or
will drive me from here still in mid-pains, before seeing clear sky, before
putting drugs on the wounds, 993 ill-fated, longing for light after the last
night, then who could help in vain those who lament. There is a cure for
mortals, and last of all all bonds. Already my grey head, and wrinkled limbs,
have bent toward the evening of painful life. But I have not yet endured such
and so great a pain; not even when I cut the sea from the Pharian land to
Achaea, armed with raging winds, at the rising of Taurus, which sailors most
dread, wintry, and few have loosed the cables. There I for twenty nights and
days all lay in the stern of the ship, calling on God who rules on high with
prayers. And the wave foamed upon the ship 994 like mountains or rocks here
and there, much

fell within. And all the tackling was shaken, as the wind whistled sharp upon
the forestays. And the aether was blackened in clouds, and flashed with
lightnings, and was broken all around with strong voices. Then I gave myself
to God, and escaped the wild sea that fell by holy promises. Nor when all the
foundations of broad Hellas were shaken as it leaped, and no help of evil
appeared; but I trembled, since I still had a soul incomplete of heavenly
grace, when by the bath grace and the gleam of the Spirit is drawn for men.
Nor when disease filled my mouth with a violent stream, 995 and narrowed the
passages of breath, and the paths of life. Nor when, wrapping a twig about
with foolish mind, not willingly I tore the circular corner of the eyelid, and
made it bloody. And my light, as of a murderer, perished, and great grief cut
a bitter sin. Nor did I send up to God with my hands an offering of the
Spirit, not before at least, until I washed the woe with tears. For it is an
evil for one not pure to touch the holy, as for a weak eye to bear the fiery
sun opposite. And many other things I toiled; who could tell these things,
with which God, both wearing me and being kind, called me? But I have not yet
met with such evils before, as those last ones on which my wretched soul has
fallen; and it longs sometime to see a free day, having put off all, naked,
that it may flee the flames, 996 and the grips of the strong world, and the
monstrous chasm 996 of the dragon eager to gape around with his jaws whom he
may take. For my mind is a baited prey. Who will give a stream of a spring to
the head or to the eyelids, that I may cleanse all pollutions with channels of
tears, having wept sins as is fitting (for indeed the best cure for mortals
and for black souls is a tear, and ashy dust, and a strong sackcloth on the
ground.)? So that someone looking upon me may tremble, and be better, fleeing
the black plain of Egypt, and bitter works, and king Pharaoh, and may travel
toward the divine fatherland, and may not remain on the plains of rocky
Babylon, taken by the spear, sitting beside the banks of the river, having
laid aside all the instruments of song unshaken, tearful, but may hasten to
the bounds of the holy land, 997 having fled the slavish yoke from Assyria,
which formerly wore him, and may lay the foundations of the great temple with
his hands, which I, all-unfortunate, since I left, have never ceased longing
for. And a hard passion has poured upon old age, and I bend toward the earth,
increasing grief in the breast, reverencing both mortals and the immortal
King, downcast in garments and in heart, and voiceless, drawing the pity of
the King by misery, who being kind to all the lowly disregards the
overweening; as they say a traveler going down from the holy city of Solyma
toward the famous town of Jericho, evil thieves lying in wait harmed him, who
cutting him with unseemly blows, and stripping the garments that covered him,
with pitiless spirit 998 left him gasping; and swiftly travelers met him, a
Levite and a priest, and they left him with pitiless spirit. And somewhere a
man of the Samaritans came, who pitied him. And leading him he bound him, and
left drugs for the wounds, and a wage for the one tending; a great wonder, how
a Samaritan seeing him pitied him, whom the best did not pity! For I do not
clearly know what is hidden in this image, such mysteries as God covers round
with wisdom. And for these things may he be gracious. With such woes I have
met; and a robber of life, grudging me equally with souls, tore me when I had
come down from the good city, and stripped me of the grace of Christ, and made
me naked, as Adam formerly the beginning of dust and of the fall, whom tasting
sent down to earth, of which he was the race. 999 But pity me, O King, and
save from death him whom priests left, when they perceived him toiling. And
bind the wounds well, leading to the all-receiving house, and again send me
sound into the holy city, that I may remain steadfast there, and keep off evil
thieves, and the hard path, and wounds, and passers-by having a pitiless
spirit, plumed upon piety. I hear that two men, a Pharisee breathing great
things, as most excellent of all toward God, and a tax-collector worn within
the heart by unholy gains, went up into the temple; the one told each of his
fasts and tenths of the law, and measured

himself against men before him, and in words disregarded the tax-collector;
1000 but the other, pouring tears and beating the breast with hands, not even
looking opposite at the throne of the great God, the broad heaven, but looking
with slavish eyes toward the ground, and standing far off he prayed: "Be
gracious, be gracious to your servant," crying, "who is heavy with evils. Not
law, not tenths, and fair works will save me. Nor is that man a liar who
rails. And I too reverence the temple, not touching it with holy feet. But may
your grace, and your pity, drip upon me the profane, which alone, O King, you
have given as hope to wretched sinners." So they spoke; and God heard both,
and pitied him whom he perceived toiling, and dishonored the overweening. As
you saw, so you judged, giving me, O God, courage. This I am for you, that
all-wicked tax-collector; 1001 and as I groan heavily, I hope for some help as
a penalty. I beseech you, if ever a father, and a lady mother, honored you
with tears, and groanings, and vows, or allotted you some small portion from
possessions, or honored you with pure and holy sacrifices (for I have never
done even a worthy deed of you), remember these, and ward off; and send away
evil cares; and let not thorns choke me with their shoots, nor pull back one
hastening on the divine road, my defense, but send me unharmed. For I am your
servant, and your lot. And you alone are God to me from above. And a mother,
bringing me from the bowels, dedicated me to you, on that day when, longing to
set a boy, a child, upon her knees, she imitated the voice of holy Anna; 1002
"May I see a boy, and may you shut him within the precinct, Christ the King,
the flourishing fruit of my birth-pang." She spoke; and you, God, nodded, and
the divine dream followed, bringing a name to the mother, and afterwards you
gave the boy. And she dedicated me new to your holy things as Samuel, if ever
I was. But now again I am numbered with the profane, with the ravenous sons of
Eli your famous servant, who set upon the pure sacrifices with foolish spirit,
touching with greedy hands the divine cauldrons; therefore they also met a
hard end of life. But she allotted to you a portion of children with better
hopes, and she hallowed my hands with the divine Books, and embracing me she
addressed me with such a speech: "Already someone has led a dear son,
God-given, to the altar, 1003 hastening as a sacrificial victim to the
sacrifice, the best a good man, the offspring of late-bearing Sarah, and the
root of a race, which hope scored, and the promise of God. Abram was priest,
and the glorious lamb was Isaac; but I give you living as a gift to God, as I
promised. And may you fulfill for me the desire of a mother, who bore you
having prayed, and I pray that you be the best. This wealth I give you, my
child, a good thing here and afterwards. The last things are much better."
This was the longing of a mother. And I yielded to longings, being still a
child, and a tender soul received a new form of piety; and the seal of Christ
was guarded 1004 by nods, who openly consorted with his servant, and he bound
me with dear temperance, and fettered the flesh, and breathed upon me a warm
love of divine wisdom, and of the monastic life, first-fruit of the life to
come, of a body not needing a side that embraces its own, and with wheedling
words leading to a bitter tasting, but sending a holy longing to God, nor
cutting the whole one born of God for a woman and for Christ. Who stretching
me through a narrow and hard gate of the path toward a thin one with a better
escort, not passable for many, led a god from earth to God, made not born, and
immortal from death. With the image of the great God, and drawing a helping
body 1005, as a magnet stone draws bright iron. Woe is me! O soul that has
suffered mournful and unpaid works! O empty-boasting mortals! how truly like
thin breezes we who have come to be, or even with the back-wandering rushes of
Euripus we are turned about upon the earth, puffing in vain. And nothing among
men is like unto the end, not evil, nor yet good. And the roads are nearest.
Nor does the wicked know this at last, when he will cease, nor does virtue
remain steadfast for the good, but fear cuts short wickedness, as envy does
virtue. And for both Christ commanded the race of those of a day to be bent,
so that we may nod upward looking upon his

strength. And that man is best who draws a straight road, 1006 and does not
turn back toward the desert ash of Sodom, which through ravenousness of those
destroyed by a strange fire. And he flees eagerly to a mountain, and has
forgotten the fatherland, lest a word and a stone of salt be left behind. I am
a witness by my sufferings of mortal wickedness. Being a child, when there was
a little thought in the breast, following a shadowy word and good ways, I went
up toward the shining seat, going with unerring steps the kingly path. But now
when I have gathered speech, and I come to the bounds of life, as with
wine-wandering feet I, wretched, go sideways. And the battle of the crooked
creeper balances me, secretly and openly stealing the mind that has thought
good things. At one time I stretch the mind to God, and at another again 1007
I am drawn toward the evil flood of the world, and the boundless world has
harmed no small portion of my soul. But I, even if black wickedness has
covered me, and my enemy has poured dark poison before me, as a cuttlefish
inking upon the water, yet nonetheless I still look and see so much as to know
best who I am, and to whom I long to leap up, where I lie, miserable, and how
much I have slipped upon the earth or even under the earth into the wide pits.
I am not warmed by exhortations, nor am I stolen by words that are helpers to
sufferings, nor do I delight in measures of another's wickedness, as some best
man. For what grace is it for those being cut, toiling with iron, to see a
harder pain of others being cut? Or what benefit to the wicked is there of
worse lawlessness? 1008 For a good man might enjoy a better good; and so a
wicked man. For someone seeing is a benefit to the blind. But to delight in
evils is of a more complete wickedness. And if someone thinks one who is worst
to be best, it is a burden to me, and a hidden lament in the breast. It is
better being best to seem wicked, than being worst to have the glory of a good
man, to be a lying tomb for mortals, which, stinking within with rotting
corpses, flashes without with dust and pleasant colors. Let us fear the great
eye, which looks even under the earth, and the great depth of the sea, and all
that the mind of mortals hides. And time cuts nothing, but all things are
present to God. How could someone cover round his own evil? 1009 And on the
last day where shall we hide ourselves? Or who will defend? How shall we
escape the eye of God, when a purifying fire judges the works of all, feeding
on the light and dry nature of wickedness. This I tremble at and fear night
and day, looking upon the soul falling from God to the ground, and going
nearer to dust, which I was eager to flee. As when beside the banks of a
wintry river, either a pine or a plane-tree abundantly leafed, a neighboring
stream, breaking at the roots, has harmed it; of which first it shook all the
supports, and set it hanging on a cliff, and then, held by small roots, torn
up from the foundation, having broken it from the cliff it threw it in
mid-eddies, 1010 and leading it with a great crash gave it to the rocks; and
there rain and filth always pressing rotted it, and a most worthless fragment
lies beside the lips. So also my soul flourishing for Christ the King, the
unwearied enemy, rushing violently, cast to the ground, of which he destroyed
the most, and a little remnant thus wanders here and there. It is of God to
raise it again, who even when they were nothing fixed them, and afterwards
will fix those being loosed and will draw them to another life, meeting either
fire, or God the light-bearer. And if of God, even all afterwards? let it lie
elsewhere. Christ the King, even if hostile men 1011 call me a corpse and
without strength, and secretly mutter, laughing at my misery with moving
heads, do not leave me to be subdued under opposing hands; first, contend
again in heavenly hopes, and for me being quenched drip a little moisture of
oil, a help of a lamp for a thirsty lamp, so that of fire being raised, a
recovered light may come having bloomed again, and I may meet a shining life.
Second, give all burdens to the storms of wind, having thrown them from me
(and may you give a light breath), with which you have thoroughly subdued my
heart; whether punishing me of bitter wickedness, or with pains, such as
