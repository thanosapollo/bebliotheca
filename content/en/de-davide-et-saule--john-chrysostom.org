3. Yet I say none of these things, but I would say this: grant that the women
acted badly, and =54.680= that they are worthy of charges and accusation; what
is this to David? For he himself did not compose the songs, nor did he himself
persuade those women to say what they said, nor did he arrange that the praise
should come about in this way. So that if there was need to be angry, one
ought to have been angry with those women, not with the common benefactor of
the city, worthy of ten thousand crowns. But he, leaving those women aside,
went against this man. And if the blessed man, lifted up by that praise, had
envied the ruler and insulted him, and had spat upon his kingship, perhaps the
envy would have had some reason; but if he became more reasonable and gentler,
and remained keeping the rank of those under rule, what just pretext did the
grief have? For when the one being honored lifts himself against the


one outdone beside him, and continues using the honors for insult of that man,
the passion takes some occasion; but when he remains honoring him, or rather
serving him more greatly, and yielding in all things, what pretext could envy
have any longer? So that even if nothing else had been accomplished by him,
for this very reason one ought to have loved him more, because having received
so great an occasion for an attempt at tyranny, he remained keeping the
gentleness that suited him. For not only the former things, but not even the
things after these, which were far greater, lifted him up. What things were
these? David, it says, was understanding in all his ways, and the Lord
Almighty was with him, and all Israel and Judah loved David, because he went
out and came in before the face of the people. And Michal the daughter of Saul
and all Israel loved him. And he was understanding beyond all the servants of
Saul; and his name was honored exceedingly. And Jonathan the son of Saul loved
David exceedingly. Yet having attached to himself the whole people and the
house of the king, and prevailing everywhere in the wars, and nowhere missing
the mark, and receiving such returns in place of those benefits, he was not
thrown off his neck, nor did he desire the kingship, nor did he avenge himself
on the enemy, but he remained doing good, and accomplishing the wars on his
behalf. Whom would these things not have persuaded, even one made beastlike
and wild, to lay aside the enmity and be freed from envy? But that cruel and
inhuman man none of these things persuaded; but having been blinded toward all
of them, and having given his soul over to envy, he attempts to destroy him;
and while he was doing what? for this is the greater and more wonderful thing:
while he was playing the harp and putting down his madness. David played with
his hand, it says, as he did each day; and the spear was in the hand of Saul;
and Saul lifted the spear, and said, I will strike David; and he struck it
into the wall; and David turned aside from his face twice. Could anyone speak
of a greater excess of wickedness than these things? See also the things after
these. Just when the enemies had been driven off, and the city had recovered
itself, and all were sacrificing victory offerings, he attempted to destroy
the benefactor and savior and cause of those goods while he was playing the
harp, and not even the occasion of the benefit put down that mad and deranged
man, but both once and twice he hurled, wishing to destroy him. And such
=54.681= returns he was giving him in place of the dangers. And he did this
many times, and not in one day. But that holy man even after all these things
remained serving that man's affairs, and running risks for his salvation, and
arraying himself in all the wars, and saving his own slayer by his own
dangers, and neither in words nor in deeds did he grieve that wild beast, but
he yielded and obeyed in all things, and though he did not receive the
appointed prize of the victory, but was robbed of the wage of those dangers,
he never even in speech brought a charge, not against the soldiers, not
against the king; for he was not doing these things for a human wage, but
awaiting the return from above. And this is not the only wonderful thing, that
he did not demand the wage, but that even when it was being given he thrust it
away through a great excess of humility. For when Saul, moving and devising
everything, was not able to destroy him, he stitches the deceit through the
marriage, and invents some novel manner of dowry and bride-gifts. For the
king, it says, does not want bride-gifts, but a hundred foreskins, to be
avenged upon the enemies of the king. And what he means is this: Destroy for
me, he says, a hundred men, and this will be for me in place of the
bride-gifts. And he said these things wishing to throw him among the enemies
on the pretext of the marriage.

4. Yet David, judging the matters according to his own meekness,


thrust away the marriage, not because of the danger, nor because of fear of
the enemies, but because he thought himself unworthy of kinship with that man,
and he says these words to his servants: Is it a light thing in your eyes for
me to become the king's son-in-law? but I am a humble man, and not honored.
And yet the matter was a debt, and a wage and a repayment of the labors; but
he had his heart so crushed that after so many successes, and a victory so
brilliant, and a promise openly agreed, he thought himself unworthy to receive
the wage owed to him; and he thought these things while he was about to run
risks again. And when he had prevailed over the enemies, and had taken the
king's daughter, again David played, and Saul sought to strike him with the
spear, and he struck; and David turned aside; and the spear struck into the
wall. Whom, even of those who know well how to philosophize, would these
things not have carried out into anger, and if nothing else, have persuaded
him, looking to his own safety, to destroy the one plotting unjustly? For the
matter was no longer even a murder, but even so he was then about to go beyond
the measure of the law. For that law commanded to dig out an eye for an eye;
but this man, even if he had slain, would have been doing one in place of
three murders, three murders that had no reasonable pretext. Yet he attempted
none of these things, but chose rather to flee, and to fall from his father's
house, and to be a wanderer, and to run away, and, being distressed, to
furnish the necessary food, than to become a cause of slaughter to the king.
For he was considering not how he might avenge himself, but how he might free
that man from the passion. Therefore he withdrew himself from the enemy's
eyes, so as to draw off the swelling, and put down the inflammation, and
console the envy. It is better for me, he says, to be wret54.682 ched and to
suffer ten thousand terrible things, than for this man to be judged before God
of an unjust slaughter. Let us not only hear these things, but also imitate
them, and let us endure to do and to suffer all things, so as to free our
enemies from their hatred toward us; and let us not seek this, whether they
hate us justly or unjustly, but that only, how they may no longer be hostile
against us. For the physician too looks to this, how he may free the sick man
from the disease, not whether he drew the illness upon himself justly or
unjustly. And you too, then, are a physician of the one who has grieved you;
seek one thing only, how you may take away his illness. Which this blessed man
also did, choosing poverty in place of wealth, solitude in place of a
fatherland, toils and dangers in place of luxury and security, continual
flight in place of staying at home, in order to free that man from hatred and
enmity toward him. But not even so did Saul gain anything, but he pursued and
went about everywhere seeking the one who had done no wrong, but had been
wronged to the last degree, and who had done him ten thousand good things in
place of these; and without knowing it he falls at last into David's very
nets. For there was a cave there, it says, and Saul went in to prepare
himself. And David and his men were sitting in the inner cave. And the men of
David said to him: Behold the day, as the Lord said: I give your enemy into
your hands, and you shall do to him what is pleasing in your eyes. And David
rose, and took away the skirt of Saul's cloak secretly. And after these things
David's heart struck him, because he had taken away the skirt of the cloak,
and David said to his men: Far be it from me from the Lord to do this thing to
my lord, the Lord's anointed, to bring my hand upon him, because he is the
Lord's anointed. Did you see the nets stretched, and the prey caught in them,
and the hunter standing, and all urging him to plunge the sword into the
enemy's breast? Behold for me then the philosophy, behold the wrestling, the


victory, the crown. For that cave was a stadium, and a wrestling match
wonderful and strange was taking place. For David was wrestling, and anger was
boxing, and Saul lay as the prize, and God was the president of the games; or
rather the war was not only against himself and his own desire, but also
against the soldiers present. For even if he wished to philosophize and to
spare the one who had grieved him, it was likely that he also feared those
men, lest they even cut him down in the cave, as being a ruin and a betrayer
of their own salvation, and as saving their common enemy. For it was likely
that each of them, being displeased, was saying these things in his mind: We
have become wanderers and fugitives, and have fallen from houses and
fatherland and all the rest, and we have shared with you in all the hardships;
and you, having taken into your hands the cause of these evils, are thinking
of letting him go, so that we may never breathe from these evils, and in your
zeal to save the enemy you betray your friends? And where is this just? For if
you despise your own salvation, at least spare our soul. Do the things that
have passed not grieve you? do you not even remember the evils you suffered
from him? For the sake of the =54.683= things to come, destroy him, so that we
may not undergo greater and harsher evils. For even if they did not say these
things in words, yet they were reckoning these things in their mind, and other
things more than these.

5. But that righteous man took account of none of these things, but looked to
one thing only, how he might be able to bind on the crown of long-suffering,
and to philosophize a philosophy somewhat novel and strange. For it was not so
wonderful if, being alone and by himself, he had spared the one who had
grieved him, as it is now strange that he did this with others present with
him. For the presence of those soldiers became a double hindrance to the
philosophy. Often at any rate we ourselves, having judged to let go anger, and
to forgive the things done amiss to someone, when we see some people
sharpening and stirring us up, dissolve our own judgment, being persuaded by
their words. But that blessed man did not suffer this, but even after the
exhortation and the counsel he remained keeping his own vote. And this is not
the only wonderful thing, that he was not turned aside by the counsels from
others, nor that he did not fear them, but that he also prepared those men to
philosophize the same things as himself. For it is a great thing for someone
even to prevail over his own passions; but far greater to be able also to
persuade others to acquire the same mind as himself; and others, not
reasonable and moderate men, but soldiers who had learned to make war, and who
had given up hope after the many toils, and who were desiring to breathe a
little, and who knew that the whole release from the evils then lay in the
slaughter of the enemy; and not only a release from evils, but also an
acquisition of ten thousand goods; for nothing hindered, once that man had
been slain, the kingship from passing over to this man at once. Yet though
there were so many things sharpening the soldiers, that noble man was able to
prevail over all these, and to persuade them to spare the enemy. And it is
worth hearing also the soldiers' counsel itself; for the malice of the
exhortation shows the firm and unturned character of the righteous man's mind.
For they did not say: Behold the one who has put you through ten thousand
evils, who desired your slaughter, who threw us into incurable terrible
things; but since they saw that he overlooked all these things, and made no
great account of the things sinned against him, they bring him the vote from
above, saying, God has delivered him, so that, having reverence for the
judgment from there, he might come more readily to the slaughter. For are you
avenging yourself? he says. You are ministering to God and


serving him, and you are ratifying that man's vote. But the more they said
these things, so much the more he spared. For he knew this, that God had
delivered him to him, in order to furnish him an occasion of greater good
repute. And you too, then, when you see the enemy fallen into your hands, do
not think that that is a time of vengeance, but of salvation. Therefore one
must spare enemies especially then, =54.684= when we have become masters of
them. But perhaps someone would say: And what is great and wonderful in
becoming master and sparing? For many other kings too, often coming into the
rule, having taken those who had formerly grieved them, thought it unworthy of
themselves, in so great a swelling of rule, to demand a penalty for the things
done amiss, and the abundance of the authority became a ground of
reconciliation. But here nothing of the kind can be said. For it was not after
going up into the rule, nor after recovering the kingship, and having Saul
under his hands, that David spared in this way, lest someone say that the
greatness of the rule dissolved the anger; but knowing that if he were saved
he would attack again with the same things, and would throw him into greater
dangers, not even so did he kill him. Let us not then compare this man with
those. For those, having a secure pledge of safety afterward, reasonably
spare; but this man, being about to let the enemy go against himself, and to
save a foe for himself, not even so did he make him vanish, and this when he
had many things pushing him toward this slaughter. For the solitude of those
helping Saul, and the exhortation of the soldiers, and the memory of the
things that had passed, and the fear of the things to come, and that it was no
longer even judged a slaughter if he killed the enemy, and that after this
murder he would be able to go beyond the law, and many other things more than
these were forcing and compelling him to push the sword into that man. But he
yielded to none of these, but remained like some adamant, keeping the law of
philosophy unmoved. Then, so that you may not say that he did not even suffer
anything of the kind that was likely, but that what happened was
insensibility, not philosophy, see how, having grown wild, he endured. For
that the waves of anger rose up in him, and a great squall of reasonings was
moved, and he was bridling the winter by the fear of God, and his mind was
being pressed, one can see from what happened. For he rose, it says, and took
away the skirt of Saul's cloak secretly. Did you see how great a winter of
anger was raised? But it did not go further, nor did it work the shipwreck;
for the helmsman, quickly perceiving, the pious reasoning, made a calm in
place of a winter. For his heart struck

him, it says, and like a horse leaping and going mad, he reined in the anger.

6. Such are the souls of the saints: before they fall, they rise, before they
come to the sin, they are reined in, since they are sober and are continually
awake. And yet how great was the interval between the body and the garment?
Yet he was able not to go further, and even for this very thing he condemned
himself exceedingly. For his heart struck him, it says, because he took away
the skirt of the cloak, and he said to the men: Far be it from me from the
Lord. What is, Far be it from me from the Lord? In place of, The Lord be
merciful to me, he says, and even if I should wish it, may God never grant me
to work this, nor permit me to come into this sin. For since he knows that
such philo54.685 sophy almost exceeds human nature, and needs the impulse from
above, and having been all but turned toward the slaughter, he prays then that
God may keep his hand clean. What could be gentler than this soul? Shall we
still call this man a man, who in human nature displayed an angelic way of
life? But the divine laws would not endure it. For who, tell me,


would quickly choose to pray such a prayer to God? Why do I say such a prayer?
Who would easily endure not even to curse the one who had grieved him? For
many of mankind have been driven to this point of beastliness, that when they
are weak, and are able to do no evil to the one who has grieved them, they
call God himself to the defense, and ask him to grant them to go after those
who have done them wrong. But this man, making his prayer as if diametrically
and oppositely to all those, asks that he not be granted to go after him,
saying thus: Let it not be mine from the Lord to bring my hand upon him, as if
conversing about a son of the enemy, as about a genuine child. And he not only
spared, but he also composes a defense on his behalf. And see how
understandingly and wisely. For since, searching through his life, he found
nothing good, nor did he have it to say, I have not been wronged

being able to do this from the life, nor from that man's works, he fled to

saying? he says; that he is polluted and all-polluted, and full of ten
thousand evils, and has put us through the last things? But he is king, but he
is a ruler, but he was entrusted with our protection. And he did not say,
king, but what? Because he is the Lord's anointed, making him reverend not
from the honor from below, but from the vote from above. Do you despise the
fellow-servant? he says. Reverence the Master. Do you spit upon the one
appointed? Fear the one who appointed him. For if we fear and shudder at these
rulers appointed by a king, even if they are wicked, even if thieves, even if
robbers, even if unjust, even if they are anything else whatever, not
despising them because of the wickedness, but being put to shame because of
the dignity of the one who appointed them: much more must one do this in the
case of God. He has not yet, he says, released him from the rule, nor made him
a private man. Let us not then overturn the order, nor make war on God,
displaying that apostolic saying through the works: Because everyone who
arrays himself against the authority has withstood the ordinance of God; and
those who have withstood will receive judgment for themselves. And he not only
called him anointed, but also his own lord. And it is not of ordinary
philosophy to address the enemy with the names of honor and of service. And
this very thing again one could see, from what others suffer, how great it is.
Many at any rate cannot endure to call their own enemies even from the bare
and stripped names, but from other names that have much accusation, stringing
together the polluted, the mad, the deranged, the one out of his mind, the
ruin, and many other such things, they so address their enemies. And =54.686=
that this is true, I will bring you the example not from far off, but from
near, from this very Saul. This holy man at any rate, because of the great
hatred, he could not endure to call by his own name, but once when a feast was
being held, seeking him he said: Where is the son of Jesse? And he called him
so, both hating his name, and at the same time expecting by the cheapness of
the one who had begotten him to ruin the righteous man's glory; not knowing
that it is not the celebrity of ancestors, but virtue of soul, that is
accustomed to make a man brilliant and notable. But the blessed David was not
so. For he neither called him from the father; and yet that man too was
exceedingly cheap and thrown away; nor from the bare and stripped name, but
from that of honor, from that of lordship. So clean of all hatred was his
soul. And you too, then, beloved, emulate this man, and be taught this first,
never to call the enemy from the names that have been slandered, but from the
honored ones. For if the mouth practices calling the one who has grieved him
by an honored name and one that has


service, the soul too, hearing, being taught and being accustomed by the
tongue, will accept the reconciliation toward that man. For the words
themselves will be an excellent drug of the inflammation in the heart.

7. I have said these things now, not so that we may only praise, but so that
we may also emulate. Let each one then paint this history upon his own heart,
as if with a hand continually sketching by his reasonings the double cave,
Saul sleeping within, and bound as by some chain to the sleep, and lying under
the right hand of the one who had been wronged in the greatest things; David
standing over him as he slept, the soldiers present, and sharpening him toward
slaughter; that blessed man philosophizing, and putting down both his own
anger and theirs, and making a defense on behalf of the one who had done so
many things amiss. Let us not only write these things on the mind, but also in
gatherings let us converse with one another about them continually; let us
also stir these narratives continually toward wife, and toward children. For
whether you wish to converse about a king, behold a king; whether about
soldiers, whether about a household, whether about civic affairs, you will see
a great plenty of these things in the Scriptures. These narratives have the
greatest benefit. For it is impossible, impossible, for a soul turning about
in these histories ever to be able to be mastered by the passion. In order
then that we may not spend the time in vain, nor consume our life to no
purpose in untimely and superfluous chatter, having learned the histories of
the noble men, let us converse continually about these things, and concerning
these. And if one of those sitting with you should wish to make a discourse
either about theaters, or about horse-racing, or about matters that belong in
no way to you, leading him away from that subject, throw him into this
narrative, so that having both cleansed the soul, and enjoyed pleasure with
safety, and made ourselves meek and gentle to all who have grieved us, we may
go away yonder having no enemy, and may obtain the eternal goods, by the grace
and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory unto the
ages. Amen.

HOMILY II. That it is a great good not only to pursue virtue, but also to
praise virtue; and that David set up a greater trophy by sparing Saul than
when he brought down Goliath; and that by doing this he benefited himself more
than Saul; and on his defense toward Saul.

1. You praised David the other day for his long-suffering; but I marveled at
you for your goodwill and affection toward David. For not only to pursue and
emulate virtue, but also to praise and admire those who pursue it, brings us
some wage not small; just as not only to emulate vice, but also to praise
those who live in it, brings us a punishment not ordinary, and if one must say
something wonderful too, one harsher than that of those who live in wickedness
themselves. And that this is true, Paul shows in the things he says; for when
he had numbered every form of vice, and had accused all those who trample the
laws of God, he added, saying about those very men: Who, knowing the ordinance
of God, that those who practice such things are worthy of death, not only do
them, but also consent with those who practice them; therefore you are without
defense, O man. Do you see that he spoke thus for this reason, in order to
show that this is harsher than that? For to praise those who sin, in addition
to sinning, is far


greater in the account of punishment; and very reasonably; for this vote
belongs to a corrupted mind and one sick incurably. For the one who, along
with doing amiss, condemns the sin, will be able at some time by time to
recover himself; but the one who praises wickedness has deprived himself of
the healing that comes from repenting. So that reasonably Paul showed this to
be harsher than that. Just as then not only those who practice the base
things, but also those who praise them, share in the same punishment as those
men, or even a harsher one: so those who praise and admire the good are
fellow-sharers of the crowns laid up for those men. And this very thing again
one can see from the Scripture. For speaking to Abraham God says: I will bless
those who bless you, and those who curse you I will curse. This one could see
happening also at the Olympic contests. For not only the athlete who has the
crown about him, nor only the one who endures the toils and the sweats, but
also the admirer of the one who has won gathers not a little pleasure from
that praise. For this very reason I count blessed not only that noble man for
his philosophy, but also you for your goodwill toward him. For he wrestled,
and won, and was crowned; but you, having praised the victory, went away
having taken no small part of the crown. In order then that the pleasure may
become greater and the gain more, come, let us also render to you the things
left of the history. For when the writer had spoken the words by which he
declined the slaughter, he added that He did not give them to rise and kill
Saul, wishing to show at once both their desire concerning the slaughter, and
this man's courage. And yet many of the enemies, even those who seem to
philosophize, =54.688= even if they themselves should not accept to destroy,
would not choose to hinder those who were about to be destroyed by others. But
David was not so, but as if he had taken some deposit, and was about to give
accounts for it, not only did he himself not touch the enemy, but he also
hindered others who wished to destroy him, becoming a bodyguard instead of an
enemy and an excellent spear-bearer. So that one would not miss the mark if he
said that David had then fallen into danger more than Saul. For he endured no
ordinary contest, being eager in every way to snatch him from the plot from
those men; nor did he so fear when he himself was about to be slaughtered, as
he feared lest some one of the soldiers, yielding to anger, should destroy the
man; therefore he also composed such a justification. For those men were
accusing, and the one sleeping was being accused, and the enemy was making the
defense, and God was judging, and he ratified David's vote. For not without
the impulse of God would he have been able to prevail over those madmen; but
it was the grace of God sitting on the prophet's lips, and giving a certain
persuasion to those words. And David too contributed not a little; for since
he had so trained them in the time before, therefore at the time of the
contests he found them prepared and yielding. For he stood over them not as a
general of soldiers, but as a priest, and that cave was an assembly
thereafter. For as if he had obtained some oversight, he so made an address to
them; and after this address he offered a sacrifice wonderful and strange, not
sacrificing a calf, not slaughtering a lamb, but, what was far more precious
than these, dedicating meekness and gentleness to God, and sacrificing the
irrational anger, and killing the wrath, and putting to death the members upon
the earth. And he himself became both victim, and priest, and altar. For the
reasoning that offered the meekness and the gentleness, and the gentleness and
the meekness and the heart, in which these things were being offered, were all
with him.


2. When then he had offered this fair sacrifice, and had completed the
victory, and had left nothing lacking to the trophy, then Saul, the subject of
the contests, rose, and went out from the cave, knowing nothing of the things
that had happened. And David too was going out behind, looking thereafter with
free eyes toward heaven, and rejoicing more then than when he brought down
Goliath, and cut off the barbarian's head. For this victory was more brilliant
than that, and the spoils more august, and the plunder more notable, and the
trophy more glorious. For there he needed both a sling and stones and an
array; but here the reasoning became everything, and the victory was
accomplished without arms, and the trophy was set up without blood. He was
returning then not carrying a barbarian's head, but anger put to death and
wrath with its sinews cut out; and he was not dedicating these spoils to
Jerusalem, but to heaven and the city above. Now it was not women dancing who
met him, receiving him with the praises =54.689=, but the people of the angels
from above applauded, admiring the philosophy and the gentleness. For he was
returning having given ten thousand wounds to the enemy, having saved Saul,
but having stabbed with many blows the true enemy, the devil. For just as when
we are angry and boxing, and colliding with one another, that one rejoices and
is glad; so when we are at peace and of one mind, and mastering anger, he is
contracted again and humbled, as being an enemy of peace, and a foe of
concord, and father of envy. David was going out then, having a right hand
counterpoised to the inhabited world, crowned together with the head. For just
as in the case of those who box best, kings have often crowned, before the
head, the right hand of the one boxing or pancratiasting; so too God was
crowning that hand, which was able to bring out the sword clean, and to show
the blade bloodless to God, and to stand against so great a rush of anger. He
did not go out having Saul's diadem, but he went out having a crown of
righteousness; he did not go out having a royal purple, but he went out
clothed in a gentleness exceeding human nature, a garment more brilliant than
every robe. He went out from the cave with as much celebrity as the three
children from the furnace. For just as the fire did not burn those men, so
neither did the pyre of anger burn this man. And for those the fire,
associating with them from outside, did nothing; but this man, having the
coals burning within, and seeing the devil from outside kindling the furnace
underneath, from the sight of the enemy, from the exhortation of the soldiers,
from the ease as to the slaughter, from the solitude of those who would help,
from the memory of the things that had passed, from the agony of the things to
come (for this too, of brushwood, and of pitch, and of tow, and of all the
things that kindle the Babylonian furnace, was raising a flame more brilliant)
was not burned, nor did he suffer anything of the kind that was likely, but he
went out clean, and seeing the enemy's face, from there especially he
philosophized. For seeing him sleeping and remaining unmoved, and able to work
nothing, he said to himself: Where now is that anger? and where the
wickedness?

and where the so many magics and plots? All those things are gone and have
perished by a small assault of sleep, and the king lies bound, we having
neither deliberated nor done anything about these things. He was looking at
him sleeping, and he was philosophizing about the common death of all. For
sleep is nothing other than a temporary death, and an ephemeral end. One would
not untimely remember Daniel here too. For just as that man was coming up from
the pit, having prevailed over the beasts: so also this man from

nothing is safer than sparing enemies, nor more slippery than wishing to

after him lay naked and unarmed and deserted of all, as if delivered up as a
captive; but the one who yielded and gave way everywhere, and who chose not
even to go after him justly, without engines, and arms, and horses, and
soldiers, was taking the enemy into his hands; and what was greater than all,
he was drawing God into greater goodwill.

3. For I do not count that holy man blessed for this, not because he saw the
enemy lying under his own feet, but because having taken him into his hands he
spared. For the one was a work of the power of God, the other of his own
philosophy. How was it likely then that the soldiers would attend to that man?
with how much goodwill would they be disposed toward him? For if they had ten
thousand souls, would they not readily have given them all for the general,
having learned by deed, in the care for the enemy, the goodwill toward their
own? For the one who is meek and gentle toward those who have grieved him was
going to be so disposed much more toward those well disposed; which was the
greatest pledge of safety for him. And they were not only more well-disposed,
but also more eager against the enemies, knowing that they have God warring on
their behalf, who is always present with the general, and makes all the
affairs easy. And they attended to David thereafter not as to a man, but as to
an angel. And before the repayment of God he himself was gathering greater
fruit even here than the one who had been saved, and he took a more brilliant
victory than if he had sacrificed Saul. For what would he have gained so much
by slaying the enemy, as he gathered by sparing now? Reckon this then yourself
too, whenever you take the one who has grieved you, that to spare is far
greater and more profitable than to destroy. For the one who has destroyed
will often even condemn himself, and will have a wicked conscience, being
pursued each day and hour by that sin; but the one who has spared and endured
a little time, rejoices afterward and lives in luxury, awaiting good hopes,
and expecting the returns of long-suffering from God. And if ever he should
fall into some terrible thing, he will demand the repayment from God with much
boldness; just as this man too enjoyed all these things, receiving later from
God great and wonderful returns for this care concerning the enemy. But let us
see also the things after these. And David went out from the cave, it says,
behind Saul and cried behind him, saying thus: My lord, O king. And Saul
looked behind him, and David stooped with his face upon the earth, and did
obeisance to him. These things bring a glory not less than saving the enemy.
For it was not of an ordinary soul not to be puffed up by the benefits toward
the neighbor, or rather not to suffer what the many suffer, who, as of slaves,
so look down on those who have been bene54.691 fited, and lift up their brows.
But the blessed David was not so, but he was more moderate, even after the
benefit. And the cause is that he thought none of these successes to be of his
own zeal, but he dedicated the whole to the divine grace. Therefore he himself
having saved, himself does obeisance to the one saved, and calls him king
again, and names himself a slave, putting down the puffing by that posture,
and consoling the anger, and taking away the envy. But let us hear also the
defense itself: Why do you hear the words of the people, saying, Behold David
seeks your soul? And yet above


these things the writer said that all the people was with him, and he was
pleasing in the eyes of the servants of the king, and the ruler's son and the
whole camp stood with him in their minds. How then does he say here that there
were those who were slandering and falsely accusing, and sharpening Saul?
Because it was not after being persuaded by others, but himself having
begotten this wickedness from home, that he was making war on the righteous
man, and this too the one who wrote the book made clear, saying that from the
praise the envy was being begotten, and going forward it was growing each day.
For what reason then does he transfer the cause onto others, saying: Why do
you hear the words of your people saying: Behold David seeks your soul? Giving
him authority to beat off the wickedness. This at any rate fathers too often
do in the case of sons; and one of them, having taken his own son corrupted,
and having worked many evils, even if he should happen to have persuaded
himself that he ran aground upon the wickedness from home and from himself,
nevertheless often transfers the charges onto another, saying thus, I know
that the sin has not been yours; others corrupted you and deceived you; the
whole doing-amiss is theirs. For the one who hears these things will be able,
looking a little away from the vice, to return more easily to virtue, being
ashamed and blushing to appear unworthy of the opinion about him. This Paul
also did, writing to the Galatians. After those many and long discourses at
any rate and the unspeakable accusations, which he accused them of, toward the
end of the letter, wishing to strip away the accusation from them, so that
having breathed a little from the charges they might be able to come to a
defense, he says somehow thus: I have confidence in you, that you will think
nothing else; but the one who troubles you will bear the judgment, whoever he
may be. So David also did here. For to say, Why do you hear the words of the
people saying: Behold David seeks your soul, showed that others were the ones
sharpening, others the ones corrupting; being eager in every way to furnish
him a defense of the charges. Then making a defense about the things
concerning himself he says: And behold your eyes have seen today, how the Lord
delivered you into my hands in the cave, and I did not wish to kill you; and I
spared you, and I said: I will not bring my hand upon my lord, because he is
the Lord's anointed. Those men then slander through words, he says; but I make
my defense through deeds, and through works I strip away the accusation. I
have no need of words, since the outcome of the deeds themselves is able more
clearly than any word to teach who those men are, and who =54.692= I am; and
that the slander that came about against me is false accusation and a lie. And
of these things I call as witness not another, but you yourself who have been
well treated.

4. And how, he says, was that man able to bear witness? For when these things
were happening, he was sleeping, and he neither heard the words, nor saw David
present, nor conversing with the soldiers. How then shall we meet it, so that
the proof may become clear? For if he had brought forward as witnesses men who
were then with him, Saul would have suspected the testimony, and would have
thought they were saying these things to gratify the righteous man. And if he
had attempted to make a defense from reasonings and probabilities, he was
going to be disbelieved more greatly, since the mind of the one judging was
corrupted. For how could the one who, after so many benefits, was making war
on the one who had done no wrong, be able to believe that the one wronged,
having taken the one who had done the wrong into his hands, spared? For, as
for the most part, the many of mankind bring their votes about others also
from the things concerning themselves; as, the one who is drunk continually
would not easily believe that there is some man living in self-control; the
one attending to harlots thinks that even those who live orderly are unchaste;
again the one who seizes the things of others would not easily be persuaded
that there are men who even their own things


have given away. So also this man, once caught by the anger, would not easily
have believed that there is a man who has so prevailed over the passion, as
not only to do no wrong, but even to save the one doing the wrong. Since then
both the mind of the one judging had been corrupted, and witnesses, if they
were brought forward, were going to be under suspicion, it was managed that
some proof should come about able even to muzzle those who are exceedingly
shameless. What then was this? The skirt of the cloak, which he also putting
forward said: Behold the skirt of the cloak in my hand, which I took away, and
I did not kill you. The witness is voiceless, but clearer than those who have
a voice. For if I had not come near, he says, and stood close to your body, I
would not have cut the part of the garment. Do you see how great a good came
about from David's being moved at the beginning? For if he had not been moved
toward the anger, we would not have learned the man's philosophy (for the
sparing would have seemed to many to have come about not from philosophy, but
rather from insensibility), nor would he have cut out the cloak; and if he had
not cut it, he had no other place from which to make the enemy trust him. But
now, having been moved and having cut, he furnished an undisputed proof of his
own care. When then he had so brought forward a true and unsuspected
testimony, he calls thereafter the enemy himself both judge and witness of his
own care, saying thus: Know and see today, that there is no wickedness in my
hand, nor transgression; and you bind my soul, to take it. From this
especially one would admire him for his greatness of soul, that he makes the
defense only from the things that happened on that day. For this he hinted by
saying: Know and see today. I say nothing of the things that have passed, he
says; the present day is enough for me toward proof. And yet he had many and
great benefits that had come54.693 to be before to number, if he had wished;
for he was able also to remind him of the single combat against the barbarian,
and to say, When the barbarian war, like some flood, was about to sweep away
the whole city, you being cowered and afraid, and expecting to die each day, I
came forward, no one compelling me, but even you hindering and holding me back
and saying, You will not be able to go, because you are a child, but he is a
man of war from his youth; I did not endure it, but before all the others I
leapt, and I received the enemy, and I cut off the head, and like some rush of
a torrent I checked the assault of those barbarians, and I set the city that
was being shaken; and because of me you have the kingship and the soul, and
all the rest have with the soul the city and the houses, and the children and
the women. And after that trophy again, he had other wars which he
accomplished, not less than that one, to speak of. And that both once and
twice and many times when he attempted to destroy him, and sent the spear
against his head, he bore no malice, and that after these things, when he was
about to pay the wage of the former battle, he demanded bride-gifts, not gold
and silver, but slaughter and utter destruction; and not even here did he
refuse. For he had all these things, and far more than these and greater, to
say; but he said none of these things. For he did not wish to reproach him
with the benefits, but only to persuade him that he is of those who love him
and care for him, not of those who plot and make war. Therefore leaving all
those things aside, he brought forward into the account of defense only what
happened on that very day. So unpuffed was he and clean of all vainglory, and
he looked to one thing only, the vote of God. Then he says: The Lord judge
between me and you. He said this word, not wishing the man to be punished, nor
to receive vengeance from him, but to frighten him by the memory of the coming
judgment; and not only to frighten, but also concerning the things about
himself


to make a defense. For especially, he says, I have proofs also from the deeds;
but if you disbelieve, I call God himself as witness, who knows the secrets of
each man's mind, and is able to search the conscience.

5. And he said these things, showing that he would not have dared to call the
judge who cannot be cheated, and to bring the case in before him, unless he
had exceedingly persuaded himself that he was clean of all plotting. And that
the things said are not a conjecture, but that wishing both to make a defense
concerning the things about himself, and wanting to bring that man to
sobriety, he reminded him of that judgment, the things already happened are
enough to make clear; and no less than those the things that happened after
these are able to confirm this. For again having taken him into his hands,
after this salvation, when he had attacked him and wished to destroy him, and
being able to cut him down with the whole camp, he let him go, having suffered
nothing of the things it was likely he would suffer. Therefore also, seeing
that he is sick incurably, and will nowhere ever cease from the enmity toward
him, he led himself out from that man's eyes, and lived among the barbarians,
serving, being dishonored, being ashamed, furnishing the necessary food for
himself from toil and distress. And this is not the only wonderful thing,
=54.694= but that even when he heard he had fallen in the array, he both tore
the tunic, and sprinkled ashes and lamented a lament, such as one would lament
who had lost a genuine only-begotten child, calling his name continually
together with the child, and composing encomia, and sending forth a bitter
voice, and remaining without food until evening, and cursing the very places
that received the blood of Saul: For mountains, he says, of Gilboa, let
neither dew nor rain fall upon you; mountains of death, because there the
shield of the mighty was taken up. What fathers often do, both turning away
from the house, and looking with displeasure on the narrow street through
which they sent forth the son, this he also did, and he was cursing the
mountains that received the slaughter. I hate even the place itself because of
the dead who fell there, he says. Be wet no longer then with the rains from
above; for you were wet once badly with the blood of my friends; and he
continually turns about their names, saying thus: Saul and Jonathan, the
beloved and beautiful, not divided in their life, and in their death they were
not divided. For since he did not have their bodies present to embrace, he
twines himself about their names, soothing his own pain, as far as possible,
with these, and consoling the greatness of the disaster. For since it seemed
to many that for both to fall in one day was an incurable evil, he took this
very thing as a ground of consolation. For to say, In their life they were not
divided, and in their death they were not divided, was constructing nothing
other than this. For one cannot say, he says, that the child lamented
orphanhood, nor that the father wailed childlessness, but what happened to no

life unlivable for himself by the separation of the other. Have you been
warmed now, have you wept, have you been confused in reasoning, and have your
eyes become ready toward a letting-go of tears? Now let each one remember for
me the enemy and the one who has grieved him, while the mind is still boiling
with the mourning, and let him both keep this man while living, and mourn him
when he has gone, not toward display, but from mind and a genuine soul; and if
he must suffer something, so as not to grieve the one who has done him wrong,
let him do and suffer all things, awaiting great returns from God. See at any
rate this man both received the kingship, and did not defile the hand, but
having the right hand clean, he so bound on the crown, and went up upon the
throne, having a ground of praise more brilliant than the purple and the
diadem, the sparing of the enemy, the lamenting


him fallen. Therefore he is remembered not only living, but also having died.
So that if you too wish both here to obtain continual glory, O man, and there
to enjoy the unmoved goods, imitate the virtue of the righteous man, emulate
the philosophy, display through the works this =54.695= long-suffering, so
that having endured the same toils as he, you may also be counted worthy of
the same goods; which may it come about that we all obtain, by the grace and
loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father together
with the Holy Spirit be glory, might, honor, now and always, and unto the ages
of ages. Amen.

HOMILY III. That it is unsafe to go up into the theaters, and that it makes
men complete adulterers, and that this is a cause of despondency and of war;
and that David surpassed every kind of long-suffering in the matters
concerning Saul; and that to bear plunder meekly is equal to giving alms.

1. I think that many of those who left us the other day, and deserted to the
theaters of lawlessness, are present today. And I wished to know these men
clearly, so as to throw them out of the sacred porches, not so that they may
remain outside continually, but so that having been corrected they may return
again; since fathers too often throw children who stumble out of the house,
and bar them from the table, not so that they may fall from these things
through all time, but so that having become better by this admonition, they
may return with the fitting glory into the paternal inheritance. This then
shepherds also do, they bar the sheep filled with scab from the healthy ones,
so that having put away the miserable illness, they may return again with
safety to the healthy, and may not, being sick, fill the whole flock with that
illness. For this very reason we too wished to know these men; but even if we
cannot distinguish them with the sensible eyes, the word will recognize them
altogether, and laying hold of their conscience will easily persuade them to
go out willingly, teaching that that man alone is within, who has a mind
worthy of the sojourn here; since the one who shares in this sacred gathering
with a corrupted way of life, even if he brings the body in here, has been
thrown out, and has been driven away more than those shut out outside and not
yet able to share in the sacred table. For those who have been thrown out
according to the laws of God, and remain outside, have for the time being good
hopes; for if they should wish to correct the falls because of which they fell
from the Church, they will be able to return again with a clean conscience.
But those who have polluted themselves, and have been commanded not to step in
before they cleanse away the stain from the sins, then acting shamelessly,
work the wound more harsh, and the sore greater. For it is not so harsh to
sin, as the shamelessness after the sin, and not to obey the priests who
command such things. And what so great thing has been sinned by these men, he
says, that one should throw them out of these sacred precincts? And what other
sin greater than this do you seek, when having made themselves complete
adulterers, they shamelessly, like raging dogs, leap upon this sacred table?
And if you wish to learn also the manner of the adultery, I will not speak to
you a word of mine, but of him who is going to judge our whole life: For the
one who has looked, he says, at a woman toward desiring her, =54.696= has
already committed adultery with her in his heart. And if the woman who met one
of herself in the marketplace and was dressed as it happened has often
captured the one who looked curiously by the spectacle of the sight; those who
not simply nor of themselves, but from so great a zeal that they even

despised the Church, and because of this went up there, and spent the day, and
were nailed to the sights of those dishonored women, how will they be able to
say that they did not look toward desire? where there are also broken words,
and songs of fornication, and a voice having much pleasure, and paintings of
eyes, and rubbings of the cheek, and a robe put together more curiously, and a
figure full of much enchantment, and many other magics prepared toward deceit
and bait of those who see, and slackness of soul of those who watch, and much
dissipation, and the invitation from the place toward wantonness, and that of
the things that went before, and that of the hearings after these; the melody
through the pipes, that through the flutes and the other such things,
bewitching,

and softening the firmness of the mind, and preparing beforehand the souls of
those sitting for the plots of the harlots, and making them easy to catch. For
if here, where there are psalms, and prayers, and hearing of divine oracles,
and fear of God, and much reverence, often, like some wicked robber, desire
has entered unnoticed; how could those sitting in a theater, and seeing and
hearing nothing sound, but full of much shamefulness, of much stupidity, and
besieged through all things, both through ears and through eyes, become
superior to that wicked desire? And not being able, how will they ever be able
to be freed from the charges of adultery? And those not freed from the charges
of adultery, how will they be able without repentance to step upon these holy
porches, and to share in this fair gathering?

2. Therefore I exhort and beg, first having wiped off by confession and
repentance and all the other things the sin from the spectacle there, so to
hear the divine oracles. For what has been done amiss by us is not ordinary;
and this one could see clearly also from examples. For if some household
servant deposited into a chest, where the master's garments, costly and
gold-woven, were stored, a slave's garment full of filth and of many lice,
would you have borne the insult meekly, tell me? And what, if someone poured
dung and mire into a golden vessel accustomed to hold myrrh continually, would
you not even have laid blows upon the one who did this amiss? Then shall we
make so great a care of chests and vessels and garments and myrrhs, and shall
we think our soul cheaper than all these? and where the spiritual myrrh was
poured in, shall we throw in besides devilish processions, and satanic
hearings, and songs full of fornication? And how will God bear these things,
tell me? And yet the interval between myrrh and mire, and master's garments
and slave's, is not so great as the interval between the spiritual grace and
this wicked activity. Do you not fear, O man, with the same eyes both looking
at the bed upon the orchestra, where =54.697= the abominable dramas of
adultery are performed, and this sacred table, where the fearful mysteries are
performed? with the same hearings hearing a harlot speaking shamefully, and a
prophet and apostle initiating you? with the same heart receiving the
destructive drugs, and the fearful and holy sacrifice? Are not from this
overturnings of lives, and corruptions of marriages, and wars and battles in
the houses? For when you have been dissipated by the spectacle there, and
having become more slack and more wanton and an enemy of all temperance, you
return and see your own wife, you will see her more displeasing altogether,
whatever she may be. For having been set on fire by the desire in the
theaters, and having been caught by that foreign bewitched spectacle, you
dishonor the temperate and orderly partner of the whole life,


you insult her, you throw her about with ten thousand reproaches, having
nothing at all to charge, but being ashamed to speak the passion, and to show
the wound, which you returned having received from there, you weave other
pretexts, seeking irrational occasions of hatred, and overlooking all the
things at home, and gaping toward that polluted and unclean desire, from which
you received the wound; and having the echo of the voice sitting in the soul
still sounding, and the figure and the look and the movements, and all the
images of fornication, you see nothing of the things in the house with
pleasure. And why do I speak of the wife and the house? For you will see even
the church itself more displeasing, and you will hear with hatred the words
about temperance, those about dignity. For what is said will no longer be
teaching for you, but accusation, and having been drawn little by little into
despair, you will tear yourself completely away from this teaching that
benefits all. Therefore I exhort all of you, both yourselves to flee the
wicked sojourns in the theaters, and to drag away from there those who have
leisure for these things. For it is not a leading of souls, but ruin, and
vengeance, and punishment, all the things that happen there. For what is the
profit of this temporary pleasure, when continual pain is begotten from there,
and being stung by the desire both by day and by night, you are harsh and
displeasing to all? Examine yourself at any rate, who you become when you
withdraw from church, and who from theaters, and compare both days, and you
will need nothing of our words. For the comparison of each day will suffice to
show the greatness both of the benefit from here, and of the harm from there.
These things I have both already said to your love, and I will never cease
saying them. For we shall both console those who are sick with such diseases,
and we shall make the healthy more safe; for the word about these things is
useful to both; to the one, so that they may stand off, to the other, so that
they may not fall in. But since those who rebuke must also do this in measure,
having set this exhortation down as far as this, we will render to you the
things left from the former subject, returning again to David. For it is a
custom with painters, whenever they are about to work some likeness of an
image, that they sit those who are about to be painted with them both one and
two and three days, so that by the continuity of the seeing they may preserve
unfailing the accuracy of the form =54.698=. Since then it lies before us too
now to paint, not a type of bodily form, but beauty of soul and intelligible
comeliness, we wish David to sit with you today also, so that all looking
toward him, each of you may wipe off onto your own souls the comeliness of the
righteous man, and the meek and gentle, and the great-souled, and all his
other virtue. For if images of a body bring some consolation to those who see,
much more the images of the soul. And those one cannot see everywhere, but it
is necessary that they be set continually in one place; but this, wherever you
wish to lead it about, there is nothing that hinders. For having stored it in
the treasuries of the mind, wherever you may be, you will be able to look
continually into it, and to gather much benefit from it. And just as those who
are sick in the eyes, holding sponges and rags of garments that are bluish,
and looking continually into them, gather some consolation of the disease from
that color; so you too, if you have the image of David before your own eyes,
and gaze continually into it, even if anger should trouble ten thousand times
and confuse the eye of the mind, looking into that character of virtue, you
will receive the health whole, and the philosophy clean.

3. For let no one say to me, I have a polluted enemy, wicked, corrupted,

three times, and many times as this man; and if both once and twice and three

4. For let us not look at this, that God prevented David from being

who once and twice and many times hurled the =54.700= spear against his head,
and having become master to destroy, then spared, and this knowing that after
this sparing he will attack again, it is quite clear that he was slaughtered
ten thousand times in purpose; and having been slaughtered ten thousand times
because of God, he has many crowns of martyrdom, and what Paul said, that I
die each day because of God, this he also was suffering because of God. For
being able to destroy the one plotting, because of God he did not wish it; but
he chose rather to run risks each day, than having worked a just slaughter to
be freed from so many deaths. And if one must not avenge oneself on and hate
the one who plots as far as life itself, much more the one who does wrong in
anything else whatever. To many it seems that to hear badly, and to be harmed
in reputation by enemies, is more unendurable than all deaths. Come then let

bitterly, and condemn the things done amiss, you will gather a wage not less

that you may learn that as many things as friends do not benefit by praising
and gratifying, so many things enemies benefit by speaking badly, even if they

friends often even flatter toward favor; but enemies bring the sins into the
midst. For since by self-love we do not see our own doings-amiss, those men
often, having seen us more accurately because of the enmity, and having
reproached us, threw us into a necessity of correction, and the enmity has
become for us a ground of the greatest benefit, not only because being
reminded by those men we understand our sins, but because we also put them
away. For if the enemy reproaches you with a sin, which you are conscious of

and ask God, you have at once put away the whole sin. What then could be more
blessed than this? and what easier toward release from sins? But so that you
may not think we are simply leading your soul, I will furnish the testimony
about these things from the divine Scriptures, so that you may no longer doubt
at all. There was a Pharisee, and a publican; the one having been driven to
last =54.701= wickedness, the other taking care of righteousness at the peak;
for

do not insult the one speaking, but groan bitterly, and ask God for the sins,
we shall be able to put away all the doings-amiss? So at any rate this man too
was justified; for since he did not insult that man in return, but groaned
over his own sins, this one went down justified more than that one.

5. Do you see how much gain the insult of enemies has, if we bear it
philosophically? When at any rate both lying and telling the truth they
benefit us, for what reason are we pained? for what reason are we bitten? If
you do not harm yourself, O man, neither friend, nor enemy, nor the devil
himself will be able to harm you. For when those who insult, those who
confiscate, those who plot as far as life itself benefit us, and some weave us
a crown of martyrdom, as we showed, and others draw off our sins, and make us
righteous, as happened in the case of the publican, for what reason do we
become beastlike toward them? Let us not then say: So-and-so sharpened me, and
so-and-so led me on to say shameful words; we are the causes of these things
everywhere. For if we wish to philosophize, not even a demon will be able to
move us toward anger; and this is clear both from many other things, and from
the history itself set before us concerning David, which it is worth bringing
into the midst today also, having first reminded your love where we left off

David saying, Behold the skirt of the cloak in my hand, and all the other

endure to call him even from the bare

For those words of David, having entered into the mind of this man made
beastlike, worked all this change, which one can see from these words. For he
did not say, Are these your words, child David? but what? Is this your voice,

just as a father, having heard the long-absent voice of a child who has
returned from somewhere, is stirred not only toward the sight alone, but also
toward the voice itself; so also Saul, when the words of David having entered
cast out the enmity, recognized the holy man thereafter, and having put away a
passion, received a passion. For having cast out anger, he received good cheer
and sympathy. And just as when night is present we often do not even recognize
a friend who is present, but when day has come, even seeing him from far we
know him; so also it is accustomed to happen in the case of enmity. As long as
we are displeasingly disposed toward one another, we both hear a voice
otherwise, and we look at a face with a corrupted mind; but when we put away
the anger, the voice that before this was hostile and enemy appears gentle and
most sweet to us, and the hostile and displeasing face longed-for and
gracious.

6. This happens also in the case of winter. The running-together of the clouds
does not let the beauty of the heaven appear; but even if we have a sight ten
thousand times sharp, we are not

able to lay hold of the brightness above. But when the heat of the ray, having
spent time and having torn the cloud, shows the sun, it shows also again the
comeliness of the heaven. So also it happens in our case when we are angry;
and just as a thickened cloud standing before the eyes and before the
hearings, the enmity makes both the voices and the faces appear otherwise. But
when someone having philosophized puts away the enmity and tears the cloud of
the grief, then with an unbribed reasoning he will both see all things, and
=54.703= hear; which then this Saul also suffered. For when the cloud of the
enmity was torn, he recognized David's voice, and he says: Is this your voice,
child David? What This? The one that brought down Goliath, and snatched the
city out of the dangers, and brought all who were running risks concerning
slavery and concerning death back again toward safety and toward freedom, the
one that put down that man's madness, having worked many and great good things
for him. For this voice brought down that barbarian; for before the stone, the
power of the prayer overcame him. For he did not simply throw the stone, but
having first said, You come to me with your gods; but I come to you in the
name of the Lord Sabaoth, whom you have reproached today, so he sent it. And
this voice guided the stone, this threw the agony into the barbarian, this cut
out the daring of the enemy. And why do you wonder, if a voice of a righteous
man puts down anger, and takes down enemies, when it even drives away demons?
The apostles at any rate only uttered, and all the opposing powers ran away.
The voice of the saints has often even stopped the elements, and changed their
activities. Joshua the son of Nun at any rate only said, Let the sun and the
moon stand, and it stood; so also Moses bound the sea, and let it go; so the
three children quenched the activity of the fire through those hymns and
through the voice. Therefore also toward this very


voice Saul, having been warmed, said: Is this your voice, child David? What
then David? Your slave, my lord the king. A contest thereafter and a rivalry
takes place, who will honor the neighbor more greatly. That man brought him
into kinship, this man named him master. And what he means is this: I seek one
thing only, he says, your salvation, and the increase of virtue. You called me
child, but I love and cherish it, if you have me as a slave, only with the
putting away of the anger, only with suspecting nothing wicked about me, and
not thinking me to be a plotter and an enemy. For he was fulfilling that
apostolic law, which commands to go before one another in honor, counting
others as exceeding themselves; not as many, being disposed worse than cattle,
cannot endure even to address the neighbor first, thinking they are insulted
and lessened, if they should give someone a bare greeting. What could be more
laughable than this mindlessness? what more shameful than this puffing and
this frenzy? For then, then you have been lessened, O man, then you were
insulted, then you were dishonored, when you wait for the neighbor to address
you first. For what is worse than frenzy? and what more laughable than puffing

always begin the greeting, and let us not think this success to be bare and
cheap, to be54.704 come easy of address and fond of addressing. This thing

just as when it is fulfilled with zeal it has dissolved long wars, and has
tightened the friendships that are. Do not then neglect, beloved, the zeal

whoever they may be, both in the greetings, and in all the other things,
first. And if another anticipates you, display the honor greater again. For
Paul too brought this forward, saying thus: Count one another as exceeding
yourselves. So David did, and he honored first, and having been honored he
honored in return more greatly again, saying: Your slave, my lord the king.
And see how much gain he worked. For when David said this, Saul could not
thereafter bear to hear this voice without tears; but he wailed bitterly,
showing through the tears the health in the soul, and the philosophy which
David had put in.

7. What could be more blessed than the Prophet, who in a brief turn of time so
recast the enemy, and having taken a soul thirsting for blood and slaughter
threw it all at once into laments and wailings? I do not so admire Moses, that
from a sheer rock he brought out springs of waters, as I admire David, that
from stony eyes he brought out springs of tears. For that man conquered
nature, but this man prevailed over choice; that man struck the stone with the
rod, this man struck the heart with the word, not so as to grieve, but so as
to make it clean and gentle; which then he also did, displaying a benefit
greater than the former. Worthy of praise and of the greatest wonder is even
not plunging the sword, nor cutting off that hostile head; but it would be of
far more crowns also to change his very choice, and to make him better, and to
change him toward his own gentleness. This benefit is greater than that. For
it is not equal to grant life, and to lead into


philosophy; it is not equal to free from anger breathing an unjust murder, to
snatch from slaughter, and to snatch from a madness traveling toward so great

him for his own meekness, admire him more greatly for the change of Saul. For

calm from so great a squall, and to fill eyes that look on murder with hot
tears. And this is what is full of much astonishment and wonder. For if Saul

run aground upon last wickedness, and hurrying toward slaughter, in a brief

celebrated for teaching of philosophy would it not hide? And you too, then,
when you take the enemy, do not look to this, how you may send him away
defending yourself and washing him with ten thousand revilings, but how you
may treat him, how you may lead him back toward gentleness; and do not stand
off before, doing and saying all things, until you prevail by meekness over
that man's cruelty. For nothing is more powerful than gentleness. And making
this very thing clear someone said, that A soft word will break bones. And yet
what is harder than bone? Yet even if someone is so hard and unbending, the
one who uses him with gentleness will easily prevail. And again: A falling

moved, and of the being reconciled, you are more master than that man. For in
us, not in those who are angry, lies both the being quenched, and the anger of
those men being carried out toward a greater flame. And this very thing again
the one who said the former things made clear through a bare example. For just
as, he says, upon a spark of fire if you blow, you kindled the flame, but if
you spit, you quenched it, and you are master of both. For both, he says, go
out from your mouth; so also in the case of the enmity of the neighbor; if you

quenched all the anger. Do not then say, I suffered this and that, I heard
this and that; for you are master of all these things. For so, just as a

painful things as you heard and suffered, forget all these; and if you should
even remember, reckon these things to the devil. But collect, if he ever said

will quickly dissolve the enmity. And if you are about to bring a charge and
converse, first having cast out the passion and quenched the anger, so demand
accounts, so refute, and you will be able to prevail easily. For being angry

been freed from the passion, we shall neither ourselves ever send out a rough

by anger. So that also in the case of enemies, if you quench the anger, if you
cast out the enmity, nothing of the things said will be able
