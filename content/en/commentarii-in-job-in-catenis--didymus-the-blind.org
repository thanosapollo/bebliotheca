* Commentaries on Job in the Catenas

=39.1120= Ch. 1, v. 1. There was a certain man in the land of Ausitis, whose
name was Job. Of Didymus. And more loftily, Ausitis is interpreted counsel,
according to the Greek tongue, in which the righteous man dwelt. For the
quick-tempered man acts with lack of counsel; therefore both deeds and words
are born blind with him, as with dogs; but the righteous man does nothing
without counsel, obeying that wise precept: Do all things with counsel. And
the name Job is understood as endurance, and it is so, so that this man became
what he was called beforehand, or was called what he became. V. 2. And there
were born to him seven sons and three daughters. Of Didymus. And here the
catalogue of the children also is honorable in Scripture, and in harmony with
mystical words. For who does not know that the seventh numbering of the seven
gifts indicates the gathering, just as also the third utterance looks toward
what is more perfect and more divine? so that we may learn that the faith in
the Trinity is the fashioner of the seventh [ἴσ. hebdomadal] grace, as a kind
of head being set upon the virtues. V. 3. And that man was well-born, of those
from the risings of the sun. Of Didymus. According to the letter, then, he is
said to be well-born of those from the risings of the sun, because of the
kinship according to the flesh with Abraham; but according to the anagoge, the
sons of light, and children of God through works of light, these are those
from the risings of the sun. For the righteous man acquires nobility, not from
men, but from the light of the sun of righteousness, as it is illumined. V. 6.
And when the days of the drinking were completed, Job sent and purified them,
rising up in the morning, etc. Of Didymus. And according to the one who said,
From night my spirit rises early toward you, O God; and: O God, my God, toward
you I rise early, the blessed Job did not rise in the morning for any other
work, but toward God [ἴσ. for God], offering [ἴσ. intending to offer] a
sacrifice on behalf of the children, according to their number; that is, for
each, and in good order; for nothing is confused in a serious man. But also
after this, he used to offer a calf on behalf of all according to his own
manner, and accustoming them by the training of the children. Thus then he
also purified them after the drinking had been completed, along with the
sensible purification, also displaying the purifications that contribute
toward the mind. =39.1121= For he appears prophetically both according to law
and according to grace fulfilling the sacrifices. According to law, the
various ones; according to grace, the one calf that is for sin. For such a
sacrifice also prefigured the truth of grace, typing the one sacrifice
according to the flesh of the only-begotten Son of God. And if the calf
offered on behalf of all indicates the Savior who endured the cross for us,
examine it together and do not wonder; for Job was not uninitiated in the
sojourn of the Savior, he who says that he himself is the one who is going to
subdue the great sea-monster. V. 7. And the devil answering said to the Lord:
Having gone round the earth and walked about the under-heaven, I am present.
Of Didymus. And it is to be noted that the earth and the under-heaven are
different places; for while the earth is under the heaven, the under-heaven
places are not altogether earth. He goes round the earth, then, that he may
consider concerning which man he will make request; but he walks about the
under-heaven, taking pleasure in the spiritual things of wickedness, which
serve him toward the most shameful things. And one must not be surprised if,
being a fine spirit, he passed through the aforementioned places; for God also
did not convict him as lying. The wild and unloving character of his manner,
however, the wicked one indicates by so great a circuit. For they are the
words of someone mad and roaming about under stupefaction; as also elsewhere
it has been said: Because our

adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walks about, seeking whom he may
swallow. For he wars sleeplessly, and goes round, drawing some as friends,
demanding others as enemies; for he does not have authority over us. And
something of this sort his words mean: Since, he says, earthly and aerial
things being subject to me, Job alone stands against, I have come to request
him. V. 9. And the devil answered and said before the Lord. Of Didymus. And it
is proper to those who are hostile toward virtue, not to reckon the good
things of neighbors as genuine, but speaking as slanderers, that this one is
righteous, through inability to do injustice; this one is temperate, through
what is called foolishness; and that one does not have money and gains,
through unsuitability toward these things; and in general, no base person
accepts anyone's virtue as genuine. V. 11. But send forth your hand and touch
all that he has, surely he will bless you to your face. Of Didymus. The hand
of God is to be taken in different ways, either the punitive power that also
serves for this, which is also called a vessel of wrath according to the: Your
hand destroyed nations; or the guarding and sheltering power, as the: You
formed me, and you laid your hand upon me. Here then the, Send forth your
hand, has been said instead of, seize him in a striking way; and the, Touch
him, instead of, =39.1124= fasten upon, bring on grievous things. And we have
also an example of this: Do not touch my christs. For it does not forbid the
simple touch, but the daring against the saints unto harm. And see how
craftily, and with hypocrisy, he does not say, Permit me, but, You do it. For
he is conscious of his own weakness, and yet he rages and boasts, which is of
extreme folly. And you, consider for me also another vice, and a diabolic
blindness, that he wants to persuade the only good one to do evil; and with
very great cruelty, he is not content to destroy a part of his substance, or
some of the children, but wants to set him all at once destitute of all;
therefore he does not request the righteous man partially, but universally,
saying: Touch all that he has, strip him of every human hope, let him be an
incomparable image of afflictions and lamentations. For then both the
thankfulness and the good sense will be wondered at, being in no way convicted
by the changes. If he will not bless you to your face, that is, if he will not
then blaspheme you openly, and sharpening his tongue being bold before you, as
not bearing the magnitude of the calamities. Here too then, instead of, he
will curse, and will blaspheme you, the devil said, using the more auspicious
name, so that he might not bring forward the harsher word toward God, nor say
that you will be set opposite to God. V. 12. Then the Lord said to the devil:
Behold, all that he has I give into your hand, but him do not touch. Of
Didymus. For the, Behold I have given all into your hand, is indicative that
no one falls under temptations without divine permission. And that it may
become clear that he also makes the permission by measure, he adds, But him do
not touch. Whence it is clear that circumstances happen neither by fate, nor
of themselves, but from permission of God. And the devil went out from the
Lord. And according to some, if the devil had not become outside of God, he
would not have worked the things against Job. And it is more properly said of
this one than of Cain: He went out from the face of the Lord; which indicates
that he is without vision of God. For this one is always of those outside,
even if he came in pretence with those who stand by God. V. 13. And it was as
this day. Of Didymus. Consistently he also mentions the day, and the gathering
of the children, so that thus also their death may become credible, they
having died all at once at one time under the fall of the house. V. 14. And
behold a messenger came to Job, and said to him, etc. Of Didymus. And it is
worth considering how the things of the announcement, having happened all at
once, also from

the narration have the heaviness, and the inclination toward exceeding grief.
But even if they were sufficient to trouble, through the suddenness =39.1125=
of the hardship, nevertheless the righteous man was not troubled. It has been
said then in one of the psalms concerning the holy one: He will not fear from
an evil hearing. V. 16. While this one was still speaking, another messenger
came, and said to Job, Fire fell from heaven, and burned up the sheep, and
likewise ate up the shepherds, and I alone having been saved, came to announce
to you. Of Didymus. For the devil does not have authority to bring down fire
from heaven, but this has come to be by divine judgment, so that God who gave
him the authority over the things of Job may present to Satan that the blessed
one does not even care for human things, even if he weaves many devices. And
yet even if the holy one, having accurately learned the doctrines of the
truth, knew that the permission of the temptations had not come about without
God, still what was happening was sufficient to bring him greatest grief,
because of those being scandalized, as even of God going against him. For that
captors having come upon them drove off the cattle and slaughtered the
servants, the simpler could reckon that it happened by law of war, but not
from God; but when fire was announced as having come down from heaven, there
was fear lest some of the weak reckon that virtue is nothing wonderful, even
when the one who has it is punished by God. But even when this happened, the
holy one did not give way; whence toward the unyielding of the athlete it has
been said most clearly [ἴσ. to be made clear]: When this one was speaking,
this one also came. For that he may give the wrestler no refreshment of the
labors, but by the continuity of the message may trouble the righteous man,
and as a ship, by the succession of waves, may prepare him to become submerged
in the passions, he densely sends against him the bitter arrows of the
calamities. Himself the worker of the evils, himself the messenger, himself
struck the cattle, and himself announces the blow, and this in continuity, so
that the hearer may not even breathe. V. 17. While this one was still
speaking, another messenger came, and said to Job, "The horsemen made three
heads against us, and surrounded the camels, etc. Of Didymus. That then he
heard also concerning the slaughter of the children, and bore it manfully,
this shows both the manliness of the righteous man and his endurance. And it
is clear that all happened at once, the sons not even having heard the things
that had befallen, for Scripture also noted that they were dining at their
elder brother. These things were sufficient to bring to ecstasy and to throw
into confusion those who do not reckon the continuous turns of affairs and
alterations, those who try to hold fast things not their own, whose possession
is more grievous even than the first deprivation. For already some, having
encountered the suddenness of the change, have been in danger even concerning
the temperate mind itself, having been so captured by the passion as even to
have loved things worthy of hatred and flight. For the unexamined quality of
much good cheer measures out despondency for them. =39.1128= For what has not
come to possession is also inexperienced of grief; but what has come into
experience has as housemate even before the deprivation the fear of the
passion, and after the deprivation it bears sharp pains. For unbridled good
cheer, and an untrained mind, is captured by last lamentations. But the
celebrated Job was not such, the wondrous thing of endurance, the highest
limit of manliness, the head of piety, the great example of philosophy. For he
knows how to bear both good cheer temperately, and calamity nobly. For the
philosophy that went before is in no way convicted by the blows that come
after. And how would the passion have become unbearable to one trained in the
divine things, and well exercised beforehand in piety? For he who has been
trained to have wealth as common, is rich in the expenditures of those in
need, and does not fear the deprivation; therefore even when standing away he
is not distressed, but has rather made the passion a starting-point of
philosophy, and on the bright things

putting a bridle of the measured character, and not enduring to yield to the
grievous things, as also being guarded from above. For philosophy knows how to
be temperate toward either change; whence he neither grew insolent [ἴσ. grows
insolent] in the luxuries (for he is rich in philosophy), nor is he drowned in
the calamities (for he practices endurance), and the trial of the envious one
becomes for him a way of salvation. For the enemy escaped his own notice,
making a road for him from there toward the possession of the goods, from
where he expected to take him; through this also a housemate plotting is
convicted, and total destruction of children does not ruin piety, and fire
flowing from heaven does not knock aside the mind, being saved by God; and the
continuous messages of the evils, and the wailings from every side, do not
shake the endurance. For everyone who converses with God cares little for the
things below and that perish. And so much the more he is made akin to the
things above and that remain, as he is also driven from the present things.
For the spirit of wickedness, and the uprising of the surge, does not know how
to harm [ἴσ. to dip] a righteous soul; for God was guarding it, having
measured the calamity; the things around it having been permitted to the
tempter, unto conviction of temperate reasoning, but having kept the mind
impassible, with which he was also going to war, and having drawn up against
which, he mastered so many trophies. V. 18, 19. While this one was still
speaking, another messenger comes, saying to Job, Of your sons, etc. Of
Didymus. Not in vain is added, Of your sons and your daughters eating and
drinking, but toward intensification of folly, the devil also managing this,
so that the announcement of the things that had happened, becoming heavy,
might implant exceeding grief in the holy one. For if it had happened while
the children were sleeping, reckoning the fall as unperceived, he would not
have been so pained; and the, suddenly, is added, expressive of no one being
able to escape, the matter having happened thus. =39.1129= Ch. 2, v. 6. And
the Lord said to the devil: Behold, I deliver him to you; only preserve his
soul (and Theodotion, you shall not touch his soul). Of Didymus. And this is
toward our benefit, so that Job, set as a character and type of manliness, as
in a stele-writing, may exist as a mark of such virtue, both for those then
and for those later; which also happened. For it is possible to hear the
athlete and contestant himself saying: I know that I shall appear righteous.
And the Lord delivering says, Only preserve his soul. Which may be understood
thus: We often see derangements, and distortions of the ruling faculty, coming
about in some, through reasons which God who knows hidden things knows. Since
then the devil was eager to bring the righteous man to madness, and ecstasy of
mind, so that he might even wrap him unwilling in the words of blasphemy,
Behold, he says, I deliver to you him whom you requested, so that that one
also may be more well-approved, and you having been put to shame [ἴσ. you may
be put to shame]; only do not work stupor for him and distortion of the ruling
faculty. For this is not permitted you, so that with reasoning in health he
may display manliness in the passions, but touch fleshes and bones, as you
requested. V. 9. And much time having advanced, his wife said to him: Until
when will you endure, saying: Behold I still wait a little time, expecting the
hope of my salvation? Of Didymus. The devil having grown slack from so many
assaults, and no longer having what he should do, comes to the memory of the
ancient plot, and having drawn aside the mind of the woman, through her he was
trying to shake the athlete; and he weaves a third ancient snare for him, her
converse, perhaps through this he might deceive Job, as also through Eve Adam.
For much time having advanced, as not even he himself has been able

to shake the tower of piety, he persuades the woman to bring forward the words
of faintness. Ch. 3, v. 8. But let him curse it who curses that day, who is
going to subdue the great sea-monster. Of Didymus. And well has the, Who is
going to subdue, been said, indicating the mystery according to the
incarnation, as not yet having happened then, but going to happen. For it was
not fitting by naked divinity to abolish [ἴσ. that there be abolished] the
devil who has the power of death, but through the temple of the Word [ἴσ. of
the Word made man] that has come to be from the seed of the one deceived by
the devil. For it would have been a boast for the wicked one, if he had
subdued him by naked divinity. And the different names of the devil indicate
different activities of his, not substances. For he is called devil and
adversary, and wicked, and lion, and dragon, and serpent, and beast, and
sea-monster, because of his manner, as he works his vice differently; which is
overturning of the Manichaean doctrine. =39.1132= Ch. 4, v. 2. Has it not
often been spoken to you in toil? and the strength of your words who will
bear? Of Didymus. Either the, Has it often been spoken to you in toil?
indicates that you yourself also often used words toward some who had fallen
into grievous things, and you brought consolation to them in toil, that is in
the calamities and the afflictions; but now your words filled with faintness
no one is able to bear, but the roughness of your speech even raises toward
contradiction the one who wants to be very quiet. V. 10. The strength of a
lion, and the voice of a lioness, and the pride of dragons has been quenched.
The ant-lion has perished from not having food; and the whelps of lions have
left one another. Of Didymus. He takes a nature of irrational animals not very
useful to our race, but even plotting, and constructs that God destroys
everything unjust and arrogant. The irresistible lion then, he says, perishes,
whenever God wills; and the wild utterance of the lioness is quenched, through
which she terrifies the weaker of the beasts; and the dragons have been
humbled; and the ant-lion has failed, food not having been supplied to it; and
the whelps of the lions have left one another, they are not sent on into
successions. According to the history, however, since Eliphaz said that those
who sow the unfitting things will perish from a command of the Lord, he added
that both lion and dragon will suffer the same, God willing. He then who also
in the irrational animals maintains good order, much more upon men. V. 17. For
what? shall a mortal be pure before the Lord? or, from his works, a blameless
man? Of Didymus. You would not, he says, say this, that being outside of sins,
you have suffered these things; since it is not even possible for human nature
to live according to the exactness of the laws. For there are times when we
fall even into voluntary things, and often also into involuntary, through the
weakness of nature. This however, being plausible, if it should be examined
according to the thought, has the refutation. For if the one who falls into
painful things is afflicted through sin, and no one is pure, no one would
exist outside of painful things. V. 8. And I will call upon the Lord the
Master of all, who does great and unsearchable things, glorious and
extraordinary, of which there is no number. Of Didymus. Having blamed the
foolish, and said what he said about them, now he confesses God as provider
and fashioner, through the things set forth. And it is not unlikely that he
has these conceptions, being a man wise in human things. And he also has a
conception concerning invisible and visible things, speaking of unsearchable
things, and great, and glorious, both of rain and of water; which water if he
should distinguish from the rain, he would be speaking of that from springs,
and winter-torrents, and veins. He says then, that I do not glorify, nor
think, as the impious, but knowing providence, and knowing God as Master
=39.1133= of all, and of times, and of places, and of affairs, I call upon
him, thinking this fitting, who according to times works wonders, and

things worthy of astonishment, toward benefit of men, whose multitude it is
not even possible to catalogue in word. And he means the varied providence
that has come about according to each generation, both commonly toward all,
and privately toward each. For as it is not possible to find a number of men,
so neither to reach the providence of God. V. 11. Who makes the lowly into
height, and raising the lost. Of Didymus. He also passes over to the
providence according to men, and being disposed thus concerning God, that he
himself from height makes low, working rich from poor, rulers from subjects,
healthy from sick. V. 17. And blessed is a man whom the Lord has reproved; and
do not refuse the admonition of the Almighty. For he himself makes to be in
pain, and again restores; he struck, and his hands healed. Of Didymus. God
reproves, and brings on the admonition of the scourge, not that he may
destroy, but that he may save the one being trained; and you then, not letting
down from your mind, receive the reproof with thanksgiving. V. 18. Six times
he will deliver you from necessities, and in the seventh evil shall not touch
you. Of Didymus. And according to some the verses suggest that for as long as
someone exists under the world that has come to be in six days, he endures the
pains and afflictions in it; even if someone is holy, he has the necessity
from the body's need; but having gone beyond the world, and having come to be
in the hebdomadal rest, he will not perceive maltreatment; since also loss
does not touch the one who has an unloving-of-money manner. And the sense is
such: In the present age he will exercise you, and in the coming he will
glorify you. And there is no necessity to level the histories in sequence with
the contemplations. V. 26. And you shall come in a tomb, as ripe grain,
harvested according to season; or as a heap of a threshing-floor gathered in
according to the hour. Of Didymus. For this the gathered heap indicates, the
fulfillment, and completion, and that the perfect one is thereafter upon what
is in truth secure. Ch. ʹ, v. 5. For what? will a wild ass cry in vain, except
seeking the grain? and if an ox also will break voice, having the foods at a
manger? Of Didymus. For a wild ass does not let out a voice vainly, but being
in need of food; nor does an ox bellow, foods lying at a manger; so that you
should not blame me either, if I cry, according to your supposition, because
of the painful things, nature being weak. And the righteous man says these
things, that As an ass does not cry out in vain, or =39.1136= an ox, but when
the foods fail; so neither was I despondent, if I had not been driven to the
extreme of evil, leading Eliphaz toward not blaming the one in pain, if he
cries. For the righteous man must not be without perception, even if he bears
the afflicting things enduringly. For this is virtue, when someone receiving
perception of the laborious things, thinks above the pains because of God. V.
6. Will bread be eaten without salt? and is there even taste in empty words?
Of Didymus. And toward the mind, Every word is empty and vain, deprived of
virtuous action. Indicative of this is the, For the kingdom of God is not in
word, but in power, not in empty promises, but in word it works, the things of
salvation exist. V. 15. My nearest did not look upon me; as a failing
winter-torrent, or as a wave, they passed me by. Of Didymus. And those who
seem to be friends, but when a grievous time has stood they are convicted,
that they lie about the friendship, and put on a form, would also be called
[ἴσ. would be said to be] a winter-torrent and a wave, through the unstable
and unfaithful. For as the winter-torrent provides an appearance of a native
and ever-flowing river, and the wave being crested seems to the inexperienced
never to be brought low, but both the drought convicts [ἴσ. convicts] the
winter-torrent as being temporary, and the calm the wave; so also a storm of a
time shows those who play-act the things of friendship as much opposed to the
true friends. For the righteous, the steadfastness they have toward those

they love, they appear keeping this in ease, and in circumstance, not
distorting the definition of friendship; and no wonder, where they even extend
love toward enemies, being peaceable with those who hate peace; but the base
hide what they are up to a point, and are detected in the circumstantial
things, having stripped off the mask of love. This then was also happening
then to Job toward testing; and perhaps this also of the one who requested,
suggesting, and persuading even the friends, because of the passion, to
despise him. And absence of friends in the grievous things pains not a little.
Enduring this affliction David the holy one also sang [ἴσ. sang]: I was
considering toward the right, and I was looking, and there was no one who
recognized me. And again lamenting, he was saying: I was forgotten as a dead
man from the heart, I became as a lost vessel. V. 18. Those who were in awe of
me, have now fallen upon me. Of Didymus. Perhaps then he gently reproves the
friends, that Not knowing the cause of the visitations, you trample upon me,
daring to say such things, which you have not said before, because of
reverence toward me. And it is likely that many of the others also were
leaping upon him because of the calamity. =39.1137= V. 21. But you also have
come upon me unmercifully. Of Didymus. And when someone admonishes those who
have fallen into gloomy things on a blameworthy charge, he does not reprove,
being moved according to reason [ἴσ. of reason]; for this is not set forth;
but rather to exhort toward manliness, of bearing it nobly. For to say, Why
then did you sin? is rather to weigh down than to lighten labors. For in truth
they also stood over him unmercifully, from having wandered concerning the
cause of the visitation, and that they were trying to reprove the one not
subject to reproof. V. 21. So that having seen my wound, fear. Of Didymus. And
most caringly the holy one also conducts himself toward those not rightly
disposed toward him, correcting those also from the things concerning himself,
and receiving from their own conception the beginning of the exhortation. For
if everyone who sins falls into gloomy things, and the one who has fallen has
suffered this from sins, and no one is clean from filth, you also fear,
receiving the fear from our wound [γρ. affair]. V. 24. Teach me, and I will be
deaf; if I have wandered at all, tell me. Of Didymus. And this too with as
much gentleness as possible the holy one trains them, how they should use
words toward those in circumstance; for he says: Teach me, that is, You ought
to teach, not to reprove untimely; unless this too must be done toward
training, and the [ἴσ. according to] what is said by Paul: Reprove, rebuke,
exhort in all long-suffering and teaching. V. 25. For I do not ask strength
from you, nor will your reproof stop me with words; for I will not endure the
utterance of a word of yours. Except that you fall upon an orphan, and leap
upon your friend. Of Didymus. And this too is of manliness. For many often of
those in circumstances, hearing burdensome words, grow weary; but the holy one
received neither the reproof from friends at all, nor the words toward
faintness, not accepting them from the beginning as able to shake him but
showing the irrationality of the rebuke against himself, and the vanity of the
reproof, he says, that Your reproof will not stop me. For a word brought [ἴσ.
brought forth] without works is unstable and unfaithful; and otherwise also,
not coming forth truly, it still has the weakness; so that you also, using
such a reproof, do not stop [ἴσ. will not stop] the saying of true things, nor
will you find him quiet toward the words from you. V. 29. Sit then, and let
there not be injustice, and again come together with the righteous. For there
is not injustice in my tongue, or does my throat not practice understanding?
Of Didymus. And these are permissive and forbearing [ἴσ. warding off evil],
training one to speak with leisure, and attention, and examination; which is
indicated through the, Sit. =39.1140= This has also been said elsewhere,
Before you examine, do not blame. And great

also is the command, Let there not be injustice; for it is not a small thing,
not to stumble in word. And having exhorted them to be at leisure for the
examination concerning him, which he interpreted through the, Sit then, and
let there not be injustice; then he brings forward a word of good boldness of
speech, saying: For there is not injustice in my tongue; gently comparing [γρ.
moving] [ἴσ. condemning] them, from the conception they had concerning him, as
having suffered through sin; and otherwise hinting to them, that they do not
have toward an unintelligent [ἴσ. add. affair], so that from this he may wake
them toward the consideration concerning him, so as to use this prudently and
understandingly. Ch. 7, v. 10. For if a man goes down into Hades, he shall no
longer go up, nor shall he return into his own house, nor shall his place
still recognize him. Of Didymus. And others [γρ. Some] say that he says this
not counseling against [ἴσ. taking away] the resurrection; but first showing
the perishable of men; then, that the one who has once died will not return
into this corruptible life, nor will he be recognized doing the customary
things; so that to the, He shall no longer go up from Hades, one must join
the, He shall not return into his own house; so that he may say, that He will
come to life again, yet he will not return into his own house. But this
interpretation is plausible, and the former is more true, that the righteous
man was ignorant of the things concerning the resurrection; whence he is even
