* On Babylas, against Julian and the Gentiles

:CUSTOM_ID: h1

Of our father among the saints John, archbishop of Constantinople, the
Chrysostom, a discourse on the blessed Babylas and against the Greeks. 1 Our
Lord Jesus Christ, being already about to go toward the passion and to die the
life-giving death, on that last night itself, having called his own disciples
privately, both discoursed many other things and exhorted them, and among the
rest he said also something of this sort to them: "Amen, amen, I say to you,
he who believes in me, the works that I do, he also will do, and greater than
these he will do." And yet many others both became teachers and had disciples
and displayed wonders, as the children of the Greeks boast; but nevertheless
none of those ever either put such a thing into his mind or dared to say it.
Nor would the children of the Greeks, even if they should be shameless in
everything, be able to exhibit a prediction or a word of this kind lying among
them; but that apparitions of the departed and images of certain dead men have
been shown, many say that many wonder-workers among them have done, and they
say also that certain voices have been brought from tombs to some; but that
any of the men who lived and were admired among them, or those whom after
death they supposed to be gods, said anything of this kind to his own
disciples, none of them would ever maintain. 2 And the cause too, if you wish,
I will tell, why, while they shamelessly and with bare head lied in all other
things, they never dared to fabricate anything of this sort. For not simply
nor without some reason did they abstain from this device, but those
destroyers saw this knavishly, that one who is going to deceive must compose
things that are plausible and full of much cleverness and hard to detect when
they are to be found out. For it is the custom of the skilled hunters of both
fish and birds not to set the traps bare, but having wrapped them round on
every side with the bait accurately, thus to get the better of either catch,
each of them. But if they uncovered them and so offered them to be seen by
those about to be caught, neither fish nor bird would ever have come within
those nets; rather they would not even have approached them at the beginning,
but each would have gone home empty-handed, both the hunter of the sea and the
hunter of the land. Since therefore it was set before these also to net men,
they did not throw the deceit bare into the of the sea, but having fabricated
and composed the things able to catch the more irrational, they refrained from
going further with the lie, fearing the excess and being afraid that by the
immoderation of the second things they might also dissolve the first. 3 For if
they had said that someone among them had promised such a thing as our Savior
said to his own disciples, even those deceived by them would have laughed at
them as not even able to lie plausibly. For both to foretell such things and
to fulfill them with truth belongs to that blessed power alone. And if demons
also were ever able to make a small show for those deceived, yet it was when
the fountain of the light was not yet known to the many. And even then it was
shown from the very facts that the things accomplished were works of demons,
both by the rest of the deceit and by the sacrifices themselves. For to
command that their own altars be stained with human blood, and to order
victims of that sort to be slain by those who had sown such things for them,
what excess of madness would this not hide? 4 For they who are never sated
with our misfortunes and who know no bound or end of the war against us, but
rave immortally always, as


if it were not enough to fill their wrath that women and children should be
slaughtered at their altars instead of sheep and oxen, they invented a strange
lawlessness of man-killing and introduced a newer manner of misfortune. For
those who ought to mourn the slaughters of the slain, these they persuaded to
lead them forward into that wretched bloodguilt. 5 And in order that not only
the laws laid down by men might be dishonored, they also tore up the very
ordinances of nature from the foundations, driving it into frenzy against
itself and introducing the most accursed murder of all into the life of men.
For no enemies did all thereafter so fear as those who had begotten them, and
those in whom they ought most of all to trust, these they held most of all
under suspicion and turned away from. For those accursed ones strove, through
those by whom God had brought them into the spectacle of this world, through
these to cast them out of this gift, making those who had served toward living
to be also causes of death, as if wishing to show that nothing more had come
to them from the goodness of God; for they will not need other slaughters but
these of those who begot them. 6 For these things, even if some great wonder
had followed, not to say if somewhere something small also was shown and
worthy of no account and full of much deceit, but even if the things that
happened were great, these things which I have said are sufficient for those
not greatly out of their wits to show who they were who worked even those
things, how polluted and all-polluted and fashioning everything for the
overthrow both of our life and of our constitution. But our Lord Jesus
commanded no such thing to us, but being wonderful through the marvels,
through the commandments of the wonders he would no less justly be worshipped
by all and believed to be God. For this lawlessness he himself came and
dissolved, and the more wonderful thing is that from the wild and harsh
tyranny he delivered not only us who worship him but also those who blaspheme
him; for no one of the Greeks is still compelled to bring such sacrifices to
his own demons. 7 With such philanthropy has he always used our race, and of
the evils which the demons worked upon their own friends God made greater
goods for the enemies. For the demons compelled those who served and honored
them to become slaughterers of their own children; but Christ delivered those
who turn away from him from these commands, and the exemption from the bestial
service and this wonderful peace he did not shut up into his own only but also
brought out to those who were alien, showing that those are certain tyrants
and enemies and destroyers of our race, wherefore they also used those
attached to them as aliens; for they were aliens. But he was himself the king
and craftsman and savior of the whole race of men, wherefore he also spared
those who had been alienated as his own. 8 For the whole nature of men was his
own work, as also his disciple says: "He came unto his own, and his own
received him not." But to recount all his philanthropy is not of the present
time; but even if one should speak about it through all the ages, even if he
should have such power as it is likely the bodiless powers have, not even so
will he ever touch the worth. For how good he is, he alone knows, since he
also alone is so good. See then what things he says to the disciples: "Amen,
amen, I say to you, he who believes in me, the works that I do, he also will
do, and greater than these he will do." For he would not have imparted to them
so great an honor if he were not exceedingly and infinitely good. 9 And if
someone should dispute with us where this oracle had its fulfillment, taking
in hand the book which is called Acts of Apostles, and it has them not all but


not even of all of them, but of one or two, and these easily numbered, and the
shadows only of those blessed men, touching them and at the same time working
the health, and for many of those who were raving nothing more of the garments
of Paul was needed toward the release from the demon that moved them. And if
someone should say that these things are puffery and an improbable
marvel-mongering of fabrications, yet the things now seen are sufficient to
stop the blasphemous mouth and to put it to shame and to hold back the
unbridled tongue. For there is not in the inhabited world that is ours any
country, not nation, not city, where these paradoxes are not sung, which would
never have been wondered at if they were fabrications. 10 And of the word you
yourselves would furnish this testimony to us. For we shall not need to take
the credit of the things said from others, when you the enemies furnish it to
us. For tell me, why do the many not even know that Zoroaster and Zamolxis by
name, or rather no one except a few? Because all the things said about those
men were fabrications. And yet both those men and those who composed their
things are said to have been skilled, the ones to invent and work sorcery, the
others to overshadow a lie with the plausibility of words. But all things
become vain and idle when the subject of the things said happens to be rotten
and false, just as when it is strong and true all things again become vain and
idle that are invented toward overthrow by the enemies. 11 For the strength of
the truth needs no help, but even if it has ten thousand who quench it, not
only is it not made to vanish, but even through those who attempt to insult it
it rises brighter and higher, laughing at those who vainly beat themselves and
rave. For the things among us which you say are fabrications, both tyrants and
kings and sophists unconquerable in words, and already also philosophers and
sorcerers and magi and demons, have been eager to pull down, and "their tongue
was weakened against them" according to the prophetic word, and "Their blows
became a dart of infants." For the kings gained so much from the plot against
us as they received among all a reputation of beastliness. For through the
wrath against the martyrs, having been carried out into the harshness of the
common nature, they did not perceive that they were wrapping themselves in ten
thousand reproaches. And the philosophers and skilled rhetors, having much
reputation among the many, some for gravity, others for power of words, after
the war against us have become ridiculous and have seemed to differ in no way
simply from children talking nonsense. 12 For from so many nations and peoples
they were able to persuade no wise man, no unwise, no man, no woman, nor even
a small child, but so great is the laughter of the things written by them that
the books also vanished long ago, and most of them perished as soon as they
were shown. And if somewhere something also should be found preserved, one
would find this saved among Christians. So far are we from suspecting any harm
from their plot, so do we laugh at the great over-curiosity of their devices.
For neither if our bodies were adamantine and incorruptible would we have
feared, squeezing "scorpions" and "serpents" and fire with our hands, but we
would even have displayed it; nor, since Christ has furnished our souls and
our faith such, do we fear, having the drugs of the enemies. For if we have
been commanded to "tread" upon "serpents" and "scorpions" and all the tyranny
of the devil, much more upon worms and beetles. For so great is the interval
of the harm of these toward the plot of that wicked demon. 13 And our things
are such, and yours. No one ever warred against them, for it is not lawful for
Christians to overturn the error by necessity and force, but by persuasion and
word and gentleness to work the salvation of men. Whence no king of those who
think the things of Christ has set decrees against you


such as those who served the things of the demons invented against us. But
nevertheless, having enjoyed so great a quiet and having been disturbed by no
one at any time, the error of the Greek superstition was quenched from itself
and fell apart about itself, just as of bodies those given over to a long
wasting, when no one harms them, perish of themselves and, being dissolved
little by little, vanish. So that even if this satanic laughter has not been
completely wiped from the earth, yet the things already come to be are
sufficient to give you credit also concerning the things to come. 14 For when
the greater part has been pulled down in so short a time, concerning what is
left no one will contend any longer. For neither, when a city has been taken
and walls brought down and council-houses and theaters and walks burned and
all those in their prime slain, would anyone, seeing colonnades half-burned
and standing parts of a few houses and old women with small children, dispute
with the one who conquered and got the better of the greater part, as not
being able to work down what was left. But the things of the fishermen are not
such, but they bloom each day; and these not through some spaciousness and
ease introduced into our life, but through affliction and wars and battle. 15
For Hellenism, having been stretched everywhere on the earth and having
occupied the souls of all men, was thus later, after so great a strength and
increase, dissolved by the power of Christ; but our preaching did not have
those who warred against it after it had been spread everywhere and stood
firmly, but before it was fixed and planted in the souls of those who heard,
from the very preludes it was compelled to array itself against all the
inhabited world: "Against the principalities, against the authorities, against
the world-rulers of the darkness of this age, against the spiritual things of
wickedness." For thus, when the spark of the faith had been kindled well,
rivers and abysses flowed in from every side. And you know, I suppose, that it
is not equal to pull up a plant rooted for ten thousand years and one just now
set upon the earth. But even with these things being so, the sea of the
opponents still flooded the spark of piety while it was yet small, as I said,
and it not only was not quenched by this, but even becoming greater and
brighter it went upon all things quickly, dissolving and consuming the things
of the enemies easily, and raising the things of its own and lifting them to
an unspeakable height, although cheap and obscure men served it. 16 And the
cause was not the words of those fishermen, nor the wonders, but the power of
Christ working in them. For of those who worked these things, the one was a
tentmaker, Paul, and Peter a fisherman, and it would never have come into the
mind of men so cheap and low to fabricate anything of this sort, unless one
should say that they were mad and out of their wits. And that they were not
mad is clear from the things they said and accomplished and from those who
still even now are persuaded by them. They would never therefore have lied
such things, nor have boasted simply. For what I said at the beginning: one
who is going to deceive does lie, but he does not lie so as to become obvious
to all. 17 For if even when the facts have come out and so many bear witness
to the end, both of those who believed in those times and of those who from
that time sing these things everywhere, not among us only but also among
barbarians and those more made into beasts than they, nevertheless there are
some who, after so many proofs and the testimony of all but the whole
inhabited world, disbelieve the things that have come to be, and many so
unexaminedly and without inquiry, who at the beginning, having neither seen
facts nor having trustworthy testimonies of these, would have received this
faith in the soul? And what at all even lifted them to fabricate and compose
anything of this sort? 18 For neither by strength of words, for how, of whom
the one did not even know letters at all?, nor did they trust in abundance of
money, for they scarcely


had even the necessary food, both living from the craft of their hands. Nor
was it possible for them to be high-minded upon splendor of race; for of the
one we do not even know who his father was, so obscure and unseen was he, and
of Peter the father is clear, but he has so much more than the other as the
Scriptures have made his name known to us only, and this because of the son.
And if someone should wish to examine also country and nation, he will find
the one a Cilician and the other a citizen of an obscure city, or rather not
even of a city but of a village of the last sort, for he is from Bethsaida of
Galilee, a place so called, from which that blessed man was. And if one hears
also their crafts he will see that they have nothing great and august; for the
tentmaker is more honorable than the fisherman, and cheaper than the other
handicraftsmen. 19 From where then, tell me, from where did they dare to play
a part so great? Lifted by what hopes? Trusting in what? Was it the reed and
the hook, or the knife and the borer? Will you not go away and hang yourselves
somewhere, or even throw yourselves down a cliff, owing so great a folly? 20
But let us set, if you wish, even according to you this impossible thing as
having become possible, and the one coming up from the lake saying that the
shadow of my body raised the dead, and the other leaping out from the hides of
the tent-shop boasting the very same things as that one about his garments.
Who of those who heard these things was so mad as to believe bare words about
such facts? And why did no handicraftsman of those times ever say anything of
this sort about himself, or another about him? And yet if our things were
fabrications, it was likely that those after them would more easily lie such
things. For those men did not have, referring to others, a hope of getting the
better of the matter concerning these things, but those after them, looking to
those, would more readily have dared the fabrication, the example of the
former encouraging the second to be confident, as if no one on earth had a
mind, but all were out of their wits and mad, and it was possible for all who
wished to say and be believed whatever they want about themselves. 21 These
things are nonsense and laughter and words of Greek folly. For just as if
someone should be led on to shoot the heaven as if he would split it with his
arrows, or to bail out the ocean as if he would empty it with his own hands,
there is no one of the more urbane who will not laugh at him, and the more
serious will even weep with many tears, so when Greeks contradict us it is
good to laugh at them and to weep; for they attempt things far more impossible
than he who hopes to wound the heaven and empty the abyss. For neither will
the light ever be darkness so long as it is light, nor will the truth of the
facts among us be convicted; for it is truth, and nothing is stronger than
this. 22 That therefore the ancient things also, which we know by hearing, are
no less credible than the present and seen, anyone whatsoever who is not out
of his wits and beside himself would confess; but in order that we may take
the victory also from surplus I wish to tell a paradox that has come to be
even upon our generation. But do not be disturbed if, having promised to speak
a wonder that has come to be in our time, I then begin from an old history to
weave the beginning of the narrative. For I will neither stop only as far as
those things, nor will I speak old things alien to the new subject; for each
of the two holds to the other, and their sequence cannot be unyoked. And you
will know well when you have heard the facts themselves. 23 There was a
certain king in the time of our ancestors, and the other things, of what sort
this king was, I cannot say, but when you have heard the pollution which he
dared you will know also concerning the rest of the cruelty of his ways. What
then was the pollution? It seemed good to a certain nation of those warring
against that king to dissolve the war and neither to cut others nor themselves
to be cut by others for the rest, but


to be released from affairs and dangers and fears, and to be content with what
they had and to seek nothing more than what was; for it is better with
fearlessness to enjoy moderate things than, reaching after the more, to fear
and tremble always and, according to themselves, both giving to others and
taking from others, to live. 24 When it had seemed good then to lay down the
war and to live the unbusied life, it seemed good by some strong ordinance and
with safe bounds to bind this good, and having made covenants and having taken
oaths and given them, they attempted from surplus to persuade the king that
he, having set down his own son, and he was quite a child, as a safe pledge of
the peace, both furnish those who were formerly enemies to be confident and
bring out a testimony of his own mind as having made the peace with them
without deceit. And saying these things they persuaded him, and the king gave
over the son, as he himself thought to friends and allies, but as the end
showed to the harshest beast of all. For having taken that royal child by a
law of friendship and covenants, he trampled all together and overturned the
oaths, the covenants, the shame toward men, the reverence toward the divine,
the pity from the age. 25 And neither did the youth bend the beast, nor did
the justice that follows such pollutions frighten that wild one, nor did the
words of the father who had deposited him come into him, which that one,
handing over the son, perhaps enjoined, asking that he keep him in much care
and naming him a father to the child and entreating him to nourish and educate
him as having begotten him and to show him worthy of the nobility of his own
ancestors, and with these words putting the right hand of the child into the
right hand of the murderer and parting with tears. None of these did that
polluted one put into his mind, but having thrown all at once from the soul he
works that murder, the most accursed of all murders. For this pollution is
even worse than child-killing, and you are witnesses who would not have been
so pained, if it is right from my sufferings to guess also at yours, if you
heard that he slaughtered his own son. For then, together with the common
laws, the ordinances of nature also seemed to have been overturned, but here
many things have run together at once and become stronger than the natural
necessity by their multitude. 26 For when I think of the youth who had wronged
nothing, who had been given over by the father, who had been torn from the
ancestral palaces and had exchanged a life in a house of luxury and glory and
honor for the sojourn in a foreign land in order that that polluted one might
be able to be confident about the covenants, then worked down by him and
deprived of the splendors at home because of him and again slaughtered by him,
I suffer a contrary passion, both melting in the soul and swelling, the one of
wrath, the other of despondency working it. For when I think of that polluted
one arming himself and stretching up the sword and holding the neck of the
child and with the right hand with which he received the deposit pushing the
sword against it, I am torn and choked with wrath. And when again I see the
young one fearing and trembling and wailing the last wails and calling upon
the father and saying that he himself had become cause of these things and
reckoning the slaughter not to the one dipping the sword into the throat but
to the one who begot him, and not being able to flee nor even to defend
himself but blaming the begetter henceforth in vain and receiving the blow and
gasping and kicking the floor and defiling the earth with the streams of the
bloods, I am cut in the inward parts and darkened in reasoning, and a certain
mist of despondency is poured over my eyes. 27 But that beast suffered nothing
of this sort, but as if about to sacrifice some lamb or calf, so was he
disposed toward that polluted slaughter. And the child lay dead, having
received the blow, and the slaughterer contended with the


pollution, striving to hide the former things by the second. Perhaps someone
thinks I will say something about the burial, and that after the slaughter he
did not even give a little earth to the murdered one. But I will say something
else bolder than this. For having stained those lawless hands with such blood
and having worked the novel tragedy, as if he had dared nothing at all, the
shameless one, harder even than the rocks themselves, hastened toward the
church of God. 28 And perhaps some wonder how he was not struck with a
god-sent blow, daring such things, or how God did not send a thunderbolt upon
him from above and before the entrance burn the shameless face with the
thunderbolt. And I, if some have taken this thought, praise them and wonder at
their fire, but I say that not a little is lacking from their wonder and from
the praises. For they had a just indignation both for the child cut down not
in justice and for the laws of God so boldly insulted, but boiling with wrath
they did not perceive as much as they ought to have seen. For of this just
thing another law much higher lies in the heavens. 29 What then is the law?
Not to bring the punishment upon those who have sinned at their heels, but to
give to the one who has offended times and a term to strip off what was
offended, and through repentance to be equaled with those who have worked
nothing terrible; which God then also displayed toward that wretched one. But
nothing more came to be, but he remained uncorrected. And the philanthropic
one, having foreknown this also, not even so overlooked him nor left off doing
the things from himself, but he himself both looked upon him when he was sick
and did the things toward health, and that one did not wish to receive the
drug but even cut down the physician sent for this. And the drug and the
manner of the healing was of this sort. 30 It happened at that time at which
this harsh and pitiable drama was dared that a certain great and wonderful
man, if indeed one must call him a man, was caring for the flock among us;
Babylas was his name. This man then, having been entrusted by the grace of the
Spirit with the church of Christ here, I would not say that he outstripped
Elijah and the zealot of that one, John, lest the thing said be even too
burdensome, but he arrived so that he was not even left behind at all from the
freedom of those noble men. For not a tetrarch of a few cities nor a king of
one nation, but the one holding the greatest part of all the inhabited world,
this very man-killer, possessing many nations and many cities and an infinite
army and being fearful from every side both from the boldness of his ways, as
a cheap slave and worth nothing, so he cast him out of the church with such
undisturbedness and fearlessness as a shepherd would keep off from the flock a
sheep filled with theft and diseased, hindering the disease of the sick one
from passing over to the rest. 31 And these things he did, confirming in deed
the word of the Savior that only "he is a slave who does the sin," even if he
has ten thousand crowns upon his head, even if he seems to rule all the men
upon the earth, and he who is conscious of nothing wicked in himself, even if
he is numbered in the rank of subjects, is more kingly than all kings. At once
then the one ruled ordered the ruler, and the subject judged the one who held
all, and he bore the vote that condemned him. And you, hearing these things,
do not pass by what was said. For even this itself by itself, announced, that
one of those under his rule drove the king from the porches of the church, is
sufficient to raise and to strike the souls of those who hear. 32 And if you
wish to learn the whole wonder accurately, do not attend to the bare word but
sketch the spear-bearers, the shield-bearers, the generals, the rulers, those
turning about in the royal courts, those stationed over the cities


the puffery of those going before, of those following, of those swaggering,
the multitude, all the rest of the attendance. Then him in the midst, stepping
on with much pride, and appearing more august from the garments and the purple
and the stones scattered everywhere upon the right hand upon the joining of
the cloak, of those flashing from the diadem from the head itself. 33 And do
not stop the image even as far as this, but stretch it also upon the servant
of God, the blessed Babylas, and the humble figure and upon the private robe
and the crushed soul and the mind released from boldness, and having thus
drawn both and set them against each other, then you will know the wonder
well; or rather not even so will you take hold of the accuracy itself. For
that freedom of speech no word nor sight would be able to present, but the
experience only and the use of it. And the stand of the soul of the noble one,
that one alone would be able to know well who has been able to arrive at the
same peak of freedom of speech with him. For how did the old man approach? How
did he cut through the spear-bearers? How did he open the mouth? How did he
speak? How did he rebuke? How did he press the right hand into the breast
still inflamed with wrath and boiling with the murder? How did he thrust away
the man-killer? Nothing of the things being done struck him or knocked him
from the purpose. O unstruck soul and high understanding, o heavenly wits and
angelic stand. For as if seeing all that display painted on a wall, so
undisturbedly did the noble man do all things. 34 For he had been educated
from the divine doctrines that a shadow and a dream and things more worthless
than these are all the affairs of the world. Therefore none of these made him
cower, but even rather they gave him confidence. For that sight of the things
seen sent the understanding on to the king above, who sits upon the Cherubim
and looks upon abysses, upon the throne the glorious and high, upon the
heavenly army, upon the myriads of the angels, upon the thousands of the
archangels, upon the fearful tribunal, upon the unbribable court, upon the
river of fire, upon the judge himself. For this reason, having transferred his
whole self from the earth into heaven, as standing beside that judge and
hearing him commanding to thrust out the accursed and polluted one from the
sacred flock, so he cast him out and marked him off from the rest of the sheep
and turned to nothing of the things seen and seeming to be fearful, but having
thrust him away very manfully and nobly he stood by the laws of God that were
being tyrannized. 35 And yet with how much freedom of speech toward the rest
is he likely to have used? For he who dealt with the ruler with such
authority, whom of the rest would he have feared? I guess concerning that man,
or rather I do not guess but I believe that he neither did nor said anything
ever toward favor, nor toward hatred, but against fear and against the
flattery stronger than this and against the other such things, and many are
the human things, he stood nobly and manfully and did not even a little
corrupt the right judgment. For if "the clothing of a man and the laughter of
teeth and the step of a foot announce the things about him," much more are so
many achievements sufficient to display to us the virtue of the rest of the
life. For one must wonder at him not only upon the freedom of speech but also
upon stretching the freedom of speech as far as so much and upon adding
nothing more to it again. 36 For such is the wisdom of God, allowing one to
contend neither deficiently nor superfluously but everywhere keeping the due
measure. And yet it was possible, if he wished, even to go further. For to one
who had forbidden life, and he would not even have approached at the beginning
if he had not armed himself with such reasonings, it was possible with
authority to do all things


and to wash the king with insults and to take down the diadem from the head
and to stretch blows into the face when he pressed the right hand into the
breast. But he did none of these things; for he had been seasoned in the soul
with the spiritual salt, wherefore he did nothing vainly and idly, but all
things with a right judgment of reasonings and with a healthy constitution. 37
Not as the wise among the Greeks, who never with due measure, but everywhere
so to speak either more or less than what is needed, use freedom of speech, in
order that they may nowhere take a reputation of courage, but everywhere of
