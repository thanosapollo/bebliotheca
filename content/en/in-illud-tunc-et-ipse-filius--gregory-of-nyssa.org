OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, ON THE SAYING, THEN EVEN THE SON HIMSELF WILL BE
SUBJECTED TO HIM WHO SUBJECTED ALL THINGS TO HIM All the oracles of the Lord
are oracles both holy and pure, as the prophet says, whenever, after the
likeness of the purification that comes about concerning silver in fire, the
mind, having been cleansed of every heretical assumption, has the proper and
according-to-nature radiance of the truth of the oracles. And before all I
think it necessary to bear witness to the doctrines of holy Paul every
radiance and purity, because having been initiated in paradise into the
knowledge of the unutterable things and having Christ speaking in himself he
uttered such things as it is likely that one trained from such a school should
utter, under the Word as both leader and teacher.

4 But since the wicked hucksters attempt to make the divine silver unapproved,
darkening the radiance of the Word by the mixture of heretical and counterfeit
thoughts, and the mysterious thoughts of the apostle, either not understanding
them or taking them wickedly toward what seems good to them, they drag them
into advocacy of their wickedness, saying toward the overthrow of the glory of
the only-begotten God that the apostolic word contributes to them which says
that Then the Son will be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, as
though such a phrase indicated some slavish lowliness; for this reason it
appeared necessary to examine with care the account concerning this, so as to
show the apostolic silver truly pure, separated from every filthy and
heretical notion and unmixed. We have come to know, then, in the usage of holy
scripture that such a voice is many-signifying and is not always fitted to the
same thoughts, but now signifying this and again indicating another, as:
Servants, he says, be subjected to your own masters; and concerning the
irrational nature, that it has been subjected to man by God, as the prophet
says: You have subjected all things under his feet; and concerning those
subdued through war he says: He subjected 5 peoples to us and nations under
our feet; and again having mentioned those who are saved through knowledge he
says as from the person of God: Foreigners were subjected to me. To which the
thing examined by us in the sixty-first psalm through Not to God will my soul
be subjected seems somehow akin; and upon all these that which is brought
forward against us by the enemies from the letter to the Corinthians, that
Then the Son himself will be subjected to him who subjected all things to him.

Since then the signification of this voice is carried upon many thoughts, it
would be well, having distinguished each of these by itself, to recognize
toward which signification of subjection the saying of the apostle is properly
related. We say then that upon those subdued through war by the dominion of
those who have prevailed, the signification of subjection indicates the bowing
unwillingly and by constraint to the victors; for if some power should come to
the captives, showing a hope of prevailing over those who have mastered them,
they raise themselves again against those who have prevailed, judging it an
outrage and a reproach to have been subjected to the enemies. But the
irrational things are under the hand of the rational in another way, by having
the nature lacking to them of the 6 greatest of goods, that is of reason, so
that there is necessity that what is lesser be subjected to what has the
advantage according to the good lot of nature. And those held in the yoke of
slavery through some lawful sequence, even if they have the equal honor in
nature, yet being unable to hold out against the law,


they receive the rank of subjects, being driven toward subjection by the
inexorable of necessity. But the aim of the subjection that comes about for us
toward God is salvation, as we have learned from the prophecy which says: Be
subjected to God, my soul; for from him is my salvation.

Whenever then there is brought forward against us by the opponents the voice
of the apostle which says that the Son will be subjected to the Father, it
would be consistent, according to the distinguished signification of such a
voice, to ask them, looking toward which signification of subjection they
suppose they must fit this voice to the only-begotten God. But it is clear
that they will say they understand the subjection of the Son according to none
of the ways that have been spoken; for neither being an enemy has he become
under the hand through war, so that again the uprising against the one who
holds power should be for him through hope and zeal; nor as one of the
irrational things does the Word have subjection necessary in the nature
through lack of the good, as sheep and cattle and oxen have toward man; but
neither according to the likeness of slaves bought with silver or born in the
house, enslaved by law, does he wait through goodwill or grace sometime to
become free of the 7 yoke of slavery; but neither according to the aim of
salvation would anyone say that the only-begotten God is subjected to the
Father, as through this, after the likeness of men, to procure salvation for
himself from God. For upon the changeable nature which comes to be in the good
through participation, subjection toward God is necessary because from there
the communion of goods comes to be for us; but in the unchangeable and
unalterable power subjection has no place, in which every good name and
thought is contemplated, the eternal, the incorruptible, the blessed, that
which is always in the same way, that which can become neither better nor
worse; for it neither admits addition in the good nor the inclination toward
the worse; <for> that which gushes forth salvation for the others is not
itself in want of that which saves.

What signification of subjection then will they reasonably say must be
understood of him properly? For all the things examined were found far from
being both thought and said properly of the only-begotten God. And if it is
necessary to add also that form of subjection which the gospel according to
Luke says, that He was subjected to his parents, having come forward to the
twelfth year, the Lord, not even that would fit to be said of the pre-eternal
and true <Son> toward his true Father. For there he who has been tried in all
things according to likeness without sin accepted also to come forward through
the ages of 8 our nature, and just as having become a child he received the
infant food, eating butter and honey, so also having come forward to boyhood
he did not refuse what was fitting and proper to such an age, having become a
type of good order for life. For since upon the other men the mind in such
things is incomplete and youth has need of the leading-by-the-hand toward the
better that comes about through those more complete, for this reason the
twelve-year-old is subjected to the mother, that he may show that what is
completed through progress, before it arrives at the complete, well accepts
subjection as a leading-by-the-hand toward the good. But he who is always
complete in every good and can accept upon himself neither progress nor
diminution, because of the unneediness of his nature and its undiminished
character, for the sake of what he is subjected they would not be able to say
who say all things without consideration. For that, associating through flesh
with human nature, he was legislating in the childish age, through the things
he did, subjection for youth, is clear from this, that having come forward to
the completeness of age he no longer looked toward the authority of the
mother.

For when she was urging him at Cana of Galilee to show the power in what was
lacking to the feast of the wedding and to grant the need of wine to the
banquet, he did not refuse to furnish the grace to those in need, but the
maternal


counsel, as no longer being brought to him according to season, he rejected,
saying: What to me and to you, woman? Do you wish to preside over this age of
mine also? My hour has not yet come which furnishes to the age the self-ruling
and autonomous? 9 If then in the life through flesh the fitting measure of age
shakes off the subjection of her who bore him, what place subjection has upon
him who is master in his dominion of the age no one would be able to say. For
it is proper to the divine and blessed life to remain always in the same and
not to admit the change from alteration. Since then the Word who is in the
beginning, the only-begotten God, is alien to every progress and alteration,
how does what he is not now become after these things? For the apostle does
not speak as of the Son being always subjected, but as of one about to be
subjected toward the end of the completion of the whole.

And yet if subjection is a good and worthy to be said concerning God, how is
the good now absent from God? For it is altogether equally a good for both,
for the Son who is subjected and for the Father who receives the subjection of
the Son. There is lacking then in the present both to the Father and to the
Son such a good, and what neither the Father nor the Son had before the ages,
this upon the completion of the times will be added both to the Father and to
the Son, the one undergoing the subjection and the other receiving through
this some addition and increase of his own glory which in the present he does
not yet have. Where then in this is the unalterable? For what becomes
something after these things but is not now is proper to the changeable
nature. If then subjection is a good, it is fitting to believe that the good
is now also with God; if such a thing is unworthy upon God, neither now nor at
another time; 10 but the apostle says that then the Son will be subjected to
God and Father, not that he has been subjected now; therefore the account
looks toward some other aim and the signification of the name is far from the
malice of the heretics. What then is the account? Perhaps through the context
of the things written <in> this part one might rather see the thought. For
since he set up a contested account toward the Corinthians, who received the
faith toward the Lord but thought the doctrine concerning the resurrection of
men a myth, saying: How are the dead raised and with what body do they come,
whose bodies after death have come round in many ways and many forms to
disappearance, either through decay or consumed by the flesh-eating reptiles,
swimming things, birds, four-footed things? For this reason he set before them
many reasonings, persuading them not to liken the power of God to their own
nor to think that what is impossible for man is equal also upon God, but from
the examples known to us to analogize the greatness of the divine authority;
and thus he sets before them the wonder-working concerning the bodies of the
seeds that are always being made new by the divine power and <he shows> that
the wisdom of God has not grown weary of inventing throughout the whole myriad
forms of bodies, of rational, irrational, aerial, terrestrial, and of those 11
appearing to us according to heaven, of the sun and of the remaining stars, of
which each, having come to be <by the> divine power, of God not being at a
loss even according to the resurrection concerning our bodies ** becomes a
demonstration for us. For if all the things that are were not refashioned
toward what appears from some underlying matter but the divine will became
matter and substance of the things created, he constructs that it is much more
possible that what already is be brought back again into its own form than
that what from the beginning is not come into subsistence and substance.
Having shown then in the accounts toward them that, the first man having been
dissolved into earth through sin and for this reason having been called
earthy, it was consistent according to him that also all those from him become
earthy and mortal, those sprung from such a one, he necessarily brought on
also the second sequence through which man is again reconstituted from the
mortal toward immortality, saying in like manner that the good has


come to be for the nature, flowing from one into all, just as also the evil
through one was poured into a multitude, being widened together by the
succession of those coming after. And he has used these words constructing the
doctrine concerning this.

The first, 12 he says, man is from earth, earthy; the second from heaven; such
as the earthy, such also the earthy ones, and such as the heavenly, such also
the heavenly ones; and just as we have worn the image of the earthy, so we
shall also wear the image of the heavenly. Having then by these and such
reasonings strengthened the account concerning the resurrection and having
through many others entangled the heretics with the syllogisms in which he was
demonstrating that the one who disbelieves the resurrection of men does not
accept even the resurrection of Christ, through the weaving of things joined
to one another he constructed the inescapable in the conclusions, saying that
If there is no resurrection of the dead, neither has Christ been raised; and
if Christ has not been raised, vain also is the faith toward him. For the
premise being true that Christ has been raised from the dead, that which is
joined to this must also altogether be true: that there is a resurrection of
the dead; for with the particular demonstration the universal is also
demonstrated together. And conversely, if someone should say that the
universal is false: that there is a resurrection of the dead, neither will the
particular altogether be found true: that Christ has been raised from the
dead; for if it is universally impossible, it is not altogether possible <in>
some one. 13 But this is faithful and indisputable to those who have received
the Word, that Christ has been raised from the dead; necessarily in the
particular faith of the resurrection of Christ the universal also will have
the faithful. Thus then having syllogistically compelled them toward the
reception of the doctrine from saying that If it is not (for what is not
universally cannot be in some one either; but if we believe that this one has
been raised, the faith concerning this becomes a demonstration of the
universal resurrection of men) and having added to the account that by which
the whole construction concerning this doctrine is concluded, As in Adam all
die so also in Christ all will be made alive, he clearly unveils the mystery
concerning this, toward what it looks, in the things that follow directing the
account through some necessary sequence toward the end of the things hoped
for. And the aim of the things said is this. I will set out first in my own
phrasing the sense of the things written, and then thus I will set beside it
the account of the apostle which is fitted to the sense set out beforehand by
us.

What then is the aim of the account which the divine apostle dogmatizes in
that part? That sometime the nature of evil will pass over toward non-being,
having been completely made to vanish from be 14 ing, and the divine and
unmixed goodness will contain in itself every rational nature, none of the
things that have come to be from God falling away from the kingdom of God,
when every wickedness mixed into the things that are, as some counterfeit
matter, has been consumed through the melting of the purifying fire, and
everything that had its coming-to-be from God has become such as it was from
the beginning when it had not yet received wickedness. And he says that this
comes to be thus: there came to be, he says, in the mortal and perishable
nature of men the pure and unmixed Godhead of the only-begotten. And from all
the human nature with which the divine was mixed, as some firstfruits of the
common lump, the man according to Christ came to subsistence, through whom the
whole human was attached to the Godhead. Since then in that one every nature
of wickedness was made to vanish, Who did no sin, as the prophet says, nor was
deceit found in his mouth, and together with the sin there vanished also in
the same the death that follows it (for there is no other genesis of death
besides sin), from that one both the vanishing of wickedness and the
dissolution of death took a beginning. Then as 15 some order was imposed
through some sequence upon what was coming to be. For that which is always
according to the stepping-down of the good found more removed from the first
or nearer, however it


has of worth and of power, each one, thus it follows the one going before; so
that after the man in Christ who became firstfruits of our nature having
received in himself the Godhead, who also became Firstfruits of those who have
fallen asleep and Firstborn from the dead Having loosed the pangs of death;
after this man then, who was separated from sin altogether and who in himself
made idle the power of death and dissolved all its rule and authority and
power, if someone should be found according to Paul, who became as far as was
possible an imitator of Christ in the alienation from evil, such a one will
follow behind the firstfruits in the time of the appearing; and again to this
one, I say by hypothesis, Timothy, if it should so happen, who as he was able
imitated in himself the teacher, or some other such; and thus in order as many
as through the gradual letting-down of the good are found behind those always
going before, until the sequence of those following arrives at those in whom,
evil being in excess, the portion of the better is found lesser; according to
the same analogy, from those having the lesser in wickedness, the
16
sequence making the order of those being dissolved toward the better upon
those excelling in evil, until the advance of the good arrives at the
uttermost bound of evil, making wickedness vanish. Which indeed is the end of
the hope, that nothing contrary to the good be left remaining, but the divine
life having gone through all things may make death vanish altogether from the
things that are, its sin having been destroyed beforehand, from which, as it
has been said, death had the kingdom against men.

Every wicked authority then and rule having been dissolved in us and no
passion any longer being lord of our nature, there is every necessity, nothing
else holding mastery, that all things be subjected to the rule over all. And
subjection to God is the complete alienation from evil. Whenever then
according to the imitation of the firstfruits we all become outside of evil,
then the whole lump of the nature, mixed together with the firstfruits and
having become one body according to the continuous, will receive upon itself
the hegemony of the good alone, and thus, the whole body of our nature having
been blended with the divine and unmixed nature, that so-called subjection of
the Son comes about through us, the subjection accomplished in his body being
referred to him who worked in us the grace of the subjection. This then is the
sense of the things dogmatized by the great Paul, as at least we have
supposed. And it would be time also to set beside it the very words of the
apostle, having 17 thus: For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be
made alive; but each in his own order; firstfruits Christ, then those of
Christ at his appearing, then the end, when he hands over the kingdom to God
and Father, when he makes idle every rule and authority and power. For he must
reign until he puts the enemies under his feet. Last enemy death is made idle;
for he subjected all things under his feet. But when he says that he has
subjected all things, it is clear that it is except him who subjected all
things to him. And when he subjects all things to him, then even he himself
will be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all
in all.

For clearly in the last of the things said he presents by the account the
non-existence of wickedness in saying that God comes to be all in all,
becoming all to each. For it is clear that then it will be true that God is in
all, when no evil is contemplated in the things that are. For it is not likely
that God come to be also in evil. So that either he will not be in all
whenever some evil is left remaining in the things that are, or, if one must
truly believe him to be in all, that there is no evil is demonstrated together
with the faith concerning this. For it is not possible for God to come to be
in evil. 18 And that God comes to be all <in all> the things that are
indicates the simple and uniform of the life hoped for by us. This


for no longer collecting our life together through many and varied things
after the likeness of the present life the account presents, that God comes to
be all things for us that seem necessary to this life, each being partaken of
through some analogy toward the more divine; so that God is also food for us,
as it is likely that God be eaten, and drink, likewise clothing and covering,
air, place, wealth, enjoyment, beauty, health, strength, prudence, glory,
blessedness, and all that is judged in the good portion of which the nature is
in want, the signification of the things said being raised toward what is
God-befitting; so as to learn through this that he who has come to be in God
has all things in having that one. And to have God is nothing other than to be
united to God. And one would not otherwise be united unless having become
one-body with him, as Paul names it; for all, being joined to the one body of
Christ through the participation, become one body of him.

Whenever then the good has come through all, then his whole body will be
subjected to the life-giving authority, and thus the subjection of this body
is said to be the subjection of the Son himself who has been blended toward
his own body, which is the church, as the apostle says to the Colossians,
speaking thus in the word: Now I rejoice in my sufferings 19 and I fill up the
lacks of the afflictions for Christ in my flesh for the sake of his body,
which is the church, of which I became a minister according to the economy.
And to the church of the Corinthians he says: You are body of Christ and
members in part. And more clearly to the Ephesians he sets beside them the
doctrine concerning this through the things in which he says that But speaking
truth in love let us grow up into him in all things who is the head, Christ,
from whom the whole body, being joined together and compacted through every
joint <of the> supply according to the working in the measure of each single
part, makes the increase of the body toward the building of itself in love; as
through those always being added to the faith Christ builds himself. Who will
then cease building himself whenever the increase and completion of his body
arrives at its own measure, and there no longer lacks to the body that which
is added through building, all having been built upon the foundation of the
prophets and apostles and having been added to the faith, whenever, as the
apostle says, We all arrive at the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of
the Son of God, at a complete man, at the measure of the age of the fullness
of Christ.

If then being head he himself builds the body next to him through those always
being added, joining together and compacting all into what each is naturally
according to the measure of the working, so as to become either a hand or an
eye or a foot or hearing or 20 some other of the things that complete the body
according to the analogy of the faith of each, and doing these things he
builds himself, as it has been said, it would be clear through these things
that coming to be in all he receives into himself all who are united to him
through the communion of the body, and he makes all members of his own body,
so that there are Many members but one body. He then who has united us to
himself and been united to us and through all become one toward us makes all
our things his own. And the chief of our goods is the subjection toward the
divine, whenever all the creation becomes of one voice toward itself and Every
knee bows to him of heavenly and earthly and subterranean and every tongue
confesses that Jesus Christ is Lord. Then, all the creation having become one
body and all having grown together with one another in him through the
obedience, he refers to himself the subjection of his own body toward the
Father. And let no one be astonished at what is said. For we also, by a
certain custom, reckon to the soul what comes about through our body, as that
one conversing with his own soul upon the good yield of the land, Eat and
drink and be glad, refers


the satiety of the flesh to the soul. So also there the subjection of the
ecclesiastical body is referred to the one dwelling in the body. And since
everything that has come to be in him is saved, and 21 salvation is
interpreted through the subjection, as the psalmody proposes that we
understand, consistently we learn in this part of the apostle to believe that
nothing is outside of those being saved. And the account indicates this by the
taking-down of death and by the subjection of the Son, because these things
coincide with one another, both that death sometime is not and that all come
to be in life; and the Lord is life, through whom there comes about, according
to the apostolic account, for his whole body the bringing-near toward the
Father,

When he hands over our kingdom to God and Father. And his body, as it has been
said many times, is all the human nature with which he was mixed. And through
this very thought the Lord was also named by Paul Mediator of God and men. For
he who is in the Father and has come to be in men fulfills the mediation in
this, in uniting all to himself and through himself to the Father, as the Lord
says in the gospel, making the account toward the Father: That they all may be
one just as you, Father, in me and I in you, that thus they also may be one in
us. For this clearly presents that he who is in the Father, having united us
to himself, works through himself our conjunction toward the Father. But also
the things that follow of the gospel agree with the things said: The glory
which you have given me I have given them; for glory 22 I think he says here
the Holy Spirit which he gave to the disciples through the breathing-upon. For
it is not otherwise possible for those standing apart from one another to be
united unless they grow together by the unity of the Spirit; For if someone
does not have the Spirit of Christ, this one is not of him. And the Spirit is
the glory, as he says elsewhere toward the Father: Glorify me with the glory
which I had from the beginning with you before the world was. For the God Word
who before the world had the glory of the Father, since in the last of the
days he became flesh, it was necessary [and] that the flesh also through the
blending toward the Word become that which the Word is; and it becomes this
from receiving that which the Word had before the world; and this was the Holy
Spirit; for nothing else is pre-eternal besides Father and Son and Holy
Spirit. For this reason he says also here that The glory which you have given
me I have given them, that through it they may be united to me and through me
to you. And let us see also the things set next in the gospel: That they may
be one just as we are one; you in me and I in them; because I and you are one;
that they may be completed into the one. For these things I think need no
explanation toward being fitted to the thought set forth, the phrase itself
clearly setting out the doctrine concerning these things. That they may be one
just as we are one; for it is not possible otherwise for 23 all to become one
Just as we are one, unless having been separated from all the things that
divide them from one another they are united to us who are one, That they may
be one just as we are one. And how does this come to be? Because I in them.
For it is not possible that I come to be in them alone, but altogether also
you, since I and you are one. And thus they will become Completed into the one
who have been completed in us; for we are the one.

And he indicates such a grace more clearly by the following account, having
said thus that You have loved them just as you have loved me. For if the
Father loves the Son, and in the Son we all come to be who through the faith
toward him become his body, consistently he who loves his own Son loves also
the body of the Son as the Son himself; and we are the body. Therefore through
the things said the apostolic thought has become clear, that the subjection of
the Son toward the Father indicates the knowledge of the one who is and the
salvation that comes about of all human nature.

And the account would become clearer for us also from certain other apostolic
thoughts, of which I will mention one only, the many of the testimonies, by
reverence


of not extending the account into a multitude, having declined. For Paul says
somewhere of his own accounts that I have been crucified with Christ; and I
live 24 no longer I, but Christ lives in me. Therefore if Paul no longer
lives, he who has been crucified to the world, but Christ lives in him,
everything that comes about and is said by Paul is reasonably referred to the
Christ living in him. For also the words of Paul he says are spoken by Christ,
he who said: Or do you seek a proof of the Christ who speaks in me? And the
evangelical achievements he says are not his own but he ascribes them to the
grace of Christ that dwells in him. If then the Christ living in him is said
according to sequence both to work and to utter the things of that one, and
Paul having stood away from all the things that formerly prevailed, when he
was a blasphemer and a persecutor and an insolent man, looks toward the true
good alone and makes himself obedient and subject to this, then also the
subjection of Paul that has come about toward God has the reference to the one
living in him who both speaks in Paul the good things and works them. And the
chief of all goods is the subjection toward God.

And what the account found upon the one, this will reasonably be fitted also
to all the creation of men, whenever, as the Lord says, the gospel comes to be
in all the world. For all having put off the old man with his deeds and his
desires and having received the Lord in themselves, necessarily the one living
in them works the good things that come about from them. 25 And the uttermost
of all goods is the salvation that comes about for us through the alienation
from evil; but it is not otherwise possible to be separated from evil unless
having been blended with God through the subjection; therefore even this very
subjection toward God has the reference to the one living in us. For if
anything is fair, it is of him, and if anything is good, it is from him, says
one of the prophets. Since then the subjection has been shown to be both fair
and good, of that one, and the good of this is altogether from him from whom
is the nature of every good, as the account of the prophet. And let no one,
looking toward the common catachresis of the name of subjection, set aside the
name. For the wisdom of the great Paul knows how to use the words according to
authority toward what seems good, and to fit the emphases of the words to the
proper sequence of the thought, even if custom carries the catachresis of the
phrases toward certain other notions. For from where has the use of He emptied
himself been taken by him, and My boast no one will empty, and The faith has
been emptied, and That the cross of Christ may not be emptied? From what usage
did he receive these into his own account? And who will judge him having said
Homeiromenoi of you, through which phrase he indicates the loving relation?
And from where did he indicate the unarrogant of love through the phrase of
not vaunting? And the contentious and defensive rivalry, how is it signified
by him by the word of eritheia, it being clear to all that from 26
wool-working the wool-worker is named out<side> of scripture and we are
accustomed to signify the zeal concerning wools by the name of eritheia? But
nevertheless Paul, having let the cold etymologies go, presents through the
phrases he wishes the thought he wishes. For also many other things would be
found by those who examine accurately in the accounts of the apostle not
enslaved to the usage of custom, but according to some peculiar thought
brought forward by him upon authority, turning not at all toward custom. Thus
then also here the signification of subjection has been thought by Paul
something other than the common notions. And a demonstration of the account is
that not even the subjection of the enemies mentioned by him in this part has
the constrained and unwilling, as those enslaved to custom would say, but
clearly upon them salvation is interpreted by the name of subjection; and a
token is that the name of enmity has been distinguished according to this part
by Paul into a double signification; for of the enemies, some will be
subjected


he says, and others will be made idle. Made idle then is the enemy by nature,
that is death, and the rule and authority and power of sin concerning it. And
those will be subjected who according to another account are called enemies of
God, those who from the 27 kingdom deserted toward sin, of whom he also makes
mention in the account toward the Romans saying that For if being enemies we
were reconciled to God; for the subjection here he names there reconciliation,
having indicated one thought through each of the names, the salvation. For as
from the subjection being saved is added, so also in another place he says
that Having been reconciled we shall be saved in his life.

Such enemies then he says are subjected to God and Father, but death and the
rule concerning it will no longer be. For this the phrase of being made idle
indicates; so that through this it becomes clear that the dominion of the
evils will be made completely idle. And those who through the disobedience
were called enemies of God, these through the subjection will become friends
of the Lord, whenever they are persuaded by the one who says that We are
ambassadors for Christ, as God entreating through us; we beg for Christ, be
reconciled to God, and according to the promise that has come about in the
gospel they will no longer be numbered by the Lord among the slaves but among
the friends, having been reconciled. And For he must reign, until he puts the
enemies under his feet, we shall piously, as I think, receive, having
understood by the reigning his being champion. For then the one mighty in war
ceases from the championship, whenever everything opposing the good has
vanished, whenever having gathered all his own kingdom he brings it to God and
Father, having united all things to himself. For his handing over 28 the
kingdom to the Father is the same according to the sense as bringing all to
God, through whom we have the bringing-near in one Spirit toward the Father.
All then who were once enemies having become a footstool of the feet of God by
receiving the divine footprint in themselves, and death having been made idle
(for if there are not those who die neither will death altogether be), then in
the subjection of us all, which is thought not as slavish lowliness but as
kingdom and incorruption and blessedness, the one living in us is said by Paul
to be subjected to God, he who completes our good through himself and does in
us for himself what is well-pleasing. These things according to the measure of
our mind, as much as we had room for, we have understood from the great wisdom
of Paul in this part, wishing to show that the champions of the heretical
doctrines have not examined the aim of the apostle looking toward which he has
made the present account. If then the full assurance upon this inquiry has
become sufficient for you through the things said, the grace is to be referred
to God; but if something should also seem to you to be lacking, we shall
receive with eagerness the filling-up of what is lacking, if it should both be
made known to us from you through the letter and the manifestation of the
hidden things should be made clear from the Holy Spirit through our prayers.
