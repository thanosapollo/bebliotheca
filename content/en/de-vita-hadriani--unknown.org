* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The emperor Hadrian's origin is older among the Picenes; the later line comes
from Spain. For Hadrian himself records in the books of his own life that his
ancestors, sprung from Hadria, settled at Italica in the time of the Scipios.

Hadrian's father was Aelius Hadrianus, surnamed Afer, a cousin of the emperor
Trajan. His mother was Domitia Paulina, from Gades. His sister Paulina was
married to Servianus. His wife was Sabina. His great-great-grandfather was
Marullinus, the first in his family to be a senator of the Roman people.

He was born at Rome on the ninth day before the Kalends of February, in the
seventh consulship of Vespasian and the fifth of Titus. In his tenth year he
lost his father, and had as guardians Ulpius Trajan, then of praetorian rank,
his cousin, who later held the empire, and Caelius Attianus, a Roman knight.

He was steeped more eagerly in Greek studies, his mind leaning so far that way
that by some Little Greek

he was called. In his fifteenth year he returned to his native town and at
once entered military service. He was devoted to hunting even to the point of
blame, and for that reason Trajan took him away from his native town and
treated him as a son. Not long after he was appointed one of the ten judges of
lawsuits, and from there was made tribune of the Second Legion Adiutrix. After
that he was transferred to Lower Moesia, already in the last years of
Domitian.

There he is said to have learned from a certain astrologer of his future
empire, the same that he had already learned had been foretold by his
great-uncle Aelius Hadrianus, who was skilled in the knowledge of the heavens.
When Trajan was adopted by Nerva, Hadrian was sent to congratulate the army
and was transferred to Upper Germany. Hastening from there to Trajan, so that
he might be the first to report Nerva's death, he was long detained by
Servianus, his sister's husband (who, by revealing both his expenses and his
debts, stirred Trajan's dislike of him), and delayed when his carriage was
broken on purpose. Making the journey on foot, he outstripped Servianus's own
courier. He had Trajan's affection, and yet, through the attendants of the
boys Trajan loved too warmly, [ellipsis] with Gallus favouring him, he was not
left without support. At that time, anxious about the emperor's judgment of
him, he consulted the lots from Virgil, and this lot fell out:

/Who is that far off, marked by olive boughs, bearing the sacred things? I
know the locks and the hoary chin of the Roman king, who will first found the
city with laws, sent from little Cures and a poor land into a great empire,
whom thereafter will succeed/

[ellipsis]

which others said had come to him from the Sibylline verses. He also had a
presentiment of empire soon to come from an answer that issued from the shrine
of Jupiter the Trophy-bearer, which Apollonius the Syrian Platonist put in his
books. At last, with Sura's support, he returned at once to a fuller
friendship with Trajan, taking as wife the granddaughter of Trajan's sister,
Plotina favouring it, Trajan only mildly willing, as Marius Maximus says.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

He held the quaestorship in the fourth consulship of Trajan and the consulship
of Articuleius. In that office, when he was laughed at for delivering the
emperor's speech in the senate too roughly, he applied himself to Latin until
he reached the highest skill and fluency.

After the quaestorship he kept the acts of the senate, and went with Trajan to
the Dacian war on closer terms. Indeed he says that he gave himself to wine,
falling in with Trajan's habits, and was for that reason most richly rewarded
by Trajan. He was made tribune of the people in the second consulship of
Candidus and Quadratus. In that magistracy he claims an omen of perpetual
tribunician power was given him, because he lost the heavy cloaks which the
tribunes of the people used to wear in rainy weather, but emperors never; and
from this even today emperors are seen without such cloaks among men in togas.
In the second Dacian campaign Trajan set him over the First Legion Minervia
and took him with him, and many of his exploits then became famous. For this
he was given the diamond which Trajan had received from Nerva, and was raised
to the hope of succession. He was made praetor in the second consulship of
Suburanus and the second of Servianus. He received two million sesterces from
Trajan for giving games. Afterward, sent as praetorian legate to Lower
Pannonia, he checked the Sarmatians, kept military discipline, and restrained
procurators who were ranging too freely. For this he was made consul. In that
magistracy, when he learned from Sura that he was to be adopted by Trajan,
Trajan's friends stopped scorning and neglecting him. And after Sura's death
Trajan's intimacy with him grew, chiefly because of the speeches which

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

he had drafted for the emperor. He also enjoyed Plotina's favour, by whose
zeal he was even appointed legate at the time of the Parthian expedition. At
that season Hadrian used the friendship of Sosius Papus and Platorius Nepos
from the senatorial order, and from the equestrian, of Attianus, once his
guardian, and of Livianus and Turbo. He came into the pledge of adoption when
Palma and Celsus, always his enemies, and whom he later pursued, had fallen
under suspicion of aiming at usurpation. Made consul for the second time by
Plotina's favour, he earned the full expectation of adoption. A strong opinion
held that in those times when he was more at home in the palace he had bribed
Trajan's freedmen, courted the emperor's favourites, and often been intimate
with them.

On the fifth day before the Ides of August, as legate of Syria, he received
the letter of adoption, and he ordered the birthday of his adoption to be
kept. On the third day before the Ides of the same month, which he also
appointed to be kept as the birthday of his rule, Trajan's death was reported
to him.

There was, to be sure, a widespread opinion that Trajan's mind had been to
leave Neratius Priscus, not Hadrian, as his successor, many friends agreeing
in this, to the point that he once said to Priscus:

/I commend the provinces to you, if anything fatal befalls me./

And many say Trajan had it in mind to die without a certain successor, after
the example of Alexander of Macedon. Many say he had meant to send a speech to
the senate asking that, if anything happened to him, the senate should give a
prince to the Roman state, with names added from which the senate itself
should choose the best. Nor are there wanting those who have reported that
Hadrian was taken into adoption by Plotina's faction after Trajan was already
dead, a man being put in place who spoke for Trajan in a failing voice.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Once he had obtained the empire he at once set himself to the ancient manner
of rule and gave his effort to keeping peace throughout the world.

For the nations Trajan had subdued were falling away: the Moors were
harassing, the Sarmatians were making war, the Britons could not be held under
Roman sway, Egypt was hard pressed by riots, and Libya and Palestine besides
were lifting rebellious spirits. So he left all that lay beyond the Euphrates
and Tigris, following, as he said, the example of Cato, who declared the
Macedonians free because they could not be held.

Parthamasiris, whom Trajan had made king of the Parthians, he gave as king to
the neighbouring peoples, because he saw that the man was of no great weight
among the Parthians.

He showed such a zeal for clemency at once that, though in the first days of
his rule he was advised by letter from Attianus that Baebius Macer, prefect of
the city, should be put to death if he resisted his rule, and likewise
Laberius Maximus, who was in exile on an island under suspicion regarding the
empire, and Frugi Crassus, he harmed no one. Yet afterward a procurator killed
Crassus without Hadrian's order, when Crassus had left the island, as if he
were plotting a revolution.

He gave the soldiers a double largess for the auspices of his rule. Lusius
Quietus he disarmed, taking from him the Moorish tribes he governed, because
he had been under suspicion regarding the empire, Marcius Turbo being assigned
to put down the unrest in Mauretania after he had checked the Jews.

After this he left Antioch to look upon Trajan's remains, which Attianus,
Plotina, and Matidia were bringing. When he had received them and sent them by
ship to Rome, he himself returned to Antioch, set Catilius Severus over Syria,
and came to Rome through Illyricum.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He asked the senate for divine honours for Trajan in a letter most carefully
composed, and obtained them, all being willing, so that the senate of its own
accord decreed many things in Trajan's honour which Hadrian had not asked.

When he wrote to the senate he asked pardon for not having left the senate a
judgment on his own rule, having of course been hailed emperor too hastily by
the soldiers, because the state could not be without an emperor. When the
senate offered him the triumph that had been due to Trajan, he refused it
himself and carried Trajan's image in the triumphal chariot, so that the best
of emperors should not lose the dignity of a triumph even after death. The
name of Father of his Country, offered him at once and again later, he put
off, because Augustus had earned that name only late. He remitted the
crown-gold from Italy and lessened it in the provinces, and that after he had
set out the difficulties of the treasury with ambitious care.

Then, hearing of an uprising of the Sarmatians and Roxolani, he sent the
armies ahead and made for Moesia. Marcius Turbo, after Mauretania, he adorned
with the insignia of the prefecture and set over Pannonia and Dacia for a
time.

With the king of the Roxolani, who was complaining of reduced subsidies, he
settled the matter and made peace.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

He escaped a plot of Nigrinus, which that man, with Lusius in his confidence
and many others, had prepared against Hadrian while he was sacrificing, though
Hadrian had even marked Nigrinus out as his own successor.

For this Palma was killed at Tarracina, Celsus at Baiae, Nigrinus at Faventia,
and Lusius on the road, by order of the senate, against Hadrian's will, as he
himself says in his own life. Hadrian therefore came at once to Rome to refute
the blackest opinion of himself, that he had allowed four men of consular rank
to be killed at one time. Dacia was entrusted to Turbo, who was given the
title of the Egyptian prefecture so that he might have more authority. To
crush the talk about himself Hadrian gave the people a double largess in
person, three gold pieces each having already been distributed while he was
away. In the senate too he excused what had been done and swore that he would
never punish a senator except by the senate's sentence.

He set up a regular fiscal post, so that magistrates should not be burdened
with this charge. Sparing nothing to win favour, he remitted an endless sum of
money owed to the treasury by private debtors in the city and Italy, and in
the provinces huge sums even from arrears, the bonds being burned in the Forum
of the deified Trajan, so that security might be more firmly established for
all. He forbade the goods of the condemned to be taken into his private
treasury, the whole sum being received into the public treasury.

To the boys and girls to whom Trajan had already granted maintenance he added
an increase of bounty. To senators who had not failed through their own fault
he made up the senatorial census according to the number of their children, so
that to most he paid a measured amount for the term of their lives without
delay. To fill out honours he bestowed much not only on friends but even on a
good many at large. He helped some women with funds to sustain their lives. He
gave a gladiatorial show for six days running and exhibited a thousand wild
beasts on his birthday.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

He took the best men of the senate into the companionship of imperial majesty.
He scorned circus games except those decreed for his birthday. Both in
assembly and in the senate he often said that he would so manage the state
that they should know it was the people's affair, not his own. He made a great
many men consul for the third time, once he himself had been consul three
times, and he heaped the honour of a second consulship on countless men. He
himself held the third consulship for only four months, and in it he often sat
in judgment. He always attended the regular senate when he was in the city or
near it.

He raised the senate's dignity so high, making senators with such difficulty,
that when he made Attianus a senator, already endowed from the prefecture of
the guard with consular ornaments, he showed that he had nothing more that
could be conferred on him. He did not allow Roman knights to judge concerning
senators either without him or with him. For it was then the custom that when
the prince heard cases he would call both senators and Roman knights into
counsel and deliver a sentence from the deliberation of all. Finally he cursed
those princes who had paid less deference to senators.

On Servianus, his sister's husband, to whom he showed such respect that he
always went to meet him when Servianus came from the bedchamber, he conferred
a third consulship, though not as his colleague, since Servianus had been
consul twice before Hadrian, so that Servianus should not have only the
second vote. He granted it without Servianus asking and without any petition.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Meanwhile, for all that, he also abandoned many provinces Trajan had acquired,
and against everyone's wishes he pulled down the theatre Trajan had set in the
Field of Mars. These things seemed the more grim because Hadrian pretended
that everything he had seen fit to dislike had been secretly charged to him by
Trajan to do. When he could not bear the power of Attianus, his prefect and
once his guardian, he tried to have him cut down, but he was checked, because
he was already under the odium of four consulars killed, whose death he indeed
laid to Attianus's counsels. Since he could not give Attianus a successor,
because the man did not ask, he so managed that Attianus did ask, and as soon
as he asked, Hadrian transferred the power to Turbo. At the same time he also
gave Septicius Clarus as successor to Similis, the other prefect.

When he had removed from the prefecture those to whom he owed the empire, he
made for Campania and relieved all its towns with benefits and largesses,
binding every best man to his friendship. At Rome he attended the duties of
the praetors and consuls, was present at his friends' dinners, visited the
sick two and three times a day, and some Roman knights and freedmen as well.
He comforted them, helped them with advice, always had them at his own
dinners, and in short did everything in the manner of a private man. He paid
his mother-in-law special honours with gladiatorial games and other
attentions.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

After this he set out for the Gallic provinces and relieved all the cities
with various acts of bounty. From there he crossed into Germany. More eager
for peace than for war, he trained the soldier as if war were at hand,
schooling him in proofs of endurance. He himself also lived the soldier's life
among the companies, gladly using even camp fare in the open, that is bacon,
cheese, and sour wine, after the example of Scipio Aemilianus and Metellus and
his own adoptive father Trajan. He gave many men rewards and some honours, so
that they could bear the harsher things he ordered. For he himself, after
Caesar Octavian, restored the discipline that had been slipping through the
neglect of earlier princes, setting both duties and expenses in order. He
never allowed anyone to be away from camp unjustly. Tribunes were recommended
not by the soldiers' favour but by justice. He urged the rest on by the
example of his own hardiness as well, for he would even march twenty miles
armed on foot. He tore dining-rooms, colonnades, vaults, and pleasure-gardens
out of the camps. He often put on the humblest clothing, took a sword-belt
without gold, fastened it with a clasp without gems, and sheathed his long
sword with a hilt scarcely of ivory. He visited sick soldiers in their
quarters, chose the site for camp, gave the vine-staff to none but a strong
man of good name, and made no one tribune unless he was full-bearded or of an
age whose prudence and years could fill out the strength of the tribunate. He
would not let a tribune take anything from a soldier. He cleared away all
softness on every side, and last of all he set their arms and their kit in
order. He also judged of the soldiers' ages, so that no one younger than
valour required, or older than humanity could bear, should serve in camp
against the old custom. He took care that they should always be known to him,
and

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11

that their number should be known. He further took pains to know the military
stores with care, and he shrewdly examined the provincial revenues too, so
that if anything were lacking anywhere he might make it good. Above all,
though, he strove never to buy or keep anything idle.

So, when the soldiers had been brought round in royal fashion, he made for
Britain, where he set many things right and first drew a wall for eighty
miles, to divide barbarians from Romans.

He gave successors to Septicius Clarus, prefect of the guard, and to Suetonius
Tranquillus, secretary of letters, and to many others, because they had then
conducted themselves with his wife Sabina more familiarly, without his order,
than the reverence of the court required. He would even have sent his wife
away, as difficult and harsh, so he himself used to say, if he had been a
private man.

He was inquisitive not only about his own household but about his friends as
well, so that through the commissaries he searched out all secrets, and his
friends would not notice that their life was known to the emperor before the
emperor himself showed it.

Hence it is not unamusing to insert a matter from which it is clear that he
learned much about his friends. For when someone's wife had written to him
that, held back by pleasures and baths, he was unwilling to return to her, and
Hadrian had learned this through the commissaries, then, when the man asked
for leave, Hadrian reproached him with baths and pleasures. The man said:

/Surely my wife has not written to you what she wrote to me?/

This they think a most vicious trait, and they add what they assert about his
passion for grown youths and his adulteries with married women, with which
Hadrian is said to have been afflicted, joining to this that he did not keep
faith even with his friends.

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

When affairs in Britain had been settled he crossed into Gaul, disturbed by a
riot at Alexandria that had arisen over Apis. When that bull had been found
after many years, it raised tumults among the peoples as to where he ought to
be housed, all competing eagerly. About the same time he built a basilica of
wonderful work at Nemausus in honour of Plotina. After this he made for the
Spains and wintered at Tarraco, where at his own cost he restored the temple
of Augustus. He called all the Spaniards to an assembly at Tarraco.
They drew back from the levy, the men of Italica jokingly, as Marius Maximus
sets down the very words, the rest most vehemently. He dealt with them wisely
and carefully. At that time, not without glory, he met a most
grave danger at Tarraco: while walking in the pleasure-grounds a slave of his
host rushed at him quite madly with a sword. Hadrian, holding him, handed him
to the servants as they ran up, and when it was established that the man was
mad, he gave him to the physicians to be treated, wholly unmoved.

In those times and often elsewhere, in many places where barbarians are
divided not by rivers but by frontier-lines, he separated the barbarians by
great stakes sunk deep and joined in the manner of a mural fence.

He appointed a king for the Germans, crushed a rising of the Moors, and earned
thanksgivings from the senate. The Parthian war at the same time had only been
set in motion, and it was checked by a conference with Hadrian.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

After this he sailed through Asia and the islands to Achaia, and took the
Eleusinian rites after the example of Hercules and Philip. He bestowed much on
the Athenians and sat as president of the games. In Achaia, they say, this too
was observed, that though many in the rites had knives, no one armed entered
when Hadrian was there. Afterward he sailed to Sicily, where he climbed Mount
Aetna to see the sunrise, varied, as they say, in the likeness of a rainbow.
From there he came to Rome, and from Rome he crossed to Africa and assigned
many benefits to the African provinces. Scarcely any prince ever traversed so
much of the earth so swiftly.

Then, when he had returned from Africa to Rome, he set out at once for the
East, made his way through Athens, and dedicated the works he had begun among
the Athenians, such as the temple of Olympian Jupiter and an altar to himself.
In the same way, travelling through Asia, he consecrated temples of his own
name.

Then from the Cappadocians he took slaves who would be of use to the camps.

He invited local rulers and kings to friendship, inviting even Osroes, king of
the Parthians, sending back to him the daughter Trajan had captured, and
promising the throne that had likewise been taken. When certain kings had come
to him, he dealt with them so that those who had been unwilling to come were
sorry, especially Pharasmanes, who had proudly neglected his invitation. And
as he went about the provinces he punished procurators and governors according
to their deeds, so severely that he was believed, of himself,

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

to put accusers forward. Among these things he so hated the people of Antioch
that he wished to separate Syria from Phoenicia, so that Antioch should not be
called the metropolis of so many cities.

In that season the Jews also began a war, because they were forbidden to
mutilate the genitals. But on Mount Casius, when he had climbed by night for
the sake of seeing the sunrise, a rain came on and a bolt of lightning fell
and struck the victim and the attendant as he was sacrificing. After
traversing Arabia he came to Pelusium and built up Pompey's tomb more
magnificently.

He lost his Antinous while sailing on the Nile, and wept for him as a woman
would. About him the report varies, some asserting that he had devoted himself
for Hadrian, others pointing to what his beauty shows and Hadrian's excessive
pleasure. The Greeks, at Hadrian's wish, consecrated him, claiming that
oracles were given through him, which Hadrian himself is rumoured to have
composed.

For he was too eager a student of poems and letters, most skilled in
arithmetic, geometry, and painting. He even made a show of knowledge of
playing and singing. He was excessive in pleasures; for he composed many
verses about his favourites and wrote love poems.

He was likewise most skilled in arms and most knowing in military affairs, and
he handled gladiatorial weapons too. He was severe and affable, grave and
wanton, a delayer and in haste, tight-fisted and liberal, a pretender and
frank, cruel and mild, and always in all things various.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

He enriched his friends, even those who did not ask, while he denied nothing
to those who did. Yet he readily listened to whatever was whispered about his
friends, and so afterward he treated almost all, even the most intimate, or
those he had raised to the highest honours, as if they were enemies: so
Attianus and Nepos and Septicius Clarus. Eudaemon, once privy to his coming to
empire, he reduced to poverty. Polaenus and Marcellus he drove to a voluntary
death. Heliodorus he attacked in a most notorious letter. Titianus he allowed
to be accused and proscribed as privy to a usurpation. Ummidius Quadratus and
Catilius Severus and Turbo he pursued harshly. Servianus, his sister's
husband, already in his ninetieth year, he forced to die, so that the man
should not outlive him. He persecuted freedmen, and some soldiers as well.
And though he was most ready in speech and verse and most skilled in
all the arts, he always laughed at, scorned, and crushed the professors of all
the arts, as being more learned himself. With these same professors and
philosophers he often contended in books or poems published in turn.

Favorinus, indeed, when a word of his had once been faulted by Hadrian, and he
had yielded, raised a most pleasant laugh among friends who blamed him for
yielding wrongly to Hadrian over a word that sound authors had used. For he
said:

/You do not advise me well, my friends, who will not let me believe that man
more learned than all, who has thirty legions./

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Hadrian was so greedy of a famous name that he gave the books of his own life,
written by himself, to his literary freedmen, ordering them to publish them
under their own names. For even Phlegon's books are said to be Hadrian's.

He wrote most obscure books, the Catachannae, in imitation of Antimachus. To
the poet Florus, who wrote to him:

/I do not want to be Caesar, to walk among the Britons, to lurk among
[ellipsis] to endure Scythian frosts,/

he wrote back:

/I do not want to be Florus, to walk among the taverns, to lurk among the
cookshops, to endure round mosquitoes./

He loved besides an old-fashioned kind of speech, and he declaimed
controversies. He preferred Cato to Cicero, Ennius to Virgil, Caelius to
Sallust, and with the same display he judged of Homer and Plato. He thought he
knew astrology so well that he wrote on the Kalends of January what could
happen to him through the whole year, so that in the year he died he wrote
what he was going to do, down to the very hour at which he died.

But though he was ready in finding fault with musicians, tragic and comic
actors, grammarians, and rhetoricians, he nevertheless both honoured all
professors and made them rich, even if he always harried them with questions.
And though he himself was the cause of many going away from him downcast, he
would say that he took it hard if he saw anyone sad.

He had in closest intimacy the philosophers Epictetus and Heliodorus, and, not
to name them all, grammarians, rhetoricians, musicians, geometers, painters,
and astrologers, Favorinus standing out above the rest, as many assert.
Teachers who seemed unfit for their profession he dismissed from it, after
enriching and honouring them.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Those he had as enemies in private life he merely neglected as emperor, so
that to one man whom he had held as a capital foe he said, once he was made
emperor:

/You have escaped./

To those he himself called to military service he always furnished horses,
mules, clothing, expenses, and all equipment. He often sent Saturnalia gifts
and little figurines to friends who were not expecting them, and he himself
gladly received such things from them and gave others in turn. To catch the
frauds of the caterers, when he dined with a great many semicircular tables,
he ordered dishes from other tables, even the last, to be set before him.

He outdid all kings with his gifts. He often bathed in public and with
everyone, from which that bath-house joke became known. For when at one time
he had seen a certain veteran known to him in the service rubbing his back and
the rest of his body against the wall, he asked why the man was giving himself
to the marble to be scraped. When he heard it was done because the man had no
slave, he gave him both slaves and money. But on another day, when a number of
old men were rubbing themselves against the wall to call out the prince's
bounty, he ordered them summoned and to be rubbed down by one another in turn.

He was also a most ostentatious lover of the commons, so eager for travel that
he wished to learn in person all he had read about the places of the world. He
bore cold and storms so patiently that he never covered his head.

He showed the greatest deference to many kings, but from most he even bought
peace, and by some he was scorned. He gave huge gifts to many, but to none
greater than to the king of the Iberians, to whom after magnificent presents
he also gave an elephant and a cohort of fifty. When he himself had received
huge gifts from Pharasmanes, and among them gold-embroidered cloaks as well,
he sent three hundred condemned men into the arena in gold-embroidered cloaks,
to mock the man's gifts.

When he sat in judgment he had in counsel not only his friends or companions
but jurists, and especially Iuventius Celsus, Salvius Julianus, Neratius
Priscus, and others, provided the whole senate had approved them. He
established, among other things, that in no city should any house be pulled
down for the sake of any material to be moved to another town. To the children
of the proscribed he granted a twelfth of the goods. He did not admit charges
of treason. He refused the inheritances of strangers, and did not accept those
of acquaintances if they had children. Concerning treasure-trove he provided
that if anyone found it on his own land he should have it himself; if on
another's, he should give half to the owner; if on public land, he should
share equally with the treasury. He forbade slaves to be killed by their
masters, and ordered them condemned by judges if they deserved it. He forbade
a slave or slave-woman to be sold to a pimp or a gladiator-trainer unless
cause were shown. Bankrupts of their own property, if they were of independent
standing, he ordered to be flogged in the amphitheatre and then dismissed. He
abolished the workhouses of slaves and of free men. He separated the baths by
sexes. If a master had been killed in the house, he directed that inquiry
should not be made of all the slaves, but of those who by nearness could have
been aware.

* Chapter 19

:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

In Etruria he held the praetorship as emperor. Through the Latin towns he was
dictator, aedile, and one of the two chief magistrates. At Naples he was the
people's magistrate, in his own native town a five-year magistrate, and
likewise at Hadria a five-year magistrate, as if in another native town, and
at Athens he was archon.

In almost all cities he both built something and gave games. At Athens he
exhibited a hunt of a thousand wild beasts in the stadium. He never called
away from the city of Rome any hunter or stage-player. At Rome, after other
immense pleasures, he gave the people spices in honour of his mother-in-law,
and in honour of Trajan he ordered balsam and saffron to flow down the steps
of the theatre.

He gave plays of every kind in the theatre after the ancient manner, and he
put the court actors on the public stage. In the Circus he killed many wild
beasts and often a hundred lions. He frequently exhibited military war-dances
to the people, and he often watched gladiators. Though he had made endless
works everywhere, he never inscribed his own name except on the temple of
Trajan his father. At Rome he restored the Pantheon, the Voting Enclosures,
the Basilica of Neptune, many sacred temples, the Forum of Augustus, the Baths
of Agrippa, and he consecrated all these in the proper names of their authors.
He also built a bridge of his own name and a tomb beside the Tiber and a
temple of the Good Goddess.

He also moved the Colossus, standing and slung, by the architect Decrianus,
from the place where the Temple of the City now is, with huge effort, so that
he even furnished twenty-four elephants for the work. And when he had
consecrated this statue to the Sun, after Nero's face, to whom it had formerly
been dedicated, he undertook, with the architect Apollodorus as author, to
make another such for the Moon.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

In conversation even with the humblest he was most courteous, detesting those
who begrudged him this pleasure of humanity as if they were guarding the
prince's eminence.

At Alexandria in the Museum he put many questions to the professors and
himself solved those that were put.

Marius Maximus says he was cruel by nature, and therefore did many things
piously because he feared that the same thing that happened to Domitian might
happen to him.

And though he did not love inscriptions on his works, he named many cities
Hadrianopolis, such as Carthage itself and a part of Athens. He also named
endless aqueducts by this name. He first established an advocate of the
treasury.

He had a huge memory and immense facility; for he himself both dictated
speeches and answered everything.

A great many of his jokes survive; for he was sharp-tongued too. Hence this
also became known, that when he had refused something to a man whose hair was
turning white, and the same man asked again but with his hair dyed, he
answered:

/I already refused this to your father./

He returned their names to a great many without a name-prompter, names he had
heard only once and piled together, so that he often corrected the
name-prompters when they erred. He also spoke the names of veterans whom he
had once discharged. Books read at once, and unknown to most, he reproduced
from memory.

At one time he wrote, dictated, listened, and talked with friends, if it can
be believed.

He grasped all public accounts so that any careful father of a household did
not know his private house so well.

He so loved horses and dogs that he set up tombs for them. He founded the town
of Hadrianotherae in a certain place, because he had hunted there with success
and had once killed a she-bear.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

Concerning all judges he always searched out everything, inquiring until he
found the truth.

He did not want his freedmen to be known in public or to have any power with
him, charging by his own word the vices of freedmen to all earlier princes. He
condemned all his own freedmen who had boasted of him. Hence there is also
that remark about slaves, severe indeed but almost a joke. For when at one
time he had seen a slave of his walking in sight between two senators, he sent
a man to give him a blow and say:

/Do not walk among those whose slave you can still be./

Among foods he uniquely loved the four-fold dish, which was of pheasant, sow's
udder, ham, and pastry.

In his times there were famines, pestilence, and earthquakes, all of which he
provided for as far as he could, and he helped many cities laid waste by these
things. There was also a flood of the Tiber. He gave Latin rights to many
cities and remitted tribute to many.

There were no grave campaigns under him. Wars even were carried through almost
in silence. He was much loved by the soldiers because of his excessive care of
the army, and also because he was most liberal toward them. He always kept the
Parthians in friendship, because he withdrew from them the king Trajan had
imposed. He allowed the Armenians to have a king, though under Trajan they had
had a legate. He did not exact from the Mesopotamians the tribute Trajan had
imposed.

He held the Albanians and the Iberians as closest friends, because he attended
their kings with largesses, though they had scorned to come to him. The kings
of the Bactrians sent envoys to him as suppliants for the sake of seeking
friendship.

* Chapter 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

He very often appointed guardians. He kept civil discipline no less strictly
than military. He ordered senators and Roman knights always to be in togas in
public, unless they were returning from dinner. He himself, when he was in
Italy, always went forth in a toga. He received senators coming to dinner
standing, and he always reclined either covered with a Greek cloak or in a
toga. With the greatest care he set the cost of the dinner day by day and
brought it back to the ancient measure.

He forbade vehicles with huge loads to enter the city.

He did not allow horses to be ridden in the cities. He suffered no one to
bathe in public before the eighth hour except the sick. He was the first to
have Roman knights as secretaries of letters and of petitions. Those he saw
poor and innocent he enriched of his own accord; those enriched by cunning he
even hated. He cared for the Roman rites most diligently and scorned foreign
ones. He performed the office of chief pontiff. He often heard cases at Rome
and in the provinces, calling into his counsel consuls and praetors and the
best senators. He drained Lake Fucinus. He appointed four men of consular rank
as judges throughout all Italy. When he came to Africa it rained at his
arrival after five years, and therefore he was loved by the Africans.

When he had traversed all parts of the world bareheaded, and often in the
heaviest rains and cold, he fell into an illness that kept him in bed. Made
anxious about a successor, he first thought of Servianus, whom afterward, as
we have said, he forced to die, and likewise of Fuscus, because, stirred by
omens and portents, Fuscus hoped for the empire.

He held Platorius Nepos in the utmost detestation, whom he had once loved so
much that when Hadrian came to him in his illness he was refused admission, a
thing not left unpunished. Led by suspicions, he treated Terentius Gentianus
in the same way, and this man more violently, because he then saw him loved by
the senate. Last of all he detested all those of whose rule he had thought, as
if they would be emperors.

And he so far checked all the force of his inborn cruelty until at the Villa
at Tibur he nearly came to his end by a flow of blood. Then freely he forced
Servianus to die as a seeker of empire, because he had sent a dinner to the
palace slaves, because he had sat on a royal seat placed beside the couch,
because as a man of ninety he had gone forth upright to the soldiers' posts.
Many others were killed either openly or by ambush. And indeed his wife Sabina
died not without the story that poison had been given by Hadrian.

Then he resolved to adopt Ceionius Commodus, son-in-law of Nigrinus the former
plotter, recommended to him by his beauty. He therefore adopted Ceionius
Commodus Verus against everyone's will and called him Helius Verus Caesar. For
his adoption he gave circus games and spent a donative on the people and the
soldiers. He honoured him with the praetorship and at once set him over the
Pannonias, with the consulship decreed and with funds. He also designated this
same Commodus consul for the second time.

When he saw that the man was less than sound, he very often used to say:

/We have leaned on a falling wall and lost four hundred million sesterces,
which we gave to the people and the soldiers for the adoption of Commodus./

Commodus, however, because of his health could not even give thanks in the
senate to Hadrian for the adoption. At last, having taken an antidote too
freely, as his health grew worse, he died in his sleep on the very Kalends of
January. For that reason Hadrian forbade him to be mourned, on account of the
vows.

And when Helius Verus Caesar was dead, Hadrian, as a most grim illness came
on, adopted Arrius Antoninus, who was afterward called Pius, and that on the
condition that he should adopt two for himself, Annius Verus and Marcus
Antoninus. These are they who afterward, the first two Augusti together,
governed the state. And Antoninus, they say, was called Pius because he
supported his father-in-law, worn with age, with his hand. Others say this
surname was given him because he had snatched many senators from Hadrian
already grown savage; others, because he had paid Hadrian great honours after
death.

A great many then grieved that Antoninus's adoption had been made, especially
Catilius Severus, prefect of the city, who was preparing the empire for
himself. When this was revealed he received a successor and was stripped of
his rank.

Hadrian, however, already seized by a last weariness of life, ordered a slave
to run him through with a sword. When this had been revealed and had come even
to Antoninus's knowledge, the prefects and his son came in to him and asked
that he bear the necessity of the disease with an even mind, Antoninus saying
that he would be a parricide if he, the adopted son, allowed Hadrian to be
killed. Hadrian, angry at them, ordered the author of the disclosure put to
death, who was nevertheless saved by Antoninus.

He at once wrote his will, and yet he did not neglect the acts of the state.
And after the will he tried again to kill himself; the dagger being taken
away, he grew more savage.

He also asked for poison from his physician, who killed himself so that he
should not give it.

At that season there came a certain woman who said she had been warned in a
dream to tell Hadrian not to kill himself, because he would be well. When she
had not done it, she had been blinded. She had been ordered again to say the
same things to Hadrian and to kiss his knees, and she would recover her eyes
if she did this. When she had fulfilled the dream she recovered her eyes,
after she had washed them with the water that was in the shrine from which she
had come. There also came from Pannonia a certain old blind man to Hadrian in
his fever and touched him, by which act he himself recovered his eyes and the
fever left Hadrian, though Marius Maximus records that these things were done
by pretence.

After this Hadrian made for Baiae, leaving Antoninus at Rome to govern. When
he made no progress there, Antoninus was summoned, and in his sight Hadrian
died at Baiae itself on the sixth day before the Ides of July. Hated by all,
he was buried at Cicero's villa at Puteoli.

At the very time of his death he also forced Servianus, in his ninetieth year,
as was said above, to die, so that he should not outlive him and, as Hadrian
thought, rule. For slight offences he ordered a great many killed, whom
Antoninus saved. And dying, he is said to have made these verses:

/Little soul, wandering, charming, guest and companion of the body, to what
places will you go now, pale, stiff, naked? Nor will you give your jokes as
you used to!/

He made verses of this kind, and not many better, and Greek ones too.

He lived sixty-two years, five months, seventeen days. He ruled twenty years,
eleven months.

* Chapter 26

:END:

He was tall in stature, well-kept in appearance, with hair curled by the comb,
a full beard, so as to cover the natural scars on his face, of a robust build.

He rode and walked a great deal and always exercised himself with arms and the
javelin. In the hunt he very often killed a lion with his own hand. In
hunting, though, he broke his collarbone and a rib. He always shared the hunt
with his friends.

At dinner he always presented tragedies, comedies, Atellan farces,
harp-players, readers, and poets, according to the occasion. He built up the
Villa at Tibur wonderfully, so that in it he inscribed the most famous names
of provinces and places, calling them, for instance, the Lyceum, the Academy,
the Prytaneum, Canopus, the Painted Porch, Tempe. And, so as to omit nothing,
he even fashioned the underworld.

He had these signs of death. On his last birthday, when he was commending
Antoninus, the bordered toga slipped down of its own accord and bared his
head. The ring on which his own image was carved slipped from his finger of
its own accord. On the day before his birthday some person came howling to the
senate, against whom Hadrian was so moved as if the man were speaking of his
death, though no one understood his words. Likewise when he wished to say in
the senate

/after my son's death,/

he said

/after my own./

He dreamed besides that he had obtained a sleep-bringing draught from his
father, and likewise he dreamed that he had been overpowered by a lion.

After his death many things were said by many people. The senate wished his
acts to be made void, and he would not have been called divine if Antoninus
had not asked.

Antoninus established a temple for him in place of a tomb at Puteoli, and a
five-year contest, and priests, and a priestly college, and many other things
that would belong to the honour of a kind of divinity. For this reason, as was
said above, many think he was called Antoninus Pius.
