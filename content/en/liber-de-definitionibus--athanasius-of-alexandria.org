* On Definitions

OF THE SAME, ON DEFINITIONS. Various definitions according to the tradition
and faith of the catholic Church, collected from Clement and other holy men
and blessed Fathers; which he who wishes, by the help of God, to preside over
the Word of truth must pursue and store in the breast before every other
lesson, and every man who thinks orthodoxly, and has sincere faith toward God.
And for a more exact tradition one must know this, that every inquiry or
question whatsoever has three questionings and examinations: such as what this
thing is, and according to what this has been said, and (in how many ways)
this name is understood. And when we ask, What is this? it is clear that we
seek the definition of the thing; but when we say, According to what has this
been said? we demand the etymology of the name. And when we say, In how many
ways is this name understood? it is clear that we seek how many are the
significations of the name. He therefore who attempts to dogmatize outside
knowledge of the definitions differs in nothing from a blind traveler
wandering here and there. It must therefore be said that the examination of
the aforesaid modes is to be made in three ways. The definition of definitions
is he who defines all things, but is himself defined by none; so that God
alone is the definition of definitions. A definition is a concise word
declarative of the nature of the underlying thing. And they are called
definitions, as being sights and eyes of the things, and as marking off
concisely the power of the intelligible. For man is an essence composed of
flesh and soul, and of word and blood, and of elements, and of many other
things. But we bound these, and say concisely through the definition, that Man
is a rational mortal animal, receptive of mind and of knowledge. And again the
complete name of man is said to be upright-walking. But the namers cut the
many words, and named him man for the sake of brevity. And man is called
upright-walking, because the irrational animals, walking, bending the head,
look down toward the earth; but man walking looks and contemplates the things
above. And in syncope he is named man. Again man is called so, because of
looking up with the face; that is, because of contemplating upward the
operation of our eyes. For the eye and the mind always look upward. And for
this reason it has been called a definition: because it bounds and cuts the
many words, and makes the long speech concise. There are then many and
numberless definitions concerning every thing. But from many we insert a few
of the more =28.536= principal, because of the indolence of men. Yet this is
to be known and believed, that he who laboriously practices the definitions
will be able easily in disputations to stop the mouths of rhetors and of
formidable talkers. For when he who is opposite is asked, What is a
definition? and according to what has definition been said? and in how many
ways is definition understood? he will be at a loss toward these things, and
is then put to shame as knowing nothing. Three things are by essence unknown
and undefined to men, God, angel, and soul, known according to essence to God
alone. What is definable? and what undefined? and what hard to define? and
what easy to define? Definable is an untransgressed standing; such as heaven
and earth, and the sea, which does not overstep its bounds. Undefined is the
only uncreated, God. Hard to define is the created and undefined; such as
angel, soul, and demon; or hard to define is that which is known not wholly,
but in part; such as the tree of life. Easy to define is that which is handled
and seen; such as bread and the like. What is God? and according to what has
God been said? and in how many ways is God understood? God is an uncaused
essence and a superessential cause of every essence; and he is named from
theein, that is, to run. But the running is not to be understood as a passage
from place to place, but that the providence of God and the operation extend
through all things; or from aithein, that is, to burn; or from contemplating
all things; for nothing of the things that are escapes his overseeing power.
And the appellation God is said in two ways in the divine Scripture, by nature
and by grace. God by nature and essence is God; but by grace and by
appointment the righteous also are called gods. For the Scripture says: "I
said: You are gods and sons of the Most High." And Father has been said,
either as keeping all things, or as keeping and establishing his own children
as a father. And Son is said, as if one should say: Such as the Father is in
essence, such also is the Son; whether uncreated from uncreated, or mortal
from mortal. And Spirit has been said, as if every inclination, swiftly
inclining and moving everywhere. And spirit has been said in the divine
Scripture in four ways, the Holy Spirit, spirit the angel, spirit the soul,
spirit wind; and sometimes also the mind has been called spirit. Everlasting
is that which always abides. And the everlasting is not also called without
beginning; but that without beginning is also called everlasting. And without
beginning is said of that which has no beginning; and this is God. Therefore
he is called both without beginning and everlasting, that is, unending. But
the angels and the souls are not called without beginning; for they received a
beginning and were created by God. But they are called everlasting, because
they will live immortal. =28.537= A property is said to be that which exists
peculiarly in the nature, and exists in no other essence at all; as in man the
capacity to laugh. For there is not laughter in any other nature whatsoever.
What is nature called? Nature has been said, because of having grown and
being, just as also essence, as being and being known in truth. Nature again
has been said, because of growing and sprouting. Nature and essence and genus
and form are one. But person and character and hypostasis and particular and
individual are one and the same. For nature is one thing and hypostasis
another. If then someone says to you that In the Godhead how many natures do
you confess? say one, but three hypostases. But if he says to you concerning
the incarnate economy, how many natures do you confess? say two; but one the
hypostasis. For Christ had the two essences and natures unchangeable and
unimpaired, the Godhead and the manhood in one hypostasis, being known as
perfect God and perfect man. And for this reason we confess two natures in one
hypostasis, the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ and God. But in theology
we confess one nature of the Holy Trinity, but three hypostases. For of Father
and Son and Holy Spirit there is one nature, and one essence, and one
authority, and kingdom, and power; but three hypostases, and three characters,
and three persons; that is, Father and Son and Holy Spirit. There is one
Godhead known in three persons. It is necessary then to say more generally
what essence is, and what hypostasis is. Essence, and genus, and nature, and
form, as has been said, are one; but person and character and hypostasis, and
the individual, and the particular, are one. For nature can have in itself
more hypostases. For we men, being from one body and one breath all, are of
one nature and essence. But hypostases are many. For hypostasis is called so
for this reason, from standing in the essence. And nature and essence are
called universal, but hypostasis particular. And universal is said of that
which includes and contains all things as a whole; but particular of that
which has some part of essence. For example, if you say, Man, behold you have
said every man; for this is universal and essential, and all men are so
called, and they are of the same essence. But if you say, Peter and Paul or
John, then you have signified one man, because this is particular. For the
common is, if you say, Man, since all men are named. But the particular is the
name of each. And this also befits him who wishes to know the nature of
things, that he may know which is the particular, and which the universal. For
not only in men, but also in every thing, heavenly and earthly, this is to be
found. And let us speak more clearly; for these things are necessary for those
who are about to inquire. And we speak thus. If you say, The angel, you have
signified the whole essence of the angels; for this name is common to the
angels and essential, =28.540= and all angels are so called. But if you say,
Michael, Gabriel, you have said one hypostasis, that is, one angel. For nature
is called the angelhood; but hypostasis is the appellation of each one angel.
For humanity is one common nature, but hypostases are Peter, Paul and Thomas,
and the remaining persons and characters. Nature then is the principal and
generative of the hypostases. And for this reason we will say again: if you
say, A good nature is the angel, you have signified their whole essence. But
if you say, Great is Gabriel, you have said one hypostasis from all. And if
you say, The bird, behold you have declared the whole essence of the birds;
but if you say, The dove, the turtledove, the nightingale, the peacock, and
the swan, behold these are hypostases. And the same rule of definitions befits
not only the intellectual and rational and ensouled, but also through every
soulless and insensate and unmoved nature; as for example I say, if you say
that God made the light, behold you have signified the whole essence of the
luminaries. This essence then is cut into its hypostases; for it is divided
into sun, into moon and into stars, into lamps, into candles, and into other
various things. Likewise also in winds: if you say, Spirit, you have signified
the whole nature of the winds. But if you say, South, or north, or euroclydon,
these are hypostases. Again if you say, The tree, you have signified the whole
essence of the trees; but if you say, The fig, the walnut, the chestnut, the
almond, you have said one hypostasis of the trees. Again if you say, The
water, you have said the whole nature of the waters; but if you say, The sea,
the lake, the well, the spring, you have said one hypostasis of the waters.
Again if you say, The river, you have said all the rivers; but if you say, the
Maeander, the Euphrates, the Nile, then you have said one hypostasis of the
rivers. And in the remaining things likewise one must thus search out all
things, and according to the manner in which they were written think and
recognize, and distinguish what nature is, and what hypostasis is. For nature
is the root and the fountain of the things; but hypostasis is a part of the
nature. For these things are necessary for those who wish to know and search
out exactly the things in the world. Concerning will, and property, and
operation, and concerning union and consubstantial. What is will? Will is an
appetite of an intellectual and rational essence toward what is desired. For
everything intellectual, it is clear, is also volitive; for will is said
according to running exceedingly, or as a taking of the willed, or as a
holding. And will is contemplated in three ways, divine, angelic, and psychic;
since these three natures alone are volitive. And we find will said in the
Scripture also in three ways, divine, the natural or the middle, and third the
fleshly, which signifies the diabolic. Divine will is the commandments of God.
And natural will is the working of the will of God. And fleshly will is the
disobedience of the divine law. These three wills are in man. And there is
also another natural will, which is called common to all men. And this is the
=28.541= love of life. For all men love life, and to see the light. Behold
also the common will. And there are also other wills in us, which are called
gnomic. For one man wishes to live privately, another to farm; and another to
sail, another to rest, and another to travel, another to keep watch, and
another to sleep, and the rest likewise; we men have many gnomic wills. But we
all have one natural will, to love life, that is, the immortality which we
received from the beginning. What is called a property. A property is called
so because they are proper together; for the properties of the natures and of
the hypostases are inseparable from them. A property is that which is known
peculiarly in some nature and is not found in another. As for example I say:
In God there is the pre-eternal, the uncreated, the uncircumscribed, which do
not exist in any other essence whatsoever. A property of the angels is the
passionless and immortal, the hymn-singing, the unsilenced. And a property of
man properly is the composite from mortal and immortal nature, known in no
other essence whatsoever. And these are natural properties; others also are
subordinate, such as whiteness, blackness, tallness, shortness, grey-eyedness,
swiftness, sluggishness, and the like, which are not found in all men. For not
all men are swift, nor all fair-haired. But the natural properties are found
equally and in the whole species; for all men likewise are mortal, and subject
to death, rational, broad-nailed, upright-walking. And there are also others,
which are not called properties properly, but accidents. And these come to be
and cease to be; such as infancy, health, disease, heat, cold; and simply
everything that can be changed is called an accident. Concerning operation.
Operation is, as the holy Gregory of Nyssa says, the natural power and
movement of each. For each essential thing has its own operation; such as the
intellectual the thinking, the sensitive the sensing, the movable the
movement, the winged the flying, the swimming the swimming, the vegetative the
sprouting, the creeping the creeping, the illuminating the illumination, the
generative the generation; which again, when the operation is taken away, the
nature itself also is taken away and perishes. For when the burning is taken
away, the fire is quenched; when the movement of the animal is taken away, the
animal perishes; when the thinking is taken away, the intellectual of the soul
perishes and its rational. And the paradox is that even certain different
natures, being deprived of their natural operations, give birth to other
operations. Such as the stone being burned both loses the natural operation,
and becomes =28.544= unslaked lime, and possesses a burning operation. And the
cedar likewise is deprived of the vegetative operation, and possesses a
healing one, making the cedria destructive of the worms. Therefore the prudent
must look at the appellations of the things that are, and examine, that they
may be able to find how many more operative appellations, but not natural
namings, every existence has, having received these from their own operations
and having them and being so called. For, as we have said before, just as God
is named from contemplating all things, and angel from his angelic operation
and ministry, and man from looking up with the face; so also in the rest; for
a virgin is so called, because of putting to death the burning of the flesh;
and a dove is so called for this reason, that it flies firmly; and a deer
because of catching the serpents; a swallow, because of shaking the lips in
speaking; a serpent, that is, he who spoke, that is, who once spoke to Eve; a
gazelle, from seeing sharply, or as seeing; a turtledove, she who keeps the
offspring, that is, her own kind; a nightingale, because of always singing,
both in summer and in winter; a vulture, because of circling heights; a horse,
from bounding with the feet; an eagle always-yeared, because of being often
renewed and living many (years); a bird, that which stretches the wing; a
river, as having a potable operation; a torrent, because it flows in winter;
nature, from growing the hypostases; a plant, because of walking upward; just
as also man, from looking upward, or as seeing. And the soul also has the
appellation of soul from its own operation; for to cool is said to be to give
life; therefore from the life-giving operation it is called soul, because of
giving life to the body; and there are also numberless others, which we pass
over, that we may not say all things according to their own appellations.
Concerning union. What is union? and in how many ways is union understood?
Union is a communal concurrence of things that stand apart; and union has been
said because of uniting, that is, of thrusting together and mixing the things.
And union is said in five ways, confusive, divisive, relative, thetic, and the
holy union of Christ, which beyond these has been said according to
hypostasis. And confusive union is said, as that of wine and of water.
Divisive, as of man toward man. Relative, as of gold toward gold. But the
union of Christ beyond these has been said according to hypostasis. And union
according to hypostasis is the self-subsistent concurrence of the two natures
in the womb of the holy Theotokos; for neither did the body subsist there
before the God Word, nor the soul; but flesh at once, and God Word at once.
Rational ensouled flesh subsisted in him, just as, as it seems to me, also in
our conception, the soul concurs self-subsistent with the body. For neither
does a body by =28.545= itself consist, nor does the soul preexist the body,
being a prefiguration of the union of the Lord. Concerning consubstantial.
What is consubstantial, and according to what has consubstantial been said?
and in how many ways the consubstantial? Consubstantial is that which exists
unvaryingly of the same essence and operation. For it is called consubstantial
for this reason, that it possesses the same essence and power. And
consubstantial has been said in five ways, and of the same genus, and kindred,
and of the same nature, and of the same tribe. And consubstantial is that
which is in the same essence, but having some difference, such as a rotten
stone, and a hard stone; and they are consubstantial, that is, of one essence.
And there is also wood of the palm, and wood of ebony; likewise also flesh of
a camel, and flesh of a fish. And these are called consubstantial, because
they are of the same essence. Just as all men are of the one essence; but they
have a difference of being, one tall, and another short; another strong, and
another wretched. But nevertheless they are of one essence, I mean of soul and
body. And of another essence is said that which exists of another essence, and
is in no way akin to the other, but is altogether alien and in every way
unlike that, such as water and fire; for the essence of fire is one, and that
of water another. And for this reason it is called of another essence, because
it exists of another nature and of another kind; as man and God, and angel,
and clay, and sun, and grass. For these are in every way unlike one another,
therefore they are also called of another essence; as if because the one does
not partake of the essence of the other; but that of the one is one, and that
of the other another, as has been said. Concerning soul and mind. The soul is
said in two ways, one the irrational, which is that of the beasts; and another
the rational, which is that of men. And that of the beasts received the
creation from earth according to the command of God, as the divine Scripture
says: "And God said: Let the earth bring forth creeping things of living
souls." So that the animals received the soul from the earth. Therefore they
have the impulse and all the appetite toward earthly things alone, and they do
not think the things above. And how their soul came to be from the earth, let
us say. The earth according to the command of God gave flesh to the beasts;
and the flesh made blood. And the blood became a bestial soul for the animal,
so that again when the animal has died, the blood, having coagulated, is
changed into flesh. And the flesh, having been corrupted, being dissolved
again, becomes earth. And for this reason they are ignorant, and do not think
the things above. But man has the soul from the divine inbreathing, therefore
he also knows the divine things, and pursues the things above, and understands
the heavenly, and is rational and possessed of mind. But let no one suppose
that the spirit which God breathed into the man, that became soul; may it not
be; but that spirit created a soul. For if =28.548= that spirit were soul in
the man, perhaps the soul would have been from the essence of God. If the soul
existed from the essence of God, why is it not always unalterable,
unchangeable, as God is? for he says: "I myself am, and I have not been
altered." But the soul is at one time foolish, at another prudent; and at one
time a sinner, at another righteous; and at another time faithful, and at
another unfaithful; and at one time ignorant, at another knowing; and at one
time eager, at another sluggish. Having these things, how can it be said to be
from the essence of God? it is not so, it is not. But that inbreathing created
a soul in the man, rational and intellectual. Therefore the soul also
intellectually imagines the things above, and seeks and desires and longs for
the things from there, because it has the creation from there. But that of the
irrational is not so, but being earthly, it seeks the earthly things alone;
therefore it also dies together with the body. But the soul of man exists
always-living and unending. And the soul has three parts, rational, irascible,
and appetitive. And through the rational part it loves God; through the
irascible part it is angry at the demons, being manly against them; and
through the appetitive part it desires the eternal goods. And man has five
bodily senses, and five psychic; and the bodily are: sight, hearing, smell,
taste, and touch. And in sight white and black are contemplated, and in
hearing the sharp and the heavy; and in smell the fragrant and the
foul-smelling; and in taste the sweet and the bitter; and in touch the hard
and the soft and the hot and the cold; and the sense-organs of the soul are
these: mind, thought, opinion, imagination, and sensation. And the organ of
each sense is called a sense-organ, from keeping the sensation, the animal
being sensitive. And one must also know this, that every flesh is also called
a body; but not every body is also named flesh. And the stars are called
heavenly bodies, but not also heavenly fleshes. And body has been said in two
ways, a material body, and a subtle body. And the material body is that which
is held by hand, and is subject to corruption and death; but a subtle body is
the unholdable and unpalpable, such as that of the sun, of the moon, and of
the stars; and the thick and material body, which is called flesh, is composed
of four elements, of blood, of phlegm, of humor, and of bile, or as of air,
earth, fire, and water; that is, of hot, and cold, and moist, and dry. The sun
the hot and vital element; the air the hot and moist; the earth the dry; the
waters beget the moist element. These are the things from which man has been
composed, or rather the flesh of man; therefore also after death it is
dissolved into them again. Concerning the corruptible. What is corruptible?
and according to what has corruptible been said? and in how many ways has
corruptible been said. =28.549= And corruptible has been said in two ways,
bodily and psychically. And psychically, when someone sins, he is said to be
corrupted, as the Scripture says: "They were corrupted and became abominable
in iniquities;" but bodily it is said in three ways: corruption, destruction,
and utter destruction. And corruption is said of the soul being separated only
from the body and dying; and destruction is when the body is eaten by the
worms, and only the bones are left. Utter destruction is said when the bones
themselves also rot, and are consumed, and pass altogether into non-being. For
this we say also concerning our Lord Jesus Christ, that he received corruption
only; that is, death. But his all-holy body received destruction or utter
destruction not at all, but he rose on the third day, having become firstborn
from the dead. Why is he called firstborn? Because he rose first from Hades,
just as we also are about to rise at the second coming. And since Lazarus rose
before him, and the daughter of Jairus, and many others, why was none of them
called firstborn? Because those, having risen from the dead, died again. But
Christ, having risen, died no more. And what will come to be in us at the
resurrection, this has come to be also in him. For he first rose from the dead
the resurrection in incorruption, of which we also hope to partake according
to the future, which death does not succeed. What is word, and in how many
ways the word? The word has been said in three ways: an essential word, the
God Word, an interior word, that of the angels, and the word spoken in our
thought; and the uttered, that through the tongue, which is called a messenger
of thought. And word has been said from speaking. What is a Christian? A
Christian is a true rational house of Christ, constituted through good works
and right doctrines. And a heretic is a slanderer and accuser of truth. Heresy
is the false supposition of that which is not. Unchangeable is that which
always has itself in the same way. Natural is that which lies in the nature,
or that which is known as truly true. Against nature is what God has not so
made, or what he has not made at all, such as is sin and death. He says there
are three things spoken among men: that according to nature, that against
nature, and that above nature. And according to nature is marriage; against
nature fornication; =28.552= above nature virginity. And again according to
nature is wealth justly gathered; against nature greed; above nature poverty.
Likewise again according to nature is measured food; against nature gluttony;
above nature fasting. Likewise again according to nature is peace; against
nature disturbance; above nature love toward those who are hostile. And in the
remaining things likewise. Etymology is the correctness of the power of the
name, interpreted from the mind itself; such as, According to what is peace?
According to the mind being at rest. Just as again disturbance, because of
being poured more easily; and error according to the mind turning aside.
Temperance, either from thinking soundly, or from guarding the body from
filth. Fornication either because of inflaming youth, or because of maiming,
or as blinding the mind, or because of nodding from afar. And in the remaining
things likewise. The body, having the composition from the elements, receives
also dissolution, and vanishes. Therefore the bodies both of men and of
animals happen to be easily corruptible and easily mortal, being composed of
four elements; I mean earth and water, and fire, and air. And the things
composed from these always come to be and always are corrupted, the
composition of the elements being dissolved, and each dissolving toward its
own place. But the elements themselves, being simple and uncompounded, have
permanence, and until the consummation are undissolved. Concerning the soul.
And concerning the soul we say, that there are five passions in it, which are
called psychic passions. And they are these: anger, grief, fear, care, and
envy. He therefore who has the reasoning prudent can master these. And the
soul exists by itself hot, as a burning fire. As therefore the fire boils the
things thrown into a cauldron, so also the fiery soul thins and consumes the
foods sent through in the belly. For it is called soul, not because the
essence is cold, as some of the simpler have supposed; but because from much
heat it dries up the moist things; as one may be fully assured also from the
operation of the sun. For being hot and fiery, it dries mud and draws up all
moisture. And that the soul is hot by nature, be persuaded also from those who
are dying. For in the hour in which the soul is separated from the body, all
the members are found cold and ice-like, no less than a wintry frost. For as
long as the soul has the food in abundance, the body flourishes and thrives.
But whenever it is in want of foods, it eats and destroys the body itself. For
the body cannot without food hold out toward the heat of the soul, just as
neither a bronze vessel, standing upon a furnace, can bear without water the
violence of the fire; for it is scorched and is completely dissolved. So then
also the cauldron of the body, not having the food, =28.553= being burned by
the fiery soul, perishes altogether. And if ever someone should dispute with
you concerning the soul, ask him: Tell me, does the soul of man lie in the
whole body, or in one place? And if he says to you that in the whole body, say
to him: And how do they cut the hand of the man, or the foot, and the soul is
not also cut, but the man still lives, and does not die? But if he says to
you, It lies in one part, say again: And how are the other parts of the man
without soul, and how can the man have a soulless member? And how is it
possible to live without soul? For everything that does not have a soul is
dead; and you do not know what you are saying. But I will tell you, in what
place, and in what manner, and where the soul rests. It is established in
three places of the body, and as the ray of the sun occupies one place, and
enters the house, yet lights the whole house; so also the soul, established in
the three parts of the body, gives life to the whole house of the body. And
the soul dwells in the heart, and in the hinder part of the head, which is
named cotylon, and in the royal veins. In these three parts then the soul
dwells. Therefore in whatever hour the heart is wounded, or much blood flows
from the royal vein, or the head is struck and cut by a sword, immediately the
soul is separated from the body, and the body is found dead. And the soul,
dwelling in these three parts, imparts to the whole body its own vital power.
