* On the Progress of the Gospel

:CUSTOM_ID: h1

To those who do not use as they ought the apostolic saying that says, "Whether
in pretence or in truth, Christ is proclaimed," and concerning humility.

=51.311= αʹ. Having recalled the other day the Pharisee and the publican, and
having yoked two chariots in our discourse from virtue and from vice, we
showed each, how great is the gain of humility, and how great the harm of
arrogance. For this, even when joined with righteousness and fasts and tithes,
fell behind; but that, even when yoked with sin, outstripped the Pharisee's
pair, though it had a worthless charioteer as well. For what is worse than a
publican? Yet nevertheless, since he crushed his soul and called himself a
sinner, which he was, he surpassed the Pharisee who had fasts to mention and
tithes, and was free from every vice. For what reason, and why? Because even
if he was free from greed and robbery, he had vainglory, the mother of all
evils, and arrogance rooted in his soul. For this reason Paul also exhorts and
says: Let each one prove his own work, and then he will have his boast in
himself, and not in the other. But that man came forward as accuser of the
whole inhabited world, and said that he was better than all men that are. And
yet even if he had set himself before only ten, or five, or two, or even one,
not even this would have been tolerable; but now he not only set himself
before the inhabited world, but also accused all. Therefore he fell behind in
the race. And just as a ship that has run through countless waves and escaped
many storms, then, in the very mouth of the harbor, striking some reef, loses
all the treasure stored within: so also this Pharisee, having endured the
toils of fasting and of all the rest of virtue, since he did not control his
tongue, suffered a heavy shipwreck in the harbor itself. For to go away from
prayer, from which one ought to have gained, rather having been so greatly
harmed, is nothing other than to suffer shipwreck in harbor.

βʹ. Knowing these things, then, beloved, even if we ascend to the very summit
of virtue, let us count ourselves last of all, having learned that arrogance
can bring down from the heavens themselves the one who does not take heed, and
that humility can raise up from the very abyss of sins to a height the one who
knows how to be moderate. For this set the publican before the Pharisee; but
that, I mean arrogance and pride, overcame even an incorporeal power, the
devil; while humility and the acknowledgment of one's own sins brought the
robber into paradise before the apostles. And if those who confess their own
sins procure for themselves so great a confidence, those who are conscious of
many good things in themselves, yet humble their own soul, of how many crowns
will they not obtain? For when sin has been joined with humility, it runs with
such ease as to overtake and outstrip righteousness that is with arrogance. If
then you join it with righteousness, where will it not arrive? How many
heavens will it not pass through? It will stand, surely, beside the very
throne of God in the midst of the angels with much confidence. Again, if
arrogance, yoked with righteousness, by the excess and weight of its own
wickedness was able to drag down that one's confidence, if it has been joined
with


sin, into how great a gehenna will it not be able to hurl the one who has it?
I say these things not that we may neglect righteousness, but that we may flee
arrogance; not that we may sin, but that we may be moderate. For humility is
the foundation of our philosophy. And even if you build countless things upon
it, even almsgiving, even prayers, even fasting, even every virtue, if this
has not been laid down first, all will be built in vain and to no purpose, and
will fall easily, like that building that was set upon the sand. For there is
nothing, nothing of our achievements, that does not need this; there is
nothing that can stand without this. But even if you mention temperance, even
virginity, even contempt of money, even anything whatsoever, all =51.313= is
unclean and accursed and abominable when humility is absent. Let us therefore
take it with us everywhere, in words, in deeds, in thoughts, and with this let
us build these things.

γʹ. But the things concerning humility have been said sufficiently, not
according to the worth of the achievement; for no one will be able to hymn it
according to its worth; but according to the understanding of your love. For I
know well that even from the few things that have been said you will draw it
to yourselves with much eagerness. But since it is necessary also to make
clear and plain the apostolic saying that was read today, which to many seems
to furnish a pretext for sloth, so that some, procuring from it a cold
defense, may not neglect their own salvation: come, let us bring the discourse
to this. What then is the saying? Whether in pretence, he says, or in truth,
Christ is proclaimed. This many carry about simply and as it happens, reading
neither the things before nor the things after these; but, having cut it off
from the sequence of the remaining members, they put it forward to the more
slothful for the destruction of their own soul. For attempting to lead them
away from the sound faith, then seeing them afraid and trembling, as though it
were not without danger to do this, and wishing to dissolve their fear, they
bring forward this apostolic saying, saying: Paul allowed this, when he said:
Whether in pretence or in truth, let Christ be proclaimed. But it is not so,
it is not. For first he did not say, let him be proclaimed, but, he is
proclaimed; and there is a great interval between this and that. For to say,
let him be proclaimed, belongs to one who legislates; but to say, he is
proclaimed, belongs to one reporting what is taking place. And that Paul does
not legislate that there should be heresies, but leads away all who attend to
him, hear what he says: If anyone evangelizes you besides what you received,
let him be anathema, even if I, even if an angel from heaven. He would not
have anathematized both himself and an angel, if he knew the matter to be
without danger. And again: For I am jealous over you with a jealousy of God,
he says; for I betrothed you to one husband as a pure virgin. But I fear lest
somehow, as the serpent deceived Eve in his craftiness, so your thoughts may
be corrupted from the simplicity that is toward Christ. See, he has set down
simplicity also, and has not given pardon. For if there were pardon, there was
no danger; and if there was no danger, Paul would not have been afraid; nor
would Christ have commanded that the tares be burned, if it were a matter of
indifference to attend to this one, and to that one, and to another, and
simply to all.

δʹ. What then is the thing that is said? I wish to relate to you the whole
history from a little above; for one must know in what circumstances Paul was
when he was writing these things. In what circumstances, then, was he then? In
a prison and in chains and in unbearable dangers. From where is this clear?
From the letter itself. For above this he says: But I want you to know,
brethren, that the things concerning me have come rather unto the progress of
the Gospel, so that my bonds have become manifest in Christ in the whole


praetorium, and to all the rest; and that most of the brethren, having become
confident by my bonds, dare more abundantly to speak the word without fear.
And it was Nero who had thrown him into the prison then. For just as some
=51.314= robber, having entered a house, while all are sleeping, stealing
everything, when he sees someone who has lit a lamp, both quenches the light
and kills the lamp-bearer, so that it may be permitted him to steal and snatch
the things of others with security: so also Nero Caesar then, like some robber
and housebreaker, while all were sleeping a deep and insensible sleep,
snatching the things of all, breaking through marriages, overturning houses,
displaying every kind of wickedness, when he saw Paul lighting a lamp
throughout the inhabited world, the word of teaching, and convicting his
wickedness, was eager both to quench the preaching and to destroy the teacher,
so that it might be permitted him to do everything with authority; and having
bound that holy man he threw him into prison. Then, therefore, the blessed
Paul was writing these things. Who would not be struck? Who would not marvel?
Or rather, who would be struck and marvel according to its worth at that noble
and heaven-reaching soul, because, bound in Rome and shut up, from so great a
distance he was writing to the Philippians? For you know how great is the
interval between Macedonia and Rome. But neither the length of the road, nor
the multitude of time, nor the crowd of affairs, nor the danger and the
successive terrors, nor anything else, cast out the love and the memory of the
disciples, but he had them all in his mind; and his hands were not so bound by
the chains as his soul was bound and nailed by the longing for the disciples;
which very thing, making clear in the preface of the letter, he was saying:
Because I have you in my heart, both in my bonds and in the defense and
confirmation of the Gospel. And just as a king, having gone up to the throne
at dawn and sitting in the royal courts, at once receives countless letters
from every side: so also that man, sitting in the prison as in royal courts,
both received and sent far more letters, the nations from every side referring
all the affairs concerning themselves to his wisdom; and he administered so
many more affairs than the one who was reigning, as he had been entrusted with
a greater rule. For God did not place into his hands those who inhabit the
country of the Romans only, but also all the barbarians, and land and sea. And
making this clear he was saying to the Romans: But I do not want you to be
ignorant, brethren, that I often purposed to come to you, and was hindered
until now, that I might have some fruit among you also, as also among the rest
of the nations; I am a debtor both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to wise
and to foolish. Each day, then, he was taking thought, now for the
Corinthians, now for the Macedonians, how the Philippians were, how the
Cappadocians, how the Galatians, how the Athenians, how those who inhabit
Pontus, how all men. Yet nevertheless, having been entrusted with the whole
earth, he was anxious not only for entire nations, but also for one man; and
now he sent the letter on account of Onesimus, and now on account of the one
who had committed fornication among the Corinthians. For he did not consider
this, that the one who had sinned and needed protection was one; but that he
was a man, man the living creature most precious to God, and the one for whom
the Father did not spare even the Only-begotten.

εʹ. For do not say this to me, that so-and-so is a runaway, and a robber and a
thief, and full of countless evils, =51.315= or that he is poor and cast
aside, and cheap, and worthy of no account; but consider that for this one
also Christ died, and this is enough for you as a ground for all providence.
Consider what sort of person that one must be, whom Christ honored so much as
not to spare even his own blood. For if a king had chosen to sacrifice himself
for someone, we would not have sought another proof


that that man is great and much sought after by the king, I at least do not
think so; for the death would suffice to show the love of the one who died
toward him. But now it is not a man, not an angel, not an archangel, but the
Master of the heavens himself, the only-begotten Son of God himself, having
put on flesh, who gave himself up for us. Shall we not then do and busy
ourselves with everything, so that men so honored may enjoy all providence
from us? And what defense shall we have? What pardon? This very thing, at any
rate, Paul also was showing when he said: Do not destroy him with your food,
for whom Christ died. For wishing to put to shame those who despise the
brethren and overlook them as being weak, and to bring them to eagerness and
persuade them to care for their neighbors, he has set down instead of all
things the death of the Master. Sitting, then, in the prison he was writing to
the Philippians from so great a distance. For such is love according to God:
it is interrupted by none of human things, having its roots and its rewards
from above, from the heavens. And what does he say? But I want you to know,
brethren. Did you see providence for disciples? Did you see a teacher's care?
Hear also the disciples' affection toward the teacher, that you may see that
this was what made them strong and unconquerable, their being bound together
with one another. For if a brother helped by a brother is as a strong city,
much more would so many, bound together by the bonds of love, have beaten off
all the plot of the wicked demon. That Paul, then, was bound together with the
disciples, we no longer need even a proof, nor a word, seeing that even when
bound he was anxious for them, and every day he was also dying for them, being
inflamed with longing.

ʹ. And that the disciples also were bound together with Paul with all
exactness, and not men only but also women, hear what he says concerning
Phoebe: And I commend to you Phoebe our sister, being a deacon of the Church
that is in Cenchreae, that you may receive her in the Lord worthily of the
saints, and that you may stand by her in whatever matter she may need of you,
who has become a patroness of many, and of myself also. But here he testified
to her eagerness as far as patronage; but Priscilla and Aquila went even as
far as death for Paul, and concerning them he writes thus, saying: Aquila and
Priscilla greet you, who laid down their own neck for my soul, that is, unto
death. And concerning another again, writing to these same, he says: Because
he drew near unto death, having hazarded his soul, that he might fill up your
lack of the ministry toward me. Did you see how they loved the teacher? How
they looked to his relief before their own soul? For this reason no one
overcame them then. And I say these things, not that we may only hear, but
that we may also im =51.316= itate; and the discourse is stretched out not
toward those who are ruled only, but also toward the rulers, so that both the
disciples may display much care for the teachers, and the teachers may have
the same affection as Paul toward those who are subject, not only those
present, but also those who are far away. For Paul, inhabiting the whole
inhabited world as one house, thus took thought for the salvation of all, and
leaving aside all his own bonds and afflictions and blows and distresses, he
was overseeing and inquiring every day how the affairs of the disciples stood;
and often for this very thing alone he sent, now Timothy, now Tychicus; and
concerning the latter he says: That he may know the things concerning you, and
may comfort your hearts; and concerning Timothy: I sent him to you, no longer
enduring, lest somehow the tempter tempted you; and Titus again elsewhere, and
another to another place. For since he himself, by the constraint of the bonds


often held in one place, was not able to be with his own bowels, he was with
them through the disciples.

ζʹ. And then, therefore, being in bonds, he writes to the Philippians, saying:
But I want you to know, brethren, calling the disciples brethren. For such is
love: it casts out all inequality, and does not know superiority and rank, but
even if someone is higher than all, he comes down to the one who is humbler
than all; which Paul also did. But let us hear what he wants them to know:
That the things concerning me, he says, have come rather unto the progress of
the Gospel. Tell me, how and in what manner? Were you released from the bonds?
Did you lay aside the chain, and do you preach with security in the city? Did
you enter a church and stretch out long and many discourses concerning the
faith, and take many disciples and go away? Did you raise the dead, and were
you marveled at? Did you cleanse lepers, and were all struck? Did you drive
out demons, and were you exalted? None of these things, he says. How then did
the progress of the Gospel come to be? Tell me. So that my bonds, he says,
have become manifest in Christ in the whole praetorium, and to all the rest.
What are you saying? This then, this is the progress? This the increase? This
the growth of the preaching, that all learned that you have been bound? Yes,
he says. Hear at any rate what follows, that you may learn that the bonds not
only did not become a hindrance, but even a ground of greater confidence: So
that most of the brethren in the Lord, having become confident by my bonds,
dare more abundantly to speak the word without fear. What are you saying, O
Paul? Did the bonds not cast anxiety upon them, but courage? Not fear, but
longing? The things said have no sequence. I know this too. For these things
were not happening according to the sequence of human affairs, he says; but
the things that were coming to be were above nature, and the achievements were
of divine grace. For this reason what produced anxiety in the others, this
furnished courage in his case. For if someone, having taken a general and shut
him up, makes this manifest, he throws the whole camp into flight; and if
someone leads a shepherd away from the flock, he drives off the sheep with
much security. But it was not so in Paul's case, but quite the contrary. For
the general was bound, and the soldiers became more eager, and with greater
confidence they sprang upon the adversaries; the shepherd was shut up, and the
sheep were not being destroyed, nor were they being scattered.

ηʹ. Who has seen, who has heard of disciples receiving greater consolation in
the terrors of their teachers? How did they not dread? How were they not
afraid? How did they not say to Paul, Physician, heal yourself? Free yourself
from the intricate terrors, and then you will procure for us the countless
good things. How did they not say these things? How? Because they had been
taught by the grace of the Spirit that these things were not coming to be from
weakness, but from the permission of Christ, so that the truth might shine
more greatly, increasing through bonds and prisons and afflictions and
distresses and being raised to a greater magnitude. Thus the power of Christ
is perfected in weakness. For if the bonds had tripped Paul up and made him
more cowardly, either he or those belonging to him ought to have been at a
loss; but if they prepared him rather to be confident and led him into greater
glory, one must be struck and marvel, how through a thing that has dishonor
glory was procured for the disciple, how through a thing that casts in
cowardice courage and consolation came to be for all those. For who was not
struck at him then, seeing him wearing a chain? Then demons were running away
more, when they saw him spending time in a prison. For a diadem does not make
a royal head so splendid as the chain made his hands, not from its own nature,
but from the grace that bloomed upon them. For this reason much consolation


came to be for the disciples. For they saw the body bound, but the tongue not
bound; the hands compressed, but the word loosed, and running through the
whole inhabited world faster than the sun's ray. And this became a consolation
to them, as they learned through the deeds that none of the present terrors is
anything. For when the soul has been genuinely dyed with divine longing and
love, it turns toward none of the present things; but just as those who are
mad dare fire and iron and beasts and the sea and all, so also these, having
gone mad with a certain most beautiful and most spiritual madness, a madness
that comes from temperance, laughed at all the things that are seen. For this
reason, seeing the teacher bound, they leaped more, they rejoiced more, giving
proof through the deeds to the adversaries that they are from every side
unconquerable and unsubdued.

θʹ. Then, therefore, when the affairs were in these circumstances, some of
Paul's enemies, wishing to rekindle the war more harshly and to make the
tyrant's hatred toward him greater, pretended that they too were preaching,
and they preached the right and sound faith, for the sake of making the
doctrine increase more greatly; but they did this not wishing to sow the
faith, but so that Nero, having learned that the preaching is increasing and
the doctrine is advancing, might lead Paul more quickly to the pit. There
were, then, two schools, of Paul's disciples and of Paul's enemies; the one
preaching from truth, the other from contentiousness and from hatred toward
Paul. And making these things clear he was saying: Some preach Christ through
envy and strife, indicating those enemies; And some also through goodwill,
saying this concerning his own disciples. He said again concerning those: The
one from faction, the enemies; not purely, =51.318= not soundly, but Thinking
to bring affliction upon my bonds; but the others from love; again this
concerning his own brethren; Knowing that I am set for the defense of the
Gospel. What then? Only that in every way, whether in pretence or in truth,
Christ is proclaimed. So that this saying is taken up upon the heresies vainly
and to no purpose. For those who were preaching then were not preaching a
corrupted doctrine, but a sound and right faith. For if they were preaching a
corrupted doctrine, and teaching things other than Paul, what they wanted
would not have gone forward for them. And what did they want? That, the faith
having increased and Paul's disciples having become many, they might stir Nero
up to a greater war. But if they were preaching other doctrines, they would
not have made Paul's disciples many; and not doing this, they would not have
provoked the tyrant. He does not say, then, that they were introducing
corrupted doctrines, but that the cause from which they were preaching, this
was corrupted. For it is one thing to speak of the pretence of the preaching,
and another that the preaching itself is not sound. For the preaching does not
become sound when the doctrines are full of error; but the pretence does not
become sound when the preaching is sound, but those who preach do not preach
on account of God, but either toward enmity or toward the favor of others.

ιʹ. He does not say, then, that they were introducing heresies, but that they
were not preaching from a right pretence, nor through piety, what they were
preaching. For they were not doing this in order that they might increase the
Gospel, but in order that they might make war upon it, and throw him into
greater danger; for this reason he accuses them. And see how he has set it
down with exactness: Thinking, he says, to bring affliction upon my bonds. He
did not say, bringing, but, Thinking to bring, that is, supposing; showing
that even if those men suppose it, he himself is not so disposed, but even
rejoices on account of the increase of the preaching. He added, then, saying:
But I also rejoice in this and


shall rejoice; but if the doctrines had error, and those men were introducing
heresies, Paul would not have been able to rejoice. But since the doctrine was
sound and unadulterated, for this reason he says, I rejoice and shall rejoice.
For what if those men destroy themselves, doing this from hatred? Yet even
unwilling they increase my affairs. Did you see how great is Paul's power? How
he is captured by none of the devil's devices? And not only is he not
captured, but he even subdues him by these very things. For great indeed is
both the devil's cunning and the wickedness of those who serve him; for under
a pretext of thinking the same things, they wished to quench the preaching.
But He who takes the wise in their craftiness did not permit this to come to
be then. This very thing, at any rate, Paul was indicating when he said: But
to remain in the flesh is more necessary on our account, and having this
confidence I know that I shall remain and continue with you all. For those men
desire to cast me out of the present life, and they endure everything for
this; but God does not let me go, on your account. Remember, then, all these
things with exactness, so that you may be able with all wisdom to correct
those who use the Scriptures simply and as it happens and for the destruction
of their neighbors. And we shall be able both to remember =51.319= the things
that have been said, and to correct others, if we always take refuge in
prayers, and entreat the God who gives a word of wisdom to give also an
understanding of hearing, and an exact and unconquerable guarding of this
spiritual deposit. For the things which we often are not able to achieve from
our own eagerness, these we shall be able to accomplish easily through
prayers, and I mean prayers that are continual. For one must always and
without ceasing pray, both the one in affliction and the one in ease, both the
one in terrors and the one who is in good things; the one in ease and in many
good things, that these may remain unmoved and unchanged and may never fall
over; and the one in affliction and in many terrors, that he may see some
useful change come to be for him, and that he may be changed into a calm of
consolation. Are you in a calm? Then entreat God that this calm remain firm
for you. Have you seen a storm rise up? Entreat God earnestly to carry aside
the surge and to make a calm from the storm. Have you been heard? Give thanks
for this, because you have been heard. Have you not been heard? Remain, that
you may be heard. For even if God sometimes postpones the gift, it is not
because he hates or turns away, but because by the delay of the gift he wishes
to hold you continually with himself, just as affectionate fathers also do;
for they too, by the postponement of the gift, contrive the continual
attendance of the more slothful children. You have no need of mediators before
God, nor of much running about, and of flattering others; but even if you are
deserted, even if you are without a patron, you yourself, having entreated God
through yourself, will obtain altogether. He is not so accustomed to assent
when entreated through others on our behalf, as through ourselves who are in
need, even if we are full of countless evils. For if in the case of men, even
if we have given countless offenses, when both at dawn, and at midday, and in
the evening we appear to those who have been grieved at us, by the continuity
and the continual meeting of the sight we easily dissolve their enmity, much
more would this come to be in the case of God.

ιβʹ. But you are unworthy? Become worthy by attendance. For that it is
possible even for the unworthy to become worthy from attendance, and that God
assents when entreated through ourselves rather than through others, and that
he often postpones the gift, not wishing us to be at a loss, nor to send us
away with empty hands, but that he may become the cause of greater good things
for us: these three things I shall try to make clear through the parable that
was read to you today. The Canaanite woman approached Christ, asking on behalf
of a daughter possessed by a demon, and crying out with much earnestness,
saying:


Have mercy on me, Lord, my daughter is badly demon-possessed. See, the woman
is a foreigner, and a barbarian, and outside the Jewish commonwealth. And what
else but a dog, and unworthy to receive the request? For it is not, he says,
good to take the children's bread and give it to the dogs. Yet nevertheless
from attendance she became worthy. For he not only brought her, being a dog,
into the nobility of the children, but also sent her away with many praises,
saying: O woman, great is your faith; be it unto you as you will. And when
Christ says, Great is the faith, seek no other proof of the greatness of soul
that was in the woman. Did you see how from attendance the woman became
=51.320= worthy, being unworthy? Do you wish to learn also that we accomplish
it when we entreat him through ourselves rather than through others? She cried
out, and the disciples coming say: Send her away, because she cries behind us;
and to them he says, I was not sent, except to the lost sheep of the house of
Israel; but when she herself approached through herself and persisted crying
out, and saying: Yes, Lord, for even the dogs eat from the table of their
masters; then he gave the grace, and says: Be it unto you as you will. Did you
see how, when those men were entreating, he thrust them aside; but when she
herself who needed the gift cried out, he assented? For to them he says, I was
not sent, except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel; but to this woman
he said, Great is your faith; be it unto you as you will. Again, at the
beginning and in the preface of the request he answered nothing; but when she
approached both once and a second time and a third, then he gave the grace,
persuading us through the end that he postponed the gift, not that he might
thrust her aside, but that he might show to us all the woman's endurance. For
if he postponed it in order to thrust her aside, he would not have given even
at the end; but since he was waiting to show to all her philosophy, for this
reason he was silent. For if he had given at once and at the beginning, we
would not have known the woman's courage. Send her away, he says, because she
cries behind us. And what does Christ do? You hear a voice, but I see the
mind; I know what she is about to say. I do not wish the treasure hidden in
her mind to escape notice, but I wait and am silent, that having uncovered it
I may set it in the midst, and make it manifest to all. ιγʹ. Having learned
all these things, then, even if we are in sins, and unworthy to receive, let
us not despair, knowing that by the attendance of the soul we shall be able to
become worthy of the request. And even if we are without a patron and
deserted, let us not give up, knowing that it is a great patronage to approach
God oneself through oneself with much eagerness. And even if he delays and
postpones toward the gift, let us not grow slack, having learned that the
delay and postponement is a token of care and loving-kindness. If we have thus
persuaded ourselves, and with a pained and warm soul and an aroused choice,
and such as that with which the Canaanite woman approached, let us also
approach him, even if we are dogs, even if we have done anything terrible
whatsoever, and we shall beat off our own evils, and we shall receive such
confidence as even to stand before others; just as this Canaanite woman also
not only herself enjoyed confidence and countless praises, but was also able
to snatch her little daughter from the unbearable terrors. For there is
nothing, nothing more powerful than a prayer that has been set on fire and is
