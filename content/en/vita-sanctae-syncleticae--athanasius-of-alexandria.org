LIFE AND CONDUCT OF THE HOLY AND BLESSED AND TEACHER
SYNCLETICA. All human
beings ought not to be uninitiated in good things. For thus, if they were
trained in the matters, they would have what is unharmed in life; and indeed
most of the useful things have escaped the more simple. And they have suffered
this from their understanding having been made more dull by negligence. For
often even pearls of great price have come to poor men. And they, having
untested skill concerning these things, have despised them as small things and
of no account. We then, having an infantile and untrained soul, coming upon
the present pearl, see nothing great in it, gazing only at the shape, but
being far from its nature as far as knowledge is concerned. But as little by
little we were taught its beauty from those nearby, a divine love was also
born in us toward what was seen; for the very facts kindled our understanding
toward the desire. But why do I speak of those present, or even number myself
with them, as if we knew something and were speaking about the celebrated and
blessed Syncletica? For I think all human nature falls short for a narration
of her good deeds. And if someone should attempt to say something about her,
he will be left far and infinitely short of what is sought, whether he is wise
or whether he is skilled. For just as those who wish to gaze at the sun injure
their eyes, so also those who try to look into her life, becoming dizzy from
the greatness of the achievements, yielding or even growing faint, undergo
confusion in the understanding. But we, tracking the things concerning her
according to our own power, and having heard cursorily from her contemporaries
the things according to her first life, and having been dimly illuminated from
the facts themselves, have come to writing, storing up for ourselves salvific
nourishments. For to speak of her according to her worth is not only
impossible for us, but also difficult for the many. She who is named from the
heavenly senate is of the country of Macedonia. For her ancestors, having
heard of the God-loving and Christ-loving character of the Alexandrians, were
coming from Macedonia to the city of the Macedonian =28.1489= . And when they
had set foot on the country, and finding the facts greater than the report,
they dwelt in it kindly. They did not rejoice in the multitude of peoples, nor
indeed did they marvel at the size of dwellings; but finding a single-minded
faith with sincere love, they set their own country second to the migration.
And the blessed Syncletica was also illustrious in family, and she had been
adorned with the other things considered delightful according to the world.
And there was with her also a sister of like mind at once, and two brothers,
themselves too brought up in a most solemn life; of whom the one, being in
childhood, died; and the other, having reached the twenty-fifth year, was
being urged by the parents toward marriage; and when all things had been
prepared for what was sought, and the customary contributions from the nation
were being performed, the young man flew away like a bird from snares, making
an exchange of the earthly bride for the blameless and free assembly of the
saints. And she, still being in her father's arms, was first training the soul
in love of God; and she did not so much make the cares of the body as she
watched the impulses of nature. For she was also most beautiful according to
the body, so that many suitors approached her at the


first age; being baited on the one hand by the abundance, and on the other by
the seemliness of the parents, and besides these being kindled by the beauty
of the girl herself. And so the parents were gladly urging the young woman
toward marriage, pressing this very thing, that from her the succession of the
family might be preserved for them. But she, chaste and noble in mind, in no
way consented to such counsels of the parents; but hearing of a worldly
marriage, she imagined a divine marriage; and overlooking many suitors, she
had her inclination toward the heavenly bridegroom alone. And one could see a
genuine disciple of the blessed Thecla, following her in the same teachings.
For of the two there was one suitor, Christ, and he himself was the bridesman
of both, Paul; and I think even the chamber did not change for them; for one
bridal room for them is the Church, and the same David sings over both the
solemn and divine songs. For with well-sounding cymbals he delights the souls
dedicated to God, and with drums and with ten-stringed psalteries he sends up
the most perfect ode. And Miriam brings the dancing women into these holy
marriages, saying: Let us sing to the Lord; for he has been glorified
gloriously. And the dishes of the divine supper are common to those who feast;
For taste, and see, that the Lord is kind. And the weave of the bridal
garments is one for them; For as many as were baptized into Christ have put on
Christ. Of like manner then was their love toward the Lord; for they were
deemed worthy of the same gifts; but they also contended in the same contests.
For the martyrdoms of the blessed Thecla have been unknown to no one, that she
contended through fire and untamed beasts =28.1492= ; and I think that the
virtuous labors and sweats of this one too do not escape the many. For if one
Savior was the one longed for by them, of necessity also one was the one
opposing them. And for Thecla I take the labors to have been milder; for the
wickedness of the enemy had been taken down, because he conversed with her
through things outside; but here he displays his wickedness more bitter, being
moved from within through his contrary and destructive thoughts. The weave of
varied clothing did not bewitch her eye, nor the various colors of costly
stones; a cymbal did not deceive her hearing, nor was a flute able to dissolve
the tension of her soul. Tears of parents did not soften her, nor any other
exhortation of kinsfolk; but having her reasoning like adamant, she was not
turned aside in mind; and shutting all her senses like windows, she conversed
with her own bridegroom in private, saying that scriptural saying: I am my
beloved's, and my beloved is mine. And if smoky and dark conversations were
present, she fled these, gathering herself into the inner treasuries of the
soul; but where there were luminous and beneficial exhortations, she stretched
all her understanding toward reception of what was said. And she did not
neglect the salvific medicine according to the body; for she had fasting so as
a friend that none of those present was equal to her. For she held this a
guard and foundation of the others. And if ever, necessity having come about,
she was fed outside the usual time, she underwent the contrary to those who
eat; for her face grew pale, and the bulk of the body collapsed; for when the
mover has been disgusted, the moved is also altered together. For however the
beginning is disposed, as a rule there follow it also the things hung from it.
For those to whom food both becomes pleasure and is offered, the bulk of the
body is flourishing; but for those to whom the contrary happens, the flesh is
without nourishment and thin. And the sick bear witness to my word. This
blessed one then, trying to tend the body as a sick thing, to the


soul she brought the flourishing; for she acted according to the Apostle
saying; For as much as our outer man is decaying, he says, so much is the
inner being renewed. Thus then she contended, escaping the notice of the many.
But when her parents had an end of life, being still more God-borne in the
divine mind, she takes also the sister with herself; for she had been deprived
of her sight; and she separates from the paternal houses; and she settles in a
tomb of some kinswoman of hers, removed from the city; and having sold all the
substance left to her, and having distributed it to the poor, having called
one of the presbyters, she cut off her own locks. Then =28.1493= she laid
aside all the adornment; for it is a custom for women to call the hair
adornment. And what was done was a symbol of her soul having become
unsuperfluous and pure. Then then she was first deemed worthy of the virginal
appellation. And having distributed all her substance to the poor, she said: I
have been deemed worthy of a great name; But what I shall give back as worthy
to the one who granted it, I do not have. For if in the case of outward
things, for the sake of a perishable dignity, they cast away all their own
substance; how much more, I having been deemed worthy of so great a grace, was
it not necessary along with the supposed possessions to betray even the body?
But why do I speak of giving possessions or body, when all things are his? For
the Lord's is the earth and the fullness of it. Having girt herself with
humility through these words, she was still. She had then even in the paternal
courts been sufficiently pre-trained in the labors; and having been brought
even in the very prime of the stadium, she advanced in the virtues. As many
then as approached this divine mystery untrained and unconsidered failed of
what was sought, not having foreseen the things according to each; but just as
those about to travel first take thought of the provisions, so also she,
having prepared herself as with provisions by the former labors, made the
journey toward the things above without fear; for having set down beforehand
the things toward the completion of the house, she was finishing her own tower
as most secure. And the work of dwellings according to history is constructed
from the outer materials; but she has done the contrary; for she did not
gather the outer materials, but rather emptied the inner ones. For having
given her money to the poor, and having left anger and remembrance of wrongs,
and having thrust away also envy and love of glory, she built her house upon
the rock, whose tower is conspicuous and the house unstormed. And why do I say
many things? for she was even surpassing through the beginnings those who were
in the habit of the solitary life. For just as the most gifted of children,
still being in the syllables, contend with those in age and having more time
with the teachers; so also she, boiling in the spirit, was outrunning the
rest. We are unable then to speak of her practical and ascetic life, since she
did not even allow anyone to become a spectator of this. For she did not want
those present to be heralds of her brave deeds. For she did not so much take
thought of doing well as of the guarding and hiding of this. And she did this
not being held by envy, but being held together by divine grace. For she bore
in mind that Dominical saying, which says that If your right hand does
anything, let your left not know. Thus hiding she was completing the things
proper to the profession. From the first age, and until the prime, she not
only fled the company of every man =28.1496= , but she also declined those of
the same kind for the most part, for two of these reasons, either that she
might not be glorified by the excess of the ascesis, or that she might not be
dragged away from virtue through bodily need. Thus then she watched the first
assaults of the soul, not allowing them to be dragged down


together with the bodily desires; but just as a tree running to wood she was
cutting around the sprouts of the unfruitful branches; for she was taking away
the thorny outgrowths of the understanding through both fasting and prayer.
And if each of these advanced even a little, she brought varied punishments
upon them, maltreating the body through all kinds of labors. For she was not
satisfied only with famine of bread, but also with scarcity of water. Whenever
a war of the enemy was joined against her, first she called her own Master
through prayer toward alliance; for she was not satisfied to quench the rush
of the lion by bare ascesis. And from the supplication the Lord was present,
and the adversary ran away; but often the enemy also lingered toward the
battle, and the Lord did not ward off the murderer, by this very thing
introducing the training of the virtuous soul. And she, as by an addition of
the gifts, was strengthened still more toward the victory against the enemy.
For she was not satisfied with putting herself to death by scarcities of
foods, but she also tried to keep herself from the regimens according to
pleasure. For she ate bran bread; and water she often did not take at all; and
she also slept on the ground up to a point. So long then as the war was being
joined, she used such weapons; putting on prayer as a weapon and putting on a
shield; and her helmet was compounded of faith and hope and love. For faith
led all things, binding together all her joints. And almsgiving also was
present, even if not that according to activity, yet at any rate that
according to intention. Through these then, the enemy undergoing defeat, she
also lightened from the most bitter ascesis. And she did this toward the
members of the body not being dissolved all at once; for this was a token of
defeat. For when the weapons have fallen together, what hope is there for the
soldier toward war? Some then, having consumed themselves immoderately and
without examination by not eating, brought upon themselves the mortal blow;
and as if having come into a declining of the opponent, they destroyed
themselves. But she did not act thus, but she did all things with discernment;
and she fought the enemy fiercely through both the prayers and the ascesis;
but toward the calm of her own ship she took care of the body. For those who
sail, when storm and surge have overtaken them, remain without food, setting
all the skill from themselves against the danger before their eyes; but
whenever they happen upon living, then they make the care of the second
salvation; and not all their time is spent toward the tossing of the sea; but
they receive as a rest from the labors the briefest calm; and not even thus do
they live without care, nor indeed are they dragged down by deep sleep
=28.1497= , having had experience of the things that have gone and taking
thought of the things that will be. For even if the storm has abated, yet the
sea has not been lessened; and if the second has passed, yet the first [third]
remains; even if what has come about has been separated, yet the causing thing
is present. Thus then also in the present case, even if the spirit of desire
has been driven away, yet the one who has authority over this is not far.
Whence one must pray unceasingly because of the unsteadiness of the sea and
the salty wickedness of the enemy. The blessed one then, knowing accurately
the present surge in life, and foreseeing the squalls of the spirits, was
carefully steering her own boat by piety toward God; for she moored it
unstormed at the salvific harbor, having set faith toward God as a most secure
anchor. Apostolic then was her life, bound together through faith and
possessionlessness, and still also shining through love and humility. This one
of the


salvific word completed the deed; For you shall tread upon asp and basilisk,
and upon all the power of the enemy. Fittingly she heard this: Well done, good
and faithful servant; you were faithful over few things, I will set you over
many. And the saying, even if it is referred to the gifts, yet let it be
understood here, as Because you have conquered the material war, you will also
plant a trophy against the immaterial, I shielding you. Let the principalities
and authorities spoken of by my servant Paul know the greatness of the faith;
for having conquered the opposing powers, you will also converse with the
better ones. Thus then withdrawing by herself, she became a perfecter of good
works. And as time went on, and her virtues flowering, the fragrance of her
most famous labors passed over into many; For nothing, it says, is hidden that
will not be made manifest. For God knows those who love him and to proclaim
them through himself toward correction of those who hear. Then then by the
will of the Better some began to visit, and to make the interviews toward
their own building; for trading the things of her life through the words they
drew still nearer, wanting to be benefited. For according to the usual
sequence they inquired of her saying: How must one be saved? and she, having
sighed heavily, and having sent forth a multitude of tears, withdrew into
herself, and as if having made the answer through the tears, she practiced a
second silence. And they, having come together, forced her to speak the great
things of God; for they marveled, being struck by the sight alone; and again
they exhorted her to utter. And when the blessed one had been forced for a
long time, after sufficient time, much stillness having come about, with a
humble voice she spoke that scriptural saying: Do not force a poor man; for he
is a beggar. And those present, having received the saying gladly, as if
having tasted of honey and honeycomb, held still more to the questioning; and
then through the scriptural =28.1500= sayings they loaded her; for they said
to her that Freely you received, freely give. And she says: See that you do
not pay the penalty of the talent-hider instead of the servant. And she said
to them: Why do you thus imagine concerning me the sinner, as if I were doing
or saying something? We have a common teacher, the Lord; from the same spring
we draw the spiritual streams; and from the same breasts we are nursed with
milk, of both the Old and the New Testament. And they say to her: We also know
that there is one pedagogue of us, the Scripture, and the same teacher; but by
unsleeping zeal you have advanced in the virtues; and it is necessary that
those who have come into the habit of good things, as more powerful, transact
with the young; for our common teacher also exhorts this. And the blessed one,
having heard these things, as suckling infants, was whimpering. And they
having come together, having set the question in second place, exhorted her to
cease from the weeping. And when she was still, again for a long time there
was stillness. And they began again to exhort her. And she, having been moved
with compassion, and having known that what was said does not assign praise to
her, but sows benefit for those present, began to speak to them thus: Little
children, we all, both men and women, know the being saved, but through our
own negligence we are left behind of the salvation. For one must first keep
the things made known through the grace of the Lord. And these are: You shall
love the Lord your God from your whole soul, and your neighbor as yourself.
From these the beginning of the law is kept, and in this the fullness of the
grace rests. Short is the utterance of the word, but much and infinite is the
power of this in this. For all the things beneficial to the soul hang from
these; and Paul bears witness, saying that


the end of the law is love. As many useful things then as human beings say
according to the grace of the Spirit, it is from love, and it ends in it. This
salvation then is this, the double love. And one must also add this, being
from it again, that each of us, recognizing what she is, should desire the
greater things. And they were at a loss toward what was said, and again they
asked in return. And she said to them: You are not ignorant of the parable in
the Gospel concerning the 100, and 60, and 30; the hundred then is our
profession; and the sixty is the order of the continent; and the thirty of
those who live chastely. And from the thirty to the sixty it is good to pass;
for from the small to the greater it is good to advance; but to run down from
the greater to the lesser is dangerous. For the one who has once inclined
toward the worse cannot even stand in the few; but as in a depth of
destruction she is carried. Some then, professing virginity, and being dragged
in reasoning of a weak judgment, make pretenses in sins. For they say to
themselves, or rather =28.1501= to the devil, that If we live chastely (or
rather foolishly), we are deemed worthy even of the thirtieth; for it says
that all the Old held to childbearing. Let it then be known that this opinion
is of the enemy. For the one who is led from the greater to the lesser is
struck by the opposing one; for like a runaway soldier, the one who does this
is judged; and it is not because she has gone over to the lesser campaign that
she is deemed worthy of pardon; but because she ran away, she receives the
punishment. One must then, as I said before, from the lesser to the advance.
And this the Apostle teaches, that Forgetting the things behind, one must
stretch forward to the things in front. Those then who hold the hundred must
cycle this into themselves, and not give an end to the number. For it says
that If you do the things commanded, say that We are unprofitable servants. We
then who have taken up this profession must hold chastity to the extreme. For
even among those in the world chastity seems to be practiced, but folly is
also present with them, because they sin with all the other senses. For they
look unseemly, and they laugh disorderly. But we, having especially left even
these, let us go up in the virtues, and let us take away from the eyes the
vain imagination. For the Scripture says: Let your eyes look straight; and to
hinder the tongue from such sins. For it is unlawful for the organ of the
hymns to bring forth shameful words. And one must not only decline to say
them, but also to hear. And these things it is impossible to keep, if we use
goings-forth continually. For through our senses, even if we do not wish, the
thieves enter. For how can a house, smoke having been moved from the outside,
and the windows having been opened, not be blackened. Necessarily then it is
proper to decline the goings-forth into the marketplace. For if we hold it
burdensome and heavy to see brothers and parents naked, how much more will it
be harmful to us to behold on the streets those unseemly stripped, and still
also speaking unbridled words? For from these it happens that unpleasant and
pestilential imaginations approach. And whenever we shut ourselves even into
the houses, we ought not even there to have the without-care, but to watch;
for it is written: Watch. And as much as we secure ourselves toward chastity,
so much do we join with more bitter thoughts. For the one who has added, it
says, knowledge, adds pain. For as much as the athletes advance, so much do
they join with greater opponents. See how far you have flown up, and do not be
faint toward the present things. The material and practical


fornication have you conquered? the enemy will set before you that through the
senses. And when you also hinder yourself from this, in the regions according
to thought =28.1504= he lurks, moving the immaterial war against you. For he
introduces seemly faces and old conversations even to those who are still. One
must then not consent to the phantasms; for it is written that If a spirit of
the one who has authority rises upon you, do not leave your place. For consent
toward these is a match for worldly fornication; For the powerful, it says,
will be examined powerfully. Great then is the contest toward the spirit of
fornication. For this is the chief of the enemy in evils toward destruction of
a soul. And the blessed Job, hinting at this, said to the devil: The one who
has the power upon the navel of the belly. With many then and various devices
the devil moves the sting of fornication against the Christ-loving human
beings. Often then the evil-minded one has also shifted brotherly love into
his own wickedness. For he has tripped virgins who have fled marriage and
every imagination of life, through a brotherly relation; and he has also
wounded monks, who themselves too have fled every fornicating form; but he has
also deceived them through a devout conversation. For this is the work of the
enemy, that, putting on the things of others, he puts forward his own things
secretly. He shows a grain of wheat, and he sets a snare under it. And I think
the Lord also says concerning this that They come to you in clothing of sheep,
but within they are rapacious wolves. What then shall we do toward this? Let
us become prudent as serpents, and simple as doves, moving a cunning thought
against his snare; for the, Become prudent as serpents, has been said toward
our not missing the rushes of the devil. For the like from the like makes the
diagnosis most swift. And the simplicity of the dove shows the purity of the
deed. Every good work then will be in flight from the worse. But how shall we
flee what we do not know? We must then, having taken in thought the
terribleness of the enemy, guard ourselves from this one's evil-device; for it
says: Who goes about seeking whom he may swallow. And his foods are choice.
One must then always watch. For he wars through the outward things, and he
tames through the inner thoughts. And more through the inner; for both by
night and by day he approaches immaterially. What then is needed toward the
present war? It is clear, as toilsome ascesis and pure prayer. But these
things in general have been by nature antidotes upon every destructive
thought; and one must also use certain particular notions toward thrusting
away the present plague of the soul; and when the most shameful thought has
come upon, to bring against it the contraries. For if there should come about
in the regions according to thought an imagination of a seemly sight, the
present thing must be punished by the word, =28.1505= and let it peck out the
eyes of the idol, and let it take out the fleshes of the cheeks; and let it
cut under also the lips, and then let it see an ugly frame of bare bones; let
it consider then what the longed-for thing was; for thus the thought would be
able to check the vain error. For the thing loved was nothing other than blood
mixed with phlegm, which also furnishes the need of a weave to the living
things. Thus then even through such thoughts it is likely that the defiled
wickedness is scared away; and as with a nail one ought to knock out the demon
with a nail. And still also one must even over the whole body of the loved one
sketch foul-smelling and putrid sores; and to speak briefly, to set before the
inner eyes something like a corpse, or even oneself a corpse. And greater than
all is to rule the belly; for thus it is also possible to master the pleasures
under the belly. There was then a divine symposium of those present; for from
the craters of wisdom


they were being gladdened. And the blessed Syncletica was pouring for them the
divine drink and stream. And each of them received what she wished, and one of
those who had come together inquired of her saying: Is possessionlessness a
perfect good? And she said: And very much a good for those who are able. For
those who endure this have affliction in the flesh, but relief in the soul.
For just as the firm garments being trampled and turned about violently are
washed and whitened, so also the strong soul through voluntary poverty is
strengthened still more; but those who have the reasoning more weakly suffer
the contrary to the first. For having been afflicted for a little, like the
torn garments, they are destroyed, not bearing the washing through virtue. And
the craftsman is one, but the end of the garments is different; for some are
torn and destroyed, and the other is whitened and renewed. Someone then might
say, possessionlessness is a good treasure for the manly mind; for it is a
bridle of the practical sins. And one must first be trained in the labors; I
mean, in fasting and sleeping on the ground, and the others in part, and thus
acquire this virtue. For those who have not done thus, but have run all at
once upon the casting away of the money, as a rule have been caught by regret.
For money is an instrument of the enjoyable life. Take away then first your
own art, that is gluttony and the luxurious way of life, and thus you will be
able easily to cut around the matter of your money. For I think it heavy, when
an art is present, for an instrument not to be. For the one who has not cast
away the first, how will he be able to thrust out the second? For this reason
also the Savior, conversing with the rich man, does not all at once command
him the casting away of the money; but he first asks him, if he has done the
things of the law. And having taken up the person of a genuine teacher the
Lord asks: If you have learned the elements, if you have received the
syllables =28.1508= , and simply if you have come into the habit of the names,
come then also upon the most perfect reading; that is: Go, sell your
belongings, and come follow me. And perhaps I think that if he had not
promised that the things examined had been done, he would not have urged him
toward possessionlessness. For how would he have been able to rush toward
reading, being ignorant of the power of the syllables? Good then is
possessionlessness for those who have come into the habit of the good things;
for having made the casting away of all the superfluous things, they bring the
inclination toward the Lord, singing purely that divine oracle, which says:
Our eyes hope toward you; and you give the food to those who love you in due
season. And otherwise again they exact a great benefit; for not having the
mind toward the treasure on earth, they put on the kingdom of heavens; and
they fulfill clearly the saying by the hymn-singer David, which says: I became
as a beast before you. For just as the beasts of burden, doing their own work,
are satisfied with the foods toward living alone; so also the workers of
possessionlessness reckon the use of silver as being nothing, and they make
the work through the body toward the daily food alone. These hold the
foundation of the faith; for to them it has been said by the Lord not to be
anxious about tomorrow; and that The birds of the heaven do not sow, nor do
they reap, and the heavenly Father feeds them. They have taken courage in
these words (for God is the one who spoke), and they utter with boldness that
scriptural saying, which says: I believed, wherefore I spoke. And the enemy
also is more defeated in the case of the possessionless; for what he may harm,
he does not have. For most of the griefs and of the temptations come about in
the takings-away of the money. For what does he have to do to the
possessionless? he does not have. Fields


to burn? they are not present. To destroy beasts of burden? but they do not
have even these. To touch dearest ones? but even to these they have said a
long farewell. Therefore possessionlessness is a greatest punishment against
the enemy and a treasure of much price for the soul. As much then as this is
wondrous and oversized toward virtue, so much toward wickedness is love of
money base and wretched; and truly the inspired Paul has said concerning this
that it is the cause of all the evils. For desire of wealth, perjury, theft,
seizure, fornication, envy, hatred of brothers, war, idolatry, greed, and the
offshoots of these, hypocrisy, flattery, mockery; the cause of all these then
has been confessed to be love of money. Whence justly the Apostle named it
mother of all the evils. And not only does God punish these; but also
=28.1509= from home they destroy themselves; for bearing an unsated
wickedness, they do not have an end of the aim, whence the wound is incurable
for them. The one who has nothing desires few things; and having obtained
