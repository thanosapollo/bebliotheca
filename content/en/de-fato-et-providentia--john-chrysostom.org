OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS JOHN, ARCHBISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE, THE
GOLDEN-MOUTHED, SIX DISCOURSES ON FATE AND PROVIDENCE.
ON FATE AND PROVIDENCE.
Discourse 1.
Our life is full of much disturbance, and the present life is full of tumults;
but this is not the grievous thing, beloved, but that these tumults and the
disturbances, being able either to shift them toward the milder, or remaining
to bear them without pain, we have practiced neither of these, but we spend
the whole time in despair; and one laments poverty, another disease, another a
mass of cares and the protection of a house, another the rearing of children,
another childlessness. And see the excess of folly; for we all lament not over
the same things, but equally over the opposite things. And yet if the nature
of the things were the cause of our lamentations, we ought not to lament
equally over the opposites; but if poverty is an evil and unbearable, the one
living in wealth ought never to be distressed; and if childlessness is an
evil, the one enjoying many children ought to be of good cheer. Again if
administering the common affairs of the city, and enjoying honors, and having
many subjects is enviable, the unbusy and calm life ought to be something to
flee, and to be hated by all men. But as it is, when you see that rich and
poor lament equally, and often the one who is rich does this again more than a
poor man, and the one enjoying office, and the one being ruled, and the father
of many children, and the one who has had none at all, let us not blame the
things =50.750= for the disorder, but those who cannot use them as they ought,
and free themselves from all despondency. For the disturbance and the tumult
is not a reverse of these things, but we and our mind; so that if this is well
ordered for us, even if countless storms are raised from every side, we will
sleep always in a calm and a harbor; just as, when it is not well, even if all
things are borne for us from a fair wind, we will be no better disposed than
those in shipwrecks. This at any rate one can see also in the case of bodies.
For the one who has made his own body strong, even if he contends with
countless irregularities of airs, will not only suffer nothing terrible, but
has also taken an addition of strength by the exercise and by the association
with the bad temperament; but if he has it weak and loose, even if he enjoys
the best mixture of the seasons, nothing more will come to him from this good
lot, the weakness from within spoiling the good fortune from the seasons. So
also we see in the case of foods; for when the body of the stomach is strong
and vigorous for us, whatever it may receive, even if it is hard, even if
unworked for it, it transforms it into a pure humor, the natural temperament
prevailing over the badness in the food; but when its tone is dissolved and
flabby, even if you bring to it the most well-humored dish of all, it re
=50.751= shaped it into the worst and corrupted it, the weakness spoiling its
virtue. So then we too, when we see a disorder of things, let us not blame
God, beloved; for this is not to find a drug for the wound, but to bring a
wound upon a wound. Let us not ascribe the economy of the world to demons, let
us not think the present things are without providence, let us not wall off a
tyranny of some nativity and fate


against the providence of God. For all these things are full of blasphemy,
and the true disturbance and disorder is not a course of the things, but the
reasoning so disposed; which, even if it enjoys countless good order, until
it corrects the disorder in itself and the disturbance, nothing more will
come to it from the good order in the things; but just as an eye, when it is
sick, even at brightest noon will see darkness, and other things instead of
other things, and nothing more will come to it from the ray, but when it is
healthy and vigorous, even in evening it will be able to guide the body with
safety; so also the eye of our mind, while it is vigorous, even if it sees
things that seem disordered, will see well; but when it is corrupted, even if
you lead it up to heaven itself, it will condemn even the things there of
much disorder and disturbance. And that these things are so, I will furnish
you many examples of this, both of old and of those in our time. How many
bear poverty easily, and do not cease giving thanks to God on this account?
how many, having wealth and luxury, do not give thanks, but blaspheme the
Master? how many, having suffered nothing terrible, accuse the providence of
the whole? how many, having dwelt in a prison the whole time, bear the misery
there more reasonably than those who have lived in license and safety? You
see that the state of the soul, and that one's own reasoning, is the cause
both of these and of those, but not the nature of the things itself. So that
if we took care of our mind, there would be no disturbance, there would be no
disorder, there would be none of the terrible things, even if the things of
our life were borne up and down more grievously than any Euripus. For why,
tell me, does Paul give thanks? And yet he is of those who have lived best,
of those who have lived with virtue the whole time, of those who have least
suffered so many terrible things; no one either has become more righteous
than that man of all men under the sun, or has suffered harder things than
that man of those since men have come to be. But nevertheless, seeing many
living in wickedness, and being of good cheer, and enjoying the present
goods, he himself gives thanks to God, and he exhorted all the others to
this. Look to that man. And you, when you see the wicked man being of good
cheer, when you see him lifted up, when you see him mastering enemies,
avenging himself on those who have pained him, having undergone no loss, the
goods flowing to him from every side, all honoring and flattering him, and
yourself in all the opposites, in insults, slanders, plots, do not think you
are of those thrown aside, but looking to Paul who is of your portion, raise
up the mind, recover the reasonings, do not fall under despondency. Do not
test the friend of God and the enemy from the good fortune and ill fortune
here, but if you see one living rightly, and being emptied by disease, and
looking toward piety, count him blessed and think him enviable, even if he is
bound with countless chains, and dwells in a prison con =50.752= tinually,
even if he is a slave to the unworthy, even if he is poor, even if he works
mines, even if he suffers anything terrible. For this one is blessed, even if
he is dug out, even if he is burned, even if the body is consumed little by
little. But if you see another living in wantonness and wickedness, and in
the last evils, and enjoying much honor, going up onto the royal throne
itself, and wearing a diadem, and having purple, and mastering the whole
inhabited world, weep even on this account for such a one, and count him
wretched. For nothing is truly more miserable than a soul so disposed, even
if it has the whole inhabited world subjected. For what is the use of being
rich in goods, when according to virtue he is poorer than all? and what is
the gain of mastering so many, when he cannot prevail over himself and his
own passions? But if we see a body badly disposed and fevered, or seized
beforehand by gout, or by some consumption, or by another incurable affection



we weep, even if he is more well-supplied than all, and so much the more, as
he is more well-supplied. For the sensation of the terrible things becomes
sharper, when one is in abundance of the necessaries. For the one prevented
from enjoyment by poverty has the necessity as a consolation; but the one who
is master to use everything, and is prevented by the illness, is inflamed the
more by this very thing. How then is it not absurd if, when we see someone
seized beforehand by a bodily disease, we count him wretched, even if he is
well-supplied, but when a soul is badly disposed, a soul than which nothing
is equal for us nor more honorable, we count it blessed because of a few
goods, or because of a temporary honor, or because of another of the things
that remain here and leave us with this life, or rather often even before the
life? Do not, I beg. These things are causes of disturbance for us, these of
tumult. For this reason many accuse God, for this reason they think the world
without providence. As if they knew that there is no good in the present
life, but virtue alone, not wealth, not goods, not health, not power, not
anything else, and that there is no evil in the present life, but vice alone
and wickedness and distortion of soul, not poverty and disease, and insult,
and slanders, and the other things that are thought to be terrible, they
would never have uttered these things which they now do, they would never
have spent their time in despondency, they would never have counted blessed
those whom they ought to count wretched, they would never have counted
wretched those whom they ought to count blessed, they would not have been
disposed as about beings of men. For to count men blessed on fleshiness, and
a plentiful manger, and much sleep is nothing other than to be disposed as
about irrational animals; since this also is their good fortune, or rather
even among the irrational animals this is a vice. At any rate many both asses
and horses have been destroyed, living in idleness and gluttony. Then, among
the irrational animals, for whom all virtue is in good condition of body,
this is harmful; but among men, for whom all the authority is in the nobility
of the soul, shall we set this down as virtue, and shall we not be ashamed
before heaven, and the angels akin to our soul? do you not blush even at the
nature of the body itself and the formation? for God did not shape it for us
as for the irrational animals, but as it was fitting for it to serve a
rational and immortal soul. For why did God make the eyes for all the
irrational animals nodding downward, but for you, bearing them, he set them
in the head as in an acropolis? was it not because those have nothing in
common toward heaven, but for you especially a law was set from the beginning
both by God and by nature to look around at the things above =50.753=? Why
did he make the body upright for you, but for those again nodding downward?
was it not again for the same cause, and from the formation itself teaching
you to have nothing in common toward the earth, nor to be glued to the
present things? Let us not then betray the nobility, nor slip out toward the
ignobility of the irrational animals, lest he say also concerning us: A man
being in honor did not understand. For to define prosperity by luxury, and
wealth, and glory, and the present things, is not of those who have
considered their own nobility, but of men who have become horses and asses.
But may it not =50.754= be that some such are present here, in a holy chorus
and spiritual theater, and a god-fearing assembly. For this reason we enjoy a
divine hearing each day, that, cutting off the passions of the mind that run
to wood, as with a sickle by the word, we may become fruit-bearing trees, and
may bring ripe fruit, stored away into a royal storehouse, bringing glory to
the common Master and farmer of our souls, and to us the immortal life, which
may it come about that we all obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our
Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father together with the Holy Spirit be
glory, and might, and honor unto the ages of the ages. Amen.



ON PROVIDENCE AND FATE.
Discourse 2.
He who speaks evil of father or mother, let him die the death, he says; the
law was laid down on the Old, when the exactness of the best way of life was
not much, when there was an introduction of the divine, when the commands were
childish, when the milk, when the pedagogue, when the lamp, when the type and
the shadow. What then would we say concerning those now in the grace, and in
the truth of the things, and in so great knowledge, who speak evil not of
father and mother, but of the God of all himself? what punishment will receive
such people? what chastisement will suffice for the measure of the vice? and
what river of fire, what unending worm, what outer darkness, what bonds, what
gnashing, what weeping? All the tortures are less, both those that are and
those to come, than a soul so disposed, that has been brought down to so great
vice. And there are many and various modes of blasphemy; wherefore it is
necessary to set them out today, so that neither willingly may someone fall
into any such thing, nor overlook a friend, nor an enemy falling. For there is
not, there is not a sin worse than this, nor even equal. but this is even an
addition of evils, and what confounds all things, and has unforgivable
chastisement and unbearable punishment.
Who are those who speak God badly? Those who wall off against the wisdom of
his providence the disorder and necessity from fate. And that the unbelievers,
and those who gape toward woods and stones and serve them, are sick with this
disease, is no wonder; but that those who have been freed from this error and
slavery, and have been counted worthy to know the God over all, the true and
really God, as from some back-flow, are dragged down toward that storm, the
reasoning and the madness, this is the most terrible, and truly worthy of many
tears and dirges; when those who say they worship Christ, and have been
counted worthy of so many mysteries, and have shared in the unspeakable
doctrines, and in the wisdom brought down to us from heaven, after so great
honor which they have enjoyed from God, willingly throw themselves down a
precipice, casting themselves out with much irrationality from the freedom
which God granted them, and subjecting themselves to a most grievous slavery,
and fabricating through the reasonings a most tyrannical tyranny that is not,
and cutting off every good hope, and attempting to cut out the sinews of those
who have been eager for virtue. Just as in a war someone of the enemies,
seeing noble soldiers, ready to give even the soul itself for the fatherland,
and not being able =50.754= to harm them by another manner, nor to make them
stand off from eagerness for the king, nor to make them cowards, nor to
prevail by engines, would ruin them otherwise, attempting to persuade them
that they display so great labor in vain and to no purpose and for nothing, so
as to loosen their hands, so as to cast down their strength, so as to quench
the eagerness, so as through all these to make them stand off from the zeal
about the war, and thinking them naked and unarmed, to take them bound as
captives; so also the devil has done. Since he saw the greater part of the
inhabited world by the grace of God laughing at the Greek error, and with all
anger and all soul advocating the word for piety, then from this much zeal of
virtue coming to be, much

contempt of vice, he does not dare to come forward and say openly, stand off
from Christ, laugh at the doctrines from him, his things are a myth and
error, he is some wicked and inhuman one; for he knows that by this they will
leap away from his tyranny more, and will hate him more greatly. For this
reason he does not bring in the accusation straight, but going around in a
circle, he sows in secret the poison of the impious doctrines, seeming to
leave them remaining in the faith, but in truth tearing it up by the root,
and dissolving all the doctrines of the truth, and pouring much slander of
God upon those who are persuaded. For this reason this deadly thing has been
prepared for him, and the death-bearing drugs of fate, that he may bring in
secretly all that I said, that he may show our mind empty and the faith
empty, that he may persuade men to have a wicked vote concerning God; which
indeed he also did in the beginning upon Adam, slandering God as some
grudging and envious one. For he all but said thus through the things he
advised: God knew, he says, that your eyes will be opened, he knew that you
will be as gods, he envied you, he grudged you a greater honor
. For even if he did not add these words, he gave them from the things he said
to suspect these things. And see the villainy. Having overturned the vote of
God, and having said that great goods will be for them if they are not
persuaded by him, such as opening of eyes and equality with God, and knowledge
hanging on it, he no longer added that the one who bars them from these is
wicked, lest he seem, as some enemy and foe speaking, to be making a defense,
but having put on the face of a counselor and guardian, he might make the
wicked advice acceptable; which indeed also happened. For he wished to
construct nothing =50.755= other through the things he said, but that you
stand off from the God who made you; he is grudging and envious, he envies you
the greater goods. But he did not set these words openly; for they would,
having suspected him to be an enemy, have fled; but having left saying these
things to them and having wrapped them, he brought in this destructive
counsel. So then even now he does not say, stand off from Christ, as if the
divine doctrines were condemned; for he knows that he was lying and was not
believed; but villainously he seems to leave us in these, and to concede
toward the truth, but otherwise, they not knowing, he takes them out of this
eternal inheritance; just as if someone holding a genuine and free child, some
simple and unwicked one, with the right hand should not cast him out of the
paternal house, but should advise him to do the things through which both
willing and unwilling he will fall out of all the paternal things. For there
is not, there is not one who attends to fate being able to obtain the heavens,
or rather there is not being able to flee Gehenna and the chastisement; for it
commands all to be persuaded contrary to the doctrines of God. God says, If
you are willing and listen to me, you will eat the good things of the land;
but if you are not willing, nor listen to me, a sword will eat you. For the
mouth of the Lord has spoken these things. Did you see how God utters and what
laws he sets? Hear also of fate, how it utters, and how it brings in contrary
ordinances, and learn how those have been said by a divine Spirit, but those
of this by a wicked demon and some untamed beast. God said, If you are
willing, and if you are not willing, making us masters of virtue and vice, and
setting it upon our judgment. But that one, what does it say? That it is not
possible to flee what is fated, whether we are willing or not willing. God
says: If you are willing, you will eat the good things of the land; it says,
even if we are willing, if it has not been given to us, there is no use in
this willing. God says, If you are not willing to attend to my words, a sword
will eat you; it says, if we are not willing, but it has been given to us, we
are saved altogether. Does fate not say these things? What then could there be


clearer than this battle? what more manifest than this war, which the demons,
teachers of wickedness, have thus shamelessly chosen against the divine
oracles? But as I said, that demons and other demon-men, I mean the Greeks,
are persuaded of these things, is no wonder; but that you, enjoying so divine
and saving teaching, dishonor these things, and run toward those most
irrational things, which have destruction of soul, this is harder than all.
For why should I even judge those outside? For me the word is for now toward
you, the members of Christ, the children of the Church, those being educated
in the paternal house, those enjoying heavenly teachings, those honored with
so great honor.
For this reason I groan, for this reason I weep and lament; for they are truly
worthy of dirges, when someone falls with falls not even worthy of pardon. For
what pardon, tell me, when God declares and de =50.756= mons speak against,
and the things said by those seem more trustworthy among the household of God?
For we are not now moving reasonings, but for now we will show the boldness of
those persuaded by them. God says, I have set before you fire and water, the
life and the death, stretch out your hand to which you will; the demon says
that it is not in you to stretch out the hand, but by some necessity and
force. And they seem to you to be more trustworthy, and you do not perceive
the interval of those advising, that the one is God, the other a demon; you do
not examine the difference of the counsel, that the one is saving and calls
toward virtue, but the other is truly both demonic and toward vice and
wickedness; you do not look at the things that have come to be for you from
God, and the things from the demon, that the one so loved you as even to give
the Only-begotten for you, than whom nothing was more honorable to the Father,
and still loves, so as even to exhort you through the apostles for your
salvation, and he does all things; but the other so hated and hates, as to war
against you through all things and not only to give nothing good, but even to
try to cast you out of the things you received from God; the one was eager to
make you equal to angels, but the other set you down more dishonored than
those who walk on the ground, and persuaded you in turn to worship them; the
one draws you into a kingdom of heavens and the other honors, but the other
even grudged you what was given here, and did not stand off before he cast you
out. For if you cannot see together his doctrines, and yet they are clearer
than the sun to those who are not rather dull; so also these things of God
have the virtue and the salvation obvious, and those the vice; but
nevertheless if you cannot see together toward them, at least from those
advising learn the saving and the harmful. For how is it not absurd to use
this judgment in the other things, and if a physician gives someone food, not
to be over-busy, but to receive it as productive of health, but if a poisoner
and sorcerer, not to be a busybody, but to turn away as a deadly thing and
harmful; but in the case of God not even to use this interval? and yet so much
more is the interval of God and demon than of physician and poisoner, as it is
not even possible to say, nor to set beside mind and word. How then is it not
of last absurdity, where the difference of those providing the foods is least,
not to be a busybody, but to use the quality of the persons instead of all
teaching; but where the interval of those advising is so great, to have need
of word toward learning that the one is saving, the other harmful? Do not, I
beg, let us not be more irrational than the irrational animals, but let us
leap away, and let us not incline the ears; Evil communications corrupt good
manners; and those who have been deceived cannot obtain pardon. For why, if
you see a pestilential and diseased place, do you flee the pastime there, even
if countless things draw you toward that dwelling, preferring the health of
the body to all; but when some are filled with the pestilential word, and a
disease, not only a body


corrupting, but also destroying a soul, and making it worse and cheaper, do
you not leap away? Hear, some wise man says, do not stand, but leap away, do
not linger, fearing even the shortest pastime in these. And we say these
things, not fearing the strength of the doctrines among them, but =50.757=
fearing your weakness. For to us by the grace of God their things appear
weaker than spiders' webs, to those founded in the faith, and if they chant
our ears countless times, they incur so much more laughter from us, just as
those who are mad and have stood out of their own wits; but we have feared
your weakness. I do not say the things said against all, but against those
who are liable to the things said. Since even Paul, who masters all, showing
the disciple Timothy not teaching only, but also the word-fights against the
aliens, advises him to stand off from empty voices. Short is the time of our
life, brief the provision for salvation. If then we spend even this short
time given to us toward learning something of the useful things, on
superfluous and unprofitable and harmful hearings, when then will we have
supply of another toward learning the necessary and urgent things? For
especially, even if it were long, one ought to spend all of it on the useful
things; but when it is also little and short, how is it not of last folly to
spend its littleness on doctrines that corrupt our soul? What need have you
of a drug? do not receive a blow, do not spend the time on healing what you
re =50.758= ceive from others, and have; from the divine Scriptures put
together the healthy, and if someone comes saying some other thing, block the
ears, leap away at once, do not linger. And if, a gathering having come to be
against kings, you would not have endured to share in the council, as by the
communion of the hearing, even if assent should not come to be, being about
to be in danger altogether; when something is said against God, and a
doctrine full of accusation of him is exercised, will you not flee away, will
you not hate the blasphemous tongue, will you not stop up the godless mouths?
And how will you be able with boldness to pray to God, sharing in the
slanders against him? Do not, I beg. These things have not been said by me
toward those present, or rather also toward those present. For even if you
are not liable, yet those whom you know to be sick with this disease, pour
upon these and upon more others words, so as to tear up the evil by the root.
And may it come about by prayers of the saints and friends of God for our
words are not so strong as the boldness of their prayer, may it come about
that we all, and as many as contribute toward the fullness of the Church,
having been freed from these evils, stand with boldness before the tribunal
of Christ, to whom be the glory. Amen.

ON FATE AND PROVIDENCE
Discourse 3.
I know that I spoke the other day about fate toward your love, but nothing
prevents us even today from exercising the word about the same subject; not
because the affection is strong, but because our slackness is unspeakable,
which makes even the small ones of the diseases great. Since even to a blind
man it is clear by nature, that the faithful has no need even of words nor of
teaching toward the flight of this evil. For just as one dwelling in the
inhabited world among us, and living as a citizen by the laws among us, and
subjected to the same kings, one need not persuade that one must not bring the
Persian constitution into ours, but only forbid; so then also here. For of the


sins some have need of word and teaching, but some are so manifest and clear,
as to be prevented by fear and punishment alone; such as to murder, to commit
adultery, to steal, they have no need of words; wherefore also the lawgiver
has not composed a teaching about these sounding around us, that the deed is
wicked; for he had seized us beforehand by the reasoning; but he only
forbade, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not murder.
But when he converses with us about neglect of widows and villainy of
deposits, he also sets reasonings, Pity the widow, he says, and the sojourner;
for you too were a sojourner in the land of Egypt. Honor the sabbath with
rest, he says; then he also sets a reasoning. Therefore to hold off from fate
is not of these that need reasoning, but of those that are clear. For just as
it is clear that to murder is wicked and to commit adultery is cheap, so it is
clear that to attend to fate is wicked and forbidden. For do not from =50.758=
those persuaded think it is of the things not prevented; since murder too is
dared, and has been prevented among the laws, and the adulteries and the
seizures are of the forbidden things, though they are done by the many. Since
that it is of the prevented things, just as murder, among the laws themselves
of the Greeks, to be persuaded by nativity, let someone of those who have
committed adultery with so-and-so's wife come forward, being accused in a
lawcourt, and say that it was not I, but the nativity; for I wished to be
temperate, but that one pushed me and threw me in; will he not on this account
give a harder penalty, as making a laughable defense? will he obtain some
pardon? By no means. And yet he ought, according to them. For if all things
come to be by nativity and not by choice, there is no defense greater than
this. For if choice is mistress and more powerful than nativity, there would
not be nativity; if even if someone devises countless things, the commands
from that must be fulfilled, this one is punished in vain, and he will not
obtain pardon; but no one will hear of him either making a defense, or
obtaining pardon; so has the thing been slandered among all, and it seems to
be a myth and nonsense. And many of the rulers, although if they should suffer
force from a man, this itself suffices toward a defense for one being charged,
rather it no longer even seems to be a charge, at any rate many of the rulers,
having been accused upon unjust murders, gave a penalty, but the executioners
who served the murder, and have become own-handed of the slaughter, no one
even dragged into a lawcourt, nor even sought, in the first place, the
necessity making a defense for them, and the dignity of the office, and the
fear of the subjection. Then they know to pardon a man, being forced by a
=50.759= man of the same race and same kind; but the one under fate, ought
they not much more to pardon? For he even ought to have fled through. But of
that one, as they say, the rule is so impossible, that whether to a desert, or
to a sea, or wherever one arrives, he will not flee through her thoughts. How
then is it not absurd that the one being choked by force of barbarians enjoys
very much pardon, so as not even to be charged in the first place, but the one
being forced by a more powerful power, as they say, gives a penalty, and they
do not even give him a share of word toward such a defense?
For no one ever putting forward fate has escaped, not a criminal in lawcourts,
not a household-slave in a house, not children into a school, not disciples in
the arts, in whatever they miss. How then do some advocate fate while
dishonoring it, and giving no share of any pardon to the necessity under it?
So in truth, and by the conscience they have persuaded themselves strongly,
that the thing is a myth. But when they fall into grievous sins, they add
another to themselves again, as by this


about to flee through the penalty laid up for them, but they will give it
more grievously. For it is not so grievous to sin, as after the sin to be
shameless, and to slander God over one's own evils. This is worse than every

So then the afflictions and the straits of good men are an addition of crowns,

the necessity from poverty, but the other has not even one specious or
reasonable refuge; so that as much as the poor man is lessened according to
the present life, so much he has more according to the com =50.770= ing in

Do not then think the poor man is wronged, nor that the rich man has more.
Just as also upon the bad, as many as give a penalty here, either have washed
off the whole, or they give a lesser punishment there, as having undergone a
penalty of great sufferings here; so also upon the good, those who enjoy honor

providence of the just, wonder at him especially on this account, that with
poverty he is such. If these things are accurately with you, you will never be
disturbed over the things that have come to be, if you look toward the things
to come, if you think the present things are nothing, if you always hope
those, if you stand yourself off from these. And may it come about that we
obtain all the goods, by the grace of Christ. Amen.

ON FATE Or against gluttony.
Discourse 6.
Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die, there were many among the Jews in
the time of the prophets saying. But that Jews say these things, Whose god is
the belly, and the glory in their shame, is no wonder; but that after the
grace, and


the contempt of present things, and so great perfection and accuracy, there
are still even now some, even if not saying it in the words, but crying this
same thing through the works, of how great accusation would it be worthy? For
there are, there truly are, those who think they have been brought into this
life for this, that having lived in luxury, and having burst the belly, and
having fattened the body, they may thus go away, preparing for the worm a
more plentiful table from their own flesh. And would that this only were the
terrible thing, that they spent the present things in vain and at random and
unto nothing needed; and yet even this is not without charges, nor
accusation.
For when we consume the goods given to us by God toward food of body, and unto
help of those in need, unto unrestraint and insolence and the last slackness,
this worst spending will not be unliable for us, but we will altogether give
an account, and what has been said about the five talents, and two, and one,
is to be said also upon goods, and all the other things. Which therefore I was
saying, that especially, even if we had come to be at random and in vain, not
even so was such a deed unliable for us; but now besides this another penalty
will also lie. For the one living with luxury, and practicing drunkenness, and
feeding parasites and flatterers, being burst with meat-eating and
wine-drinking, often =50.770=.30 both unwilling and willing must sin, both
beside the time of the present place, and after the withdrawal from there. For
just as a ship having received a multitude of cargoes greater than its own
symmetry, becoming underwater by the size of the load, so also the soul, and
the nature of our body, having received the foods more than its own power,
becomes full of water, and not bearing the weight of the things thrown on, is
sunk into the sea of destruction, and destroys besides the pilot, and sailors
and prow-man and passengers, and the load itself with all. Just as therefore
upon ships so disposed, neither a calm of sea, nor knowledge of a pilot, nor a
multitude of sailors, nor fitness of construction, nor a timely season of
year, nor anything else benefits the ship thus stormed; so then it also comes
to be upon those living in luxury; neither a multitude of reasonings, nor
teaching and exhortation, nor admonitions and counsels, nor anything else, nor
tribute of the things to come, nor shame, nor accusation of those present, nor
anything else can save the soul thus stormed; but the excess prevails over
all, bearing down underwater henceforth the one sick with such things both
unwilling and willing, and working a grievous shipwreck, from which it is not
even easy to bring up. And not even toward the things to come and the audits
there, but also toward the present life such a one becomes useless, and fit
for nothing, but everywhere laughable, both in political, and in private
affairs; and if some of the urgent things ought to be done, he will be found
having nothing provident, nothing economic, easy for all =50.771= enemies to
handle, useless to all friends and those belonging. Such a one is not only
easy to insult in dangers and circumstances, not unbearable in calm and
license, but also in the difficulties by the excess he will be useless to each
of the things that fall. For there, I mean in the ill days, cowardice, and
unmanliness, and immoderate fear, and much lack of counsel will hold him; but
here boldness, and slackness, and unrestraint, excess and conceit, and
boastfulness, make him more grievous than the former among all.
For not only do the bodies of those so disposed become both ugly, and flabby,
and soaked, and full from every side of unbearable stench, but also the soul
is much uglier than the body, having received far greater diseases than that
from the


luxury. For just as the body does not put forth only the superfluities
according to nature, but much more and from every side; for even eyes, and
noses, and mouths, the things within being weighed down by the flood that has
come to be, are filled with a cheapest moisture and corruption, and the
nature of the flesh itself having become more flabby than the natural
symmetry, filled as with mires and unclean marshes, becomes both stinking at
once and useless, and unapproved toward every good work. For even the earth
itself when, tyrannized by such greed, it loses its own and inborn heat,
destroys together with it also its strength, and is fit neither toward
working, nor toward its own generation. And from this those who live with
continual luxury are caught by grievous and incurable diseases; for they
become catchable by tremors, and paralysis, and consumption, and catarrhs,
and gouts, and hand-pains, and more others, which the children of physicians
say. So even if there were no Gehenna, nor chastisement, nor last
condemnation from God, nor blame among men, nor the untimely spending, nor
countless other things that come to be from luxury, if this alone were
present, would it not be sufficient to turn all away? For such tables will be
disposed no better than deadly drugs, or rather, if one must say the true,
even much worse. For those at once carried off the one who took them, and
brought on the end without sensation, so as not even to pain the one who died
by this itself; but these bring on a life harder than countless deaths to
those who make much of them, a life I said, not only pitiable, but also
shameful and laughable. For the other affections have many who share the
pain, but as many as have come to be from luxury and drunkenness, they do not
even let those who see, though willing, share the pain with those mastered by
them; but the excess of the evil bends toward pity, and the subject of the
disease provokes the one who knows.
Wherefore we would suffer some middle affection, neither pitying them; for the
cause of the terrible things does not let them; nor being angry; for the size
of the terrible things does not permit, but the stance is between accusation
and pardon. For they were not insulted by nature as those also, nor were they
plotted against by men, but they themselves became craftsmen of these diseases
for themselves, willingly throwing themselves into the pit of the evils. Just
as therefore we do not pity very much those who fasten nooses, and those who
let themselves down crags, and those who kill themselves through the midst of
swords, so neither these. And yet if =50.772= they ought to obtain pardon,
those would obtain it rather than these, even if they themselves also would be
very unpardonable. For those either by slanders, or losses of goods, or a
danger expected, or having hoped blows, or having awaited some other fear, and
snatching themselves from greater evils, flee for refuge to the consolation of
the end, running down to death as to some inviolable place, or as to some fair
harbor hastening and being urgent from the expected storms; but these, having
nothing such to say, hand themselves over to a life harder than countless
violent deaths. It is timely then now to say that of the wise man; Who will
pity an enchanter bitten by a snake, and all those who bring themselves to
beasts? For luxury is a beast, a grievous and untamed beast, and not so does a
scorpion or a snake sitting in our innards ruin everywhere, as the desire of
luxury overturns and destroys all things. For with those beasts the plot is as
far as the body; but this, when it sits in, destroys besides the body also the

said are true, and the thing is a ruin of all, and
