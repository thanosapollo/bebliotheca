* Monody Composed According to One's Strength
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

JOHN ANAGNOSTES. A monody according to one's strength on the capture of
Thessalonica. Who, knowing how to match laments to sufferings, would have
worthily mourned the city of the Thessalonians? For this city surpassed the
others by so great a measure in prosperity, showing herself from every side
brilliant and longed-for, and to such a degree now she has fared most
unhappily and is set forth as the subject of every dirge. For those who have
had even for a little time to enjoy the goods from her, nothing else comes to
them to say concerning her than the soul, as much as casts into despondency
and prepares fountains of tears to pour from the eyes. For she surpassed many
cities in beauty and situation, and many in size and in the abundance of good
things, more than one can say. And the things with which she formerly
flourished in goods and in the wealth that was talked of, now, as it ought not
to have been, have all vanished and been wretchedly dismantled. O, how in the
manner of a magnet she drew all into herself, and would not allow them to
stand apart even in any way at all! For she held them by so much, by the
things with which she welcomed them arriving from every side, and bound them
with unbreakable bonds of friendship, that all chose her instead of the land
that bore them and no longer even remembered return. Alas! but already not
only those from a foreign land, to whom it was given, as I said, to enjoy her
graces, but even those she herself bore, =72= she has prepared to flee with
all eagerness and to fear and tremble at her as at another Charybdis. O Time,
what things you suddenly bring forth and what changes you work upon affairs.
Rightly the race of the wise, looking to the reverse turning, have named you a
wheel; for you shift and carry round all things up and down. O city, who
formerly sailed as a ship with a fair wind, then when a terrible storm-blast
had risen was wretchedly shattered and cast away and lost the wealth stored
within, not one of those who belong to you would ever willingly come to
forgetfulness, unless he had wholly gone out of his mind. I then even become
tearful as I remember you, and to speak the prophetic word, "a thin darkness
covers me," and seeking consolation I find the others also suffering the same
as I. O, how you have been stripped of your former good order, and the
brilliance has been quenched, and you compel those who look on you to be
downcast.

For all the adornment that lay about you has been thrown into confusion, and
the beauty with which you had to adorn yourself has suddenly perished. For
formerly you were a fortunate theater, but now you have become a sight
abominable to all, and you drive away even against their will those who chose
to dwell in you, since they cannot bear to see you in such misfortune. For
those who know you as nurse and mother do things like those who beat their
breasts over a mother who has departed, and all make you a tale of dejection.
O you who are present, I, even of the other things, when I come to the memory
of the city, many and great as they are and of which no speech suffices for
the telling, am filled with dizziness and am not able to hold myself under
despondency; but whenever I also reckon the most beautiful adornment which she
has thrown away, I mean the divine temples and the dwellings of holy men
throughout every part of her, I am seen by those present as almost voiceless
as well, and I scarcely recover myself from the suffering. For these, so to
speak, were the glances and pupils of the city's eyes, and now having been put
to sleep they have =74= made her dark everywhere.

And why do I not say the greater thing? One would have likened this city aptly
to heaven and earth; for the upper part, formerly having as dwellers those who
shone in virtue, preserved the form of heaven, and the lower part toward the
sea that of earth; and if one should also call them the pillars that hold the
city together, he would not miss the mark. O strange thing, where the labors
of the ascetic wrestling-school of the whole were honored, now things hateful
to God are done, and God postpones the vengeance! O forbearance of God, how
did you endure such things? It would have been better that the earth,
splitting open, should cover them, than that these things be changed over in
this manner; it would have been better that these here should have fallen by
one shaking of the earth and that not even remnants at least be left behind in
the place; for then the suffering would not have so bitten the souls of those
who look on. To us even to enter within the sanctuary seemed to be fearful,
and it was enterable only for those appointed in this, who had first purified
soul and body for themselves. But now, alas, it is thrown open to the
abominable, and they are not kept from such daring, and neither does the earth
split and bury those who go against God, nor does the sun burn them from
above, nor does heaven send down a violent rain and sink the lawless deed.
Where, with sobriety of mind and of the senses, the strains of the divine
oracles were sung and the minds above were singing with them, now a cry of
inarticulate voices is raised, and the tribe of demons takes pleasure, seeing
the tongues that served the all-night songs fallen silent. Woe is me! how
could I bear the memory of these things without tears? Where he himself who
became among us for our sake was seen living, and the people was abashed as
seeing angels, deeds of lawlessness are dared by those opposed to God, and
thunderbolts are not brought down from heaven. O places consecrated to God,
you have been stripped of the divine grace in which you were rich, and you are
reckoned as nothing by the daring ones. O, how the season of our prosperity
has become one of assault and overrunning, and having been left behind we have
become shame and reproach! The festivals are gone, the splendors of the divine
images are gone =76=, freedom of speech has perished, the order of the monks
has been driven out, and the gray hairs of the presbyters and the dignity of
the deacons are reckoned among the dishonored. Is not our life unlivable, and
is dying less bitter than living for those struck each day by such bitter
pains? Or surely all would speak with me, who were by nature to suffer in such
things.

I have often been at a loss in myself, on account of what these things are and
how we who have suffered have come to so great a misfortune, and I found the
divine word resolving the difficulty for me, saying, "Your land shall be
desolate and your dwellings desolate, because you walked contrary to me, and I
too will walk toward you in a contrary wrath"; and from this, leaving off from
saying more, I have honored it by being silent. For those who have sinned
unforgivably must propitiate God and not speak at length for display, for we
are the causes of what has grown, according to the one who said it, having
cast down such seeds. Therefore all that is nourishing of piety and of right
doctrines of the things according to virtue, of which works we have been shown
to have been neglectful, having again brought in zeal to take hold of them,
let us by every means contend to obtain God as gracious by the works in which
he rejoices, so that from this we may sing a palinode most gladly.
