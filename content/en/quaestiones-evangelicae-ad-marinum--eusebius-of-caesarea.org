A BRIEF SELECTION FROM THE SAME EUSEBIUS TO MARINUS CONCERNING THE QUESTIONS
AND SOLUTIONS IN THE GOSPELS.

Having already previously composed two treatises of the questions and
solutions that are in doubt about the beginning in the God-inspired Gospels, I
now come, having passed over the middle parts, then to those at the end of the
same always inquired into by all; perhaps the will of God having urged us to
this through your commands, Marinus, my most honored and most labor-loving
son. And you asked first: Αʹ. How according to Matthew the Savior appears to
have been raised late on the Sabbaths, but according to Mark early on the
first of the Sabbaths.

αʹ. The solution of this would be twofold. For one, rejecting the chapter
itself, the pericope that says this, would say that it is not carried in all
the copies of the Gospel according to Mark. At any rate the accurate copies
define the end of the history according to Mark at the words of the young man
who appeared to the women and said to them, "Do not be afraid; you seek Jesus
the Nazarene"; and the following, to which he adds: "and having heard they
fled, and said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." For in this nearly in
all the copies of the Gospel according to Mark the end is defined; but the
things that follow, being carried rarely in some but not in all, would be
superfluous, and especially if they should have a contradiction to the
testimony of the rest of the evangelists. These things, then, someone would
say who declines and altogether does away with a superfluous question. But
another, daring to reject =22.940= nothing of the things carried in any way in
the writing of the Gospels, says that the reading is double, as also in many
other places, and that each is to be accepted, inasmuch as this is not more
approved than that, nor that than this, among the faithful and devout.

βʹ. And since this part is granted to be true, it is fitting to interpret the
sense of the reading. If, at any rate, we should divide the meaning of the
saying, we would not find it contrary to the things said by Matthew, that the
Savior was raised late on the Sabbaths. For "and having risen early on the
first of the Sabbath" according to Mark, we shall read with a distinction; and
after "and having risen," we shall put a comma; and we shall separate the
sense from the things said next. Then "having risen" we would refer to the
"late on the Sabbaths" in Matthew; for then he had been raised; but the next,
being constitutive of another sense, we shall join to the things said next:
for early on the first of the Sabbath he appeared to Mary Magdalene. This, at
any rate, John also made clear, himself likewise testifying that he was seen
by the Magdalene early on the first of the Sabbath. Thus then also according
to Mark he appeared to her early; not having risen early, but much earlier
according to Matthew late on the Sabbath; for then having risen he appeared to
Mary, not then, but early; so that two times are presented in these: that of
the resurrection, late on the Sabbath; and that of the Savior's appearing, the
early morning, which Mark wrote, saying what must also be read with a
distinction: "and having risen"; then, having put a comma, one must say the
next: "early on the first of the Sabbath he appeared to Mary Magdalene, from
whom he had cast out seven demons."




Βʹ. How according to Matthew the Magdalene, having seen the resurrection late
on the Sabbaths, according to John the same woman stands weeping at the tomb
on the first of the Sabbath.

αʹ. Nothing would be inquired into at these places, if we should not suppose
"late on the Sabbaths" to mean the evening hour after the day of the Sabbath,
as some have supposed, but the late and delayed time of the night =22.941=
after the Sabbath. For thus we are also accustomed to say "late in the hour,"
and "late in the season," and "late for the need"; not indicating evening, nor
the time after sunset, but signifying by this manner what is very delayed.
Whence Matthew, as interpreting himself, after "late on the Sabbaths," added
"at the dawning," he says; that is, at the hour now already beginning to show
itself, and dawning toward the Lord's day, which was late and now far advanced
from the Sabbaths. And it has been said "late of the Sabbath" by the one who
interpreted the Scripture. For the evangelist Matthew delivered the Gospel in
the Hebrew tongue; but the one who transferred it into the Greek language
called the hour dawning toward the Lord's day "late on the Sabbaths"; so that
nearly the same time, or one very near, is understood among the evangelists,
observed under different names; and that Matthew, having said: "And late on
the Sabbaths, at the dawning toward the first of the Sabbaths, Mary Magdalene
and the other Mary came to see the tomb," differs in nothing from John who
said: "And on the first of the Sabbaths Mary Magdalene comes early to the
tomb, while it was still dark." For they indicate broadly one and the same
time with different words. Matthew, having named "late" instead of "later,"
and late of the night; having named it early, the interpreter added "it being
dark," so that no one should suppose him to mean the dawn; as also Matthew, to
"late on the Sabbaths," lest anyone should think the evening hour to be meant,
added "at the dawning toward the first of the Sabbaths." Since this one also
said precisely the late of Sabbath, lest anyone should suppose the evening
after sunset to be meant, but he says late of Sabbaths.

βʹ. And it is a custom to call the whole week Sabbath, and to name all the
days thus. At any rate it is said among the evangelists "on the first of the
Sabbaths"; and in common usage, second of the Sabbaths, and third of the
Sabbaths, and fourth of the Sabbaths. Thus then Matthew named the time dawning
toward the morning of the Lord's day "late of Sabbaths"; not having said
evening of the Sabbath, nor late of Sabbath; since we would have had to break
the fast on the day of the Sabbath when evening came after sunset; and no
longer to rejoice on the Lord's day, but on the evening of the Sabbath, if the
evangelist had indicated this. But we are not accustomed to dissolve the fasts
at evening of the Sabbath, but either when night has come on, at midnight
itself, or around cockcrow, or about dawn; so that also from the matter
itself, and from the custom that has prevailed in the Churches of God, the
time indicated through "late on the Sabbaths" is not the evening hour, but
this which Matthew himself presented, saying "at the dawning toward the first
of the Sabbaths." For it would not even make sense, such wonders being
accomplished about the tomb of the Savior at the evening of the Sabbath, that
all who dwell in the city should not have learned the things taking =22.944=
place, and a concourse have come to the tomb, all being awake. And it was
consistent, the stone having been rolled away miraculously, that the guards
should immediately hasten to report the things done, the hour permitting them.
But they are also taught on the next day by the chief priests to spread about
to all, that his disciples came by night and stole him while we were sleeping;
which they would have had no room


to fabricate if he had been raised in the evening. But I think it is shown
through these things that what is said in Matthew, "late on the Sabbaths,"
does not signify the late hour of the Sabbath, nor the evening time; but
Matthew himself added the hour dawning toward the first of the Sabbaths, which
was early, while it was still dark according to John. For thus the agreement
of the Gospel voices would run together; as one time being signified through
them, but the portions of the time being interchanged in each; since even of
one and the same hour it is possible to conceive the beginning, and the
middle, and the end.

γʹ. You would not, at any rate, err in saying that the first things of the
resurrection of our Savior are indicated in John, with whom the Magdalene
early, while it was still dark, having come first and second to the tomb, and
not finding the body of the Savior, weeps because no one yet knew about his
resurrection; and that a second part of the same hour is the time in Matthew,
at which the same Magdalene, coming third to the tomb together with the other
Mary, no longer weeps, as having already in John seen the angels and the
Savior himself. For the things in Luke and Mark would be indicative of other
visits, with whom more women come to the viewing. And consider that the
Magdalene met at times not standing far from one another, but that she
happened to be present in the same hour and at the same place both the first
and the second time: the first, by herself alone; the second, with the other
Mary. Thus then the same Mary Magdalene both saw the things in Matthew and the
things in John; and she was not left out of the things recorded among the
many; for she did not depart from the place; but she was present and remained,
astonished at the things she had seen, and longing, in addition to the first,
to be deemed worthy also of second and third theophanies; which she obtained
after these things, more women arriving at the tomb in various ways, and an
angelic vision appearing to them now in one way, now in another, while she
herself was present at each viewing. Thus, at any rate, the Magdalene became a
spectator of the things recorded among the four evangelists; wherefore she was
also mentioned among them all. Thus also the time was the same that was
presented by John and by Matthew; but different intervals of the same time
were observed in each.

δʹ. And let it not trouble you that it is said in Matthew, after the two Marys
came to see the =22.945= tomb, "for the angel of the Lord, having descended
from heaven, rolled away the stone from the door." For it is not fitting to
think that the angel rolled away the stone at that very hour. For how? When it
already existed in John, with whom not Mary alone, but also two disciples have
come to the tomb. Wherefore you would say that the account in Matthew is
narrative of the things that had happened before this. For according to him
the two Marys came to see the tomb, but they found it opened, since before
this a great earthquake had occurred, and the angel had rolled away the stone,
who, standing by, again announces the good news to the women. This, then,
would be one solution of the things in doubt at this place.

εʹ. And the matters at hand would also be solved otherwise, if you should
suppose the Marys in Matthew to be other, and the one in John other. For we
find four Marys in all present at the passion of the Savior with the other
women: first, then, the Theotokos, the mother of the Savior himself; second,
her sister Mary, the one of Clopas; then third, Mary Magdalene; and fourth,
the mother of James and Joseph. And of the first three Marys John made
mention, saying thus: "And there stood by the cross of Jesus his mother, and
his mother's sister Mary of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene." And of the fourth
Mary, the mother of James and


Joseph, the remaining three evangelists made mention, taking her also together
with the Magdalene. Matthew saying thus: "And there were there many women
looking on from afar, who followed him; among whom was Mary Magdalene, and
Mary of James and Joseph, who saw the place where he was laid." And Luke also
relates concerning the other Mary thus: "And there were Magdalene Mary and
Joanna and Mary of James and the rest with them."

ςʹ. Of these four Marys, then, if you should take the two in Matthew who came
to the tomb late on the Sabbaths at the dawning toward the first of the
Sabbaths, and who saw the angel, to be other than the one who according to
John arrived alone early on the first of the Sabbaths, while it was still
dark, not knowing of the =22.948= resurrection, and therefore weeping, nothing
crooked would meet us, all difficulty and inquiry being taken out of the way;
and late on the Sabbaths, according to the things given, the things written in
Matthew having been done in the presence of the two Marys, and early on the
first of the Sabbaths another Mary having seen the things in John, both those
and these being true, and the places containing no contradiction, neither
according to the times, nor according to the persons, nor according to the
words.

ζʹ. But if the name of the Magdalene attached in both evangelists troubles the
sense, still it is not fitting to confuse the divine Scripture for the sake of
one word or name, which often happens to be attached even according to a
scribal error. For either these two also are to be thought to come from one
city or village, Magdala; or the surname of the Magdalene is attached upon one
of them, the scribe having once erred at the beginning, because from that
error those after him followed the first error. And this, having happened also
in another case, we shall show a little later. But just as in such cases it
was rightly dictated at the beginning, but by error of those after these who
were not accurate in the transmission, it has come about that certain things
are inquired into, so also you would say it has happened in the case of the
surname of the Magdalene lying superfluously upon one Mary; which being
subtracted, every inquiry is defined, no one any longer being in doubt at
these places. But also late on the Sabbaths, that is in deep night, the things
done in Matthew having been seen by the Magdalene and the other Mary; and
early, while it was still dark, another Mary having arrived at the same place;
and first being at a loss at not finding the body of the Savior; and later
herself also seeing him with her own eyes. But it is better not even to charge
an error at these places, but to say that there were truly two Magdalenes, as
we have also shown the Marys to be four; of whom it is nothing strange to say
that two Marys came from the same Magdala, and no longer to be in doubt at
all, but that the Magdalene late on the Sabbaths in Matthew is other, and
again another, herself also a Magdalene, the one in John who came early to the
tomb; and that this is the one indicated also in Mark, according to some of
the copies, from whom he had cast out seven demons; and this is likely the one
who heard "Do not touch me," but not the one in Matthew. For even if she too
most of all originated from Magdala, yet the divine Scripture does not charge
the like things of her as well.

Γʹ. How according to Matthew the Magdalene, late on the Sabbaths with the
other Mary, having touched the feet of the Savior, the same woman early on the
first of the Sabbath hears "Do not touch me" according to John.




αʹ. If then Mary should be one and the same in =22.949= both evangelists, we
shall say that the same woman often came to the same place, since the
astonishment of the matter and the joy at what had happened drew her. Having
then met first and having first seen the things in John, she went off in haste
to the apostles first, announcing the opening of the tomb; then, having seen
the resurrection, she was distressed, supposing the body of Jesus to have been
taken from the tomb, and not knowing where it had been laid; then she returned
a second time with them to the tomb; and when those turned homeward, being
left alone again in the place, she stood weeping; then, having stooped into
the tomb, she saw the two angels; then she also sees the Savior himself. Who,
since she stood weeping, and was disposed in a human and lowly way, as if his
body had been stolen, and thinking things unworthy concerning him, first names
her woman, rebuking her and reproaching the womanly passion; wherefore he
says, "Woman, why do you weep?" Then he says to her: "Mary"; through the name
reminding her of herself, and of the words long ago to her and to the rest of
the disciples concerning his resurrection. And she, having come to awareness,
and having recognized from the voice and the power of the word what it was,
says "Rabboni," which is interpreted Teacher.

βʹ. Then, since she was still rushing to approach him as a teacher and not as
God, he refuses and declines her, "Do not touch me"; for still thinking mortal
things, she was not able to touch his divinity. For it was not fitting that
she who was still weeping, and seeking him below about the tombs and graves as
a dead man, and holding lowly and human opinions concerning him, should share
in his touch. Wherefore he proved the cause: for he says he has not yet
ascended, as far as it was in her, to the Father, since she did not believe
this to have happened, but thought him to lie somewhere dead. Wherefore he
says to her: "Do not touch me," being such a one and reasoning such things
concerning me; for to you I have not yet been believed as God; to you I have
still been reasoned to be below. Thus the one weeping and having supposed him
to be the gardener, through "Do not touch me" he turned away, rebuking her; as
also through calling her woman, and through saying, "Why do you weep?" For to
weep for the living, or rather for life itself, was of extreme ignorance. And
through these things he rebuked her; but through the following he taught the
theology concerning himself. And she, greatly benefited by these things, again
withdraws from the tomb; this a second time; then, having told the other Mary
the things seen in John, she returned with her; this a third time; and she
became a spectator of the things mentioned in Matthew, no longer alone, but
with the other Mary; and no longer stooping inside the tomb as in John, nor
seeing two angels, but one sitting at the stone.

γʹ. Then upon these things she again sees the Savior himself with the other
Mary; this a second time; and she no longer =22.952= hears "Do not touch me,"
but on the contrary is commanded by him to rejoice instead of weeping; but she
is also permitted to touch him, since she also worships him as God. At any
rate Jesus says to them, "Rejoice"; and they, having come forward, took hold
of his feet and worshipped him. And thus at once the holy Gospels are true,
disagreeing in no account; introducing the same Mary Magdalene both not
touching the Savior before, when she wept and disbelieved; and touching him,
when she was commanded to rejoice. And we have shown that the things indicated
in John are to be held first of the things carried in Matthew, also through
the things exercised by us before according to one of the interpretations,
having made clear how "late on the Sabbaths" has been said in Matthew, the
evangelist not indicating the evening hour, but the time dawning toward the
first of the Sabbaths; and this was second to that indicated in John. And let
these things be said, if someone should altogether


maintain that Mary is the same in both evangelists, John and Matthew.

δʹ. But if it should be granted that she is not the same, but that the one who
according to Matthew met late on the Sabbaths with the other Mary is other,
and other the one in John who came alone to the tomb early on the first of the
Sabbaths, while it was still dark, every ambiguity would be solved: that the
first women, having arrived late on the Sabbaths, as being more zealous and
more faithful, both heard "rejoice" from the Savior, and worshipped, and were
deemed worthy to take hold of his feet; but that the Mary in John, being other
than those, met later and early; and that this is herself according to Mark,
from whom he had cast out seven demons; and that her soul was greatly troubled
and she was more unbelieving, so as to stand weeping, and to suppose the body
of the Savior to have been taken from the tomb, and to have been transferred
to another land; and her soul was so confused that she was not even astonished
at the two angels seen by her inside the tomb, nor did she recognize the
Savior himself when he appeared to her, but thought him to be the gardener.

Δʹ. Concerning the tomb and the seeming disagreement. How according to Matthew
Mary Magdalene with the other Mary saw outside the tomb the one angel sitting
upon the stone of the tomb; and how according to John Mary Magdalene sees two
angels sitting inside the tomb; and according to Luke two men stood by the
women; and according to Mark the one seen by them was a young man sitting on
the right of the tomb, to Mary Magdalene and Mary of James and Salome.

αʹ. The things in Matthew come first; whence also the two Marys saw the angel
just then standing by and having rolled away the stone; but the things in
=22.953= John happen later, two angels having been seen inside the tomb, being
other than the one who appeared outside and sat upon the stone, as Matthew
says. And what is said in Luke, that two men appeared in flashing clothing;
and further also the young man in Mark, clothed in a white robe, and seen on
the right and not on the left, the one announcing the bright and right-hand
things to the women, would themselves also be other than one another, and than
the others spoken of in the first evangelists. Wherefore these did not even
name them angels. And Mark and Luke did not even mention the things in John
and Matthew, I mean the appearances of the Savior; but they left the greater
things for the greater, Matthew and John, to say; themselves having said the
second things, and filling up what was passed over in silence by those.

βʹ. It is possible then to say thus, that there being four evangelists, equal
in number to these also appear the visions recorded by them; and the times are
four, and those who appeared at each time distinctively; and likewise also the
women who saw are different, and the words spoken to them by those who
appeared vary. How then is this? First then is the time in Matthew late on the
Sabbaths, where Mary Magdalene with the other, outside the tomb, when also an
earthquake occurred, saw one saying thus, "Do not be afraid, you; for I know
that you seek Jesus the crucified; he is not here; for he has been raised;
come, see." And fourth and last is the one in Mark, when the sun had risen,
the =22.956= young man who appeared to the women, to Mary Magdalene and to
Mary of


James and Salome, who having come with spices heard, "Do not be amazed; you
seek Jesus the Nazarene, the crucified." And in the middle are those in John
and in Luke, who also appeared at a distinctive time. For late on the Sabbaths
there appeared one angel outside the tomb; with whom also the Savior himself;
and at deep dawn, others, the two named men according to Luke, not seen inside
the tomb; last of all the young man, and before this and those in Luke the two
inside the tomb.

γʹ. That Luke says on one of the Sabbaths, at deep dawn, two women who
followed him bring spices, who were those who had followed together from
Galilee, when they buried him, having come to the tomb; who saw two angels,
who also said, "Why do you seek the living among the dead? He is not here, but
has been raised; remember how he spoke to you while still being with you,
saying that the Son of Man must suffer," and the rest.

δʹ. That John says on the first of the Sabbaths Mary Magdalene comes alone to
the tomb, it being still dark, and sees the stone taken away and comes to
Simon and to John and says, "They have taken the Lord from the tomb and I do
not know where they have laid him; Peter and John therefore came to the tomb,"
and the rest. Then he introduces the Magdalene weeping, and having stooped to
see, he says she saw two angels sitting, one at the head, and one at the feet;
and they say to her, "Woman, why do you weep?" and she said, "They have taken
my Lord and I do not know where they have laid him." Then she turned back, and
saw Jesus standing; and she did not know that it was Jesus; and Jesus says,
"Woman, why do you weep? Whom do you seek?"

εʹ. That some are at a loss how three days and three nights are fulfilled, as
Christ said, those of the resurrection. And it is possible to say: some count
from the betrayal; and others make the Preparation into two, since night came
to be and again day; on that day the sun having been darkened and again
kindled; then the whole day of the Sabbath and its night. And others count as
day the whole Preparation and its night, the whole Sabbath and its night; and
of the Lord's day they measure the beginning as a whole day; as, it having
already begun then, Christ was raised; thus they say three days. Since also in
the case of the dead and of newborn children it is the custom thus to measure
a whole day, when we measure the birth that began from the tenth hour, or the
birth that began from the beginning of the day; likewise also in the case of
the dead, doing the customary things on the third day and the ninth and the
fortieth: not the whole third with its night; nor the whole ninth with its own
night; nor likewise the fortieth whole with its night; but seeing the
beginnings of the last days we accomplish the things done, both measuring and
reckoning this as a whole day.

ςʹ. But since Christ says, I shall be three days and three nights in the heart
of the earth, it is possible to say thus: if we should see a debtor who
promised his own creditor that he would pay the debt after three days, having
paid before the appointed time, shall we judge him as having lied, or as
having been more true? And otherwise: if he rose sooner than he said, the
power is greater, and he has no accusation; but the later is full of suspicion
and is reckoned as a lie coming to be; a lie, because the stated bound has
passed; and suspect, because when the guards had withdrawn, the matter would
have been thought a theft.
