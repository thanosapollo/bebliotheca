* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Men of Carthage, always princes of Africa, noble by antiquity and fortunate in
your newness, I am glad the times treat you so well that you have both leisure
and pleasure to remark on dress. These are the idlenesses of peace and of the
grain supply.

All is well from the empire and from the sky. Yet you too once wore another
dress. Your tunics were famous for the care of the weft, the arrangement of
the sheen, and the tempering of the measure. They were neither wasteful below
the shins nor immodest above the knees, neither stingy to the arms nor tight
at the hands. A girdle ready to split the fold did not even shade them. They
hung square and even on the men who wore them. The pallium worn outside was
itself four-cornered too. It was thrown back from both sides, drawn tight
around the neck in the clasp's bite, and used to rest on the shoulders. Its
likeness
today is the priesthood of Aesculapius, who is now yours. So too the sister
city nearby used to dress, and Tyre wherever else in Africa. But when the urn
of worldly lots shifted and God preferred the Romans, the sister city hurried
of her own will to change, so that she might already have greeted Scipio on
his landing by her dress, prematurely Roman. For you, after the benefit that
came of injury, which took old age from you and not your height of rank, after
the obscene omens of Gracchus and the violent mockeries of Lepidus, after
Pompey's three altars and Caesar's long delays, when Statilius Taurus set the
walls and Sentius Saturninus recited the solemn rites, when concord is at
hand, the toga was offered. Ah, how far it wandered, from the Pelasgians to
the Lydians, from the Lydians to the Romans, so that from the shoulders of a
loftier people it might embrace the Carthaginians! From then on you hang a
longer tunic by a deciding girdle, and you prop the overflow of a pallium now
rounded with a piled gathering of folds. If condition or rank or season
clothes you with anything besides, you still remark, even forgetfully, on the
pallium as generally yours. Indeed I do not wonder, given the earlier proof.
For even the ram (not the one Laberius calls back-horned and wool-skinned and
testicle-dragging, but the beam that is a machine which serves in war to break
walls) was first armed, they say, by Carthage, fiercest of all in the pursuits
of war, for the swing of a hanging onset. No one had yet poised such a thing.
She invented the force of the engine from the rage of a beast that avenges its
head. Yet when the times of the fatherland were ending and a ram already Roman
dared against the walls that were in a way its own, the Carthaginians were
stunned at once as at a new foreign device:

So great is the power of long-drawn time to change what is old!

So, in short, the pallium is not recognized either.

Let the matter now come from another source, lest a Punic thing blush or
grieve among Romans. To change dress is, at any rate, a solemn office of all
nature. This world itself, on which we rest, also performs it for now. Let
Anaximander see to it if he thinks there are more worlds. Let anyone else see
to it, anywhere, among the Meropes, as Silenus babbles at the ears of Midas,
well fitted indeed for taller tales. But even if Plato reckons some world of
which this is the image, that one too must of necessity change likewise. For
if a world is made of diverse substances and functions, matching the pattern
of this world here (it would not be a world otherwise), then diversity is
gathered into one by change. The taking of turns binds the quarrel of
difference. So every world built from diversities and tempered by turns will
exist by changing. Our own lodging, at any rate, which is clear even with
closed eyes, or with eyes wholly Homeric, is altogether of changing form. Day
and night take turns. The sun varies by yearly stations, the moon by monthly
measures. The distinct confusion of the stars sometimes casts something down,
sometimes raises it again. The circuit of the sky is now bright under open
air, now dirty with cloud. Or rains rush down, and whatever missiles come with
rains; then a drizzle, and again fair weather. So too the sea has a bad name
for good faith: while the winds change equally, it looks honest in a calm,
temperate in a ripple, and at once it grows restless with huge waves.

So too if you review the earth, which loves to be clothed by seasons, you
would almost deny it is the same, remembering it green when you see it yellow,
soon to see it white as well. What of its other ornaments do not change from
one thing into another: mountain ridges wearing down, spring-veins playing
false, river-courses silting over? The whole world too once changed, covered
by all the waters. Still the shells and trumpets of the sea wander on the
mountains, wanting to prove to Plato that even the heights once floated. But
by swimming out again the world changed form again, another yet the same. It
even now changes dress locally, when a site is injured: when among islands
Delos is already none, Samos is sands, and the Sibyl is no liar; when in the
Atlantic something matching Libya or Asia is sought; when a former side of
Italy, shaken by the Adriatic and the Tyrrhenian, is cut through the middle
and makes Sicily of the remnants; when that whole tract of the split, twisting
back the contentious meetings of the waters in narrows, puts a new vice on the
sea, not of spitting wrecks but of swallowing them. The mainland too suffers
from the sky or from itself. Look at Palestine. Where the river Jordan is
umpire of the borders, there is a huge waste, a bereft region, and a field in
vain. Behind there were cities, and crowded peoples, and it alone used to
hear. Then, since God is a censor, impiety earned rains of fire: thus far
Sodom, and no Gomorrah, and all is ash, and the nearness of the sea lives
death together with the soil. From a cloud of this kind Tuscany too burned the
old Volsinii, that Campania may hope all the more from her mountains, now that
Pompeii has been snatched. But far be it. Would that Asia too were already
safe from the gullet of the soil! Would that Africa too had once for all
feared the chasm, atoned by the cheating of a single camp! Many other such
losses have renewed the world's dress and moved its sites. Wars too have been
allowed a great deal. But it is tedious to review sad things no less than the
turns of kingdoms, how often even these have changed from Ninus, offspring of
Bel, onward; if indeed Ninus was the first to reign, as the impiety of earlier
men asserts. Among you the pen is generally not used to go further. From the
Assyrians, perhaps, the histories of time open. We, however, who keep reading
the divine books, are equipped from the very birthdays of the world. But now
for the glad things after the bad, since even glad things change. Finally, if
the sea has washed something away, the sky has burned it, the earth has
withdrawn it, the sword has shorn it, it returns compensated by another turn.
For at first the earth, empty and vacant of men over a greater circuit, and if
anywhere some nation had occupied it, was for itself alone. So, understanding
at last that it must cultivate all things, crowding in one place, deserting in
another, it took counsel to weed and to grub, so that from there, as from
slips and layers, peoples from peoples, cities from cities, might be planted
throughout the world. Swarms of overflowing nations flew across. Scythians
overflow the Persians, Phoenicians belch into Africa, Phrygians give birth to
Romans, Chaldaean seed is brought up into Egypt, then when it is led across
from there, it is the Jewish nation. So too the posterity of Hercules,
occupying the Peloponnese together with Temenus, extend the line. So too the
Ionians, companions of Neleus, furnish Asia with new cities; so too the
Corinthians with Archias fortify Syracuse. But antiquity is already vain, when
our own courses are before us. How much this present time has reshaped the
world! How many cities the triple virtue of the present empire has either
produced or increased or restored! With God favoring so many Augusti as one,
how many censuses transcribed? how many peoples overcome? how many ranks made
illustrious? how many barbarians shut out? Truly the world is the most
cultivated farm of this empire, every aconite of hostility rooted out and the
cactus and bramble of treacherous intimacy torn up, and pleasant beyond the
orchard of Alcinous and the rose-garden of Midas. So, while you praise a
changing world, why do you remark on a man?

Beasts too change form instead of clothing. And yet the peacock's plumage is
clothing, and festival clothing at that. Where the neck flowers it is richer
than any purple dye; where the back shines it is more gilded than any border;
where the tail lies it is more flowing than any tragic train. It is
many-colored, party-colored, and change-colored: never itself, always another,
and yet always itself when it is another. It has to change as often as it has
to move. The serpent too must be named, even if after the peacock; na(??) he
also changes what he was allotted, hide and lifetime alike. For when he has
felt old age, he packs himself into a narrow place. He enters a cave and
leaves his skin at the same time; scraped from the very threshold at once, he
unfolds new, and leaves the slough there. With the scales the years too are
refused. The hyena, if you watch, is of yearly sex: it alternates male and
female. I am silent on the stag, because he too, umpire of his own lifetime,
fed on a serpent, droops by poison into youth. There is also

A four-footed beast, slow-stepping, rustic, low, rough.

Do you think it the tortoise of Pacuvius? It is not. Another little beast too
takes the verse, of middling size indeed, but a great name. If you hear of the
chameleon without knowing it beforehand, you will already fear something
larger than a lion. But when you come upon it at a vineyard, almost wholly
under a vine-shoot, you will laugh at once at the outstanding boldness of the
name. There is not even juice in the body, as is clear from creatures much
smaller. The chameleon lives by a little skin. The head starts at once from
the back, for it has no neck. Turning is therefore hard, but the scouting
little eyes, or rather points of light, spin about. Dull and tired, it
scarcely lifts itself from the ground. It labors a dazed walk and pushes on.
It shows a step more than it takes one. It is always fasting, of course, and
never fails. It feeds by gaping, chews by puffing, and takes its food from the
wind. Yet the chameleon too can change wholly, and nothing else. It has one
proper color, but it is suffused from whatever it has approached. This alone
has been granted to the chameleon, as the common saying has it: to play with
its own hide. Much had to be said so that we might arrive at man with the
ground prepared. From whatever beginning you take him, he stood naked and
unclothed before his potter. Afterward he seizes a wisdom not yet permitted,
taken too soon. There he hurries to cover what in a new body was not yet due
to shame, and wraps himself for the time in fig leaves. Then he is driven from
the place of origin, for he had done wrong, and he is given to the world as to
a mine, clothed in skins. But those things are secret, and not for all to
know. Come now with something from your own store, which the Egyptians tell
and Alexander arranges and the mother reads, from the time of Osiris when
Ammon, rich in sheep, comes to him from Libya. Finally they claim that
Mercury, delighted perhaps by the softness of a ram he had stroked, flayed a
little sheep. While he tries it, and as the easiness of the stuff invited, he
draws out a thread with a following pull, and wove it in the manner of an
earlier net which he had joined with bands of linden bark. But you have
preferred to give Minerva the whole management of wool-work and the structure
of the webs, though with Arachne there was a more careful workshop. From then
on there was material. I do not speak of Milesian and Selgic and Altinian
sheep, or those for which Tarentum or Baetica is famed, with nature coloring.
Even trees clothe, and flax, grassy after its greenness, grows white by
washing. It was not enough to plant and sow a tunic, unless it had also been
granted to fish for clothing. For from the sea too there are fleeces, where
shells more choice of mossy wooliness put forth hair. It is no secret at all
that the silkworm (it is a kind of grub) strains through the air, stretches
more aptly than the hour-threads of spiders, then swallows, and soon gives
them back unharmed. Accordingly, if you kill it, you will wind already living
threads. So great a provision of materials, then, the wits of clothing-work
have also followed, first for covering man, where necessity went ahead, then
also for adorning him, or rather for puffing him up, where ambition followed,
and have published various forms of garments. Of these a part is worn by
particular nations, not shared with the rest. A part, however, is used
promiscuously, useful to all, as this pallium. Even if it is more Greek, by
tongue it is already in Latium's keeping. With the voice the garment entered.
And so even he who voted to keep the Greeks outside the city, already an old
man learned in their letters and speech, that same Cato, at the time of his
own court, with the shoulder bared, no less favored the Greeks in pallium
dress.

What now, if Roman-ness is salvation for all, and yet you are not quite Greeks
even in honorable things? Or, if that is not so, from what nation, in
provinces better trained, which nature fitted rather for wrestling with the
field, come the pursuits of the palaestra, aging badly and toiling in vain,
and the muddy anointing and the dusty rolling and the dry fattening? Whence
among some Numidians, even those with horse-like manes, is the barber next to
the skin, and the crown of the head alone immune from the knife? Whence among
the shaggy and the bristly is resin so greedy from the armpit, the tweezers so
thieving from the chin?

It is a prodigy that these things are done without the pallium. This whole
business of Asia belongs to it. What have you, Libya and Europe, to do with
the refinements of the covered track, which you do not know how to wear? For
truly, what sort of thing is it to be depilated in Greek fashion rather than
clothed? To transfer dress is only then nearly a fault if it is not custom
that is changed, but nature. There is difference enough between the honor of
the time and religion. Let custom give credit to the time, nature to God. So
the hero of Larissa shook nature by changing into a maiden. He had been reared
on the marrows of wild beasts (whence also the composition of his name, since
his lips had gone without the taste of breasts). He had been schooled in a
stony school by a rustic, wood-dwelling, monstrous tutor. Grant that as a boy
he suffered a mother's anxiety among beasts. He was already a little actor. He
had already secretly played the man for someone. Still he endures to let the
stole flow, to arrange the hair, to paint the skin, to consult the mirror, to
stroke the neck, and even to bore the ear with iron, made womanish. The round
work at Sigeum keeps that for him. Plainly afterward he is a soldier, for
necessity gave the sex back. There had been a sound of battle, and arms were
not far. The iron itself, he says, draws the man. But if after the war-call he
had persisted as a girl as well, he could even have married. See, then, the
change. A twin monster indeed: from man a woman, then from woman a man, when
neither should truth have been denied nor deceit confessed. Each change of
dress is bad, one against nature, the other against safety.

Lust has transfigured a man by dress still more foully than some maternal
fright, even though you worship the man who ought to make you blush, that
club-arrow-hide-bearer who paid the whole lot of his epithet with a woman's
dress. So much was allowed to the Lydian woman in secret that Hercules was
prostituted in Omphale, and Omphale in Hercules. Where is Diomedes, and the
bloody mangers? Where is Busiris, and his pyre-altars? Where is Geryon, thrice
one? He still preferred the club to stink of their brains, when he was
offended by ointments. The already old blood of the Hydra and the Centaurs was
being squeezed out of the arrows with the pumice of the point, while luxury
mocked, so that after the monsters were transfixed they might perhaps sew a
garland. Not even the shoulders of a sober woman or of some virago could have
entered under the spoils of so great a beast unless they had long been
softened and unstrengthened and deodorized (which I hope was done at Omphale's
with balsam or fenugreek oil; I believe the mane too suffered a comb), lest
the lion's scab burn an unnerved neck. The gape stuffed with hair, the
grinders shaded among the forelocks, would bellow with the whole insult of the
face, if it could. Nemea at any rate, if there is any genius of the place, was
groaning; for then she looked around and saw she had lost her lion. What that
Hercules was in Omphale's silk, Omphale, already shown in Hercules' hide, has
described.

But there is also the boxer Cleomachus, who had approached before the
Tirynthian. Later, at Olympia, by an incredible change, he melted from a male,
cut within the skin and beyond, now to be crowned among the Novian Fullers,
and deservedly commemorated by the mime-writer Lentulus in the Catinensians.
As he occupied the traces of the boxing-straps with bracelets, so too he
thrust out the coarse wrap of the athlete's cloak with some fine-spun suit.
Physcon and Sardanapalus must be passed in silence. If they were not notorious
for lusts, no one would know them as kings otherwise. It must be passed in
silence, however, lest they too mutter something about certain of your Caesars
equally shameful, lest perhaps a mandate be given to canine frankness to mark
a Caesar more impure than Physcon and softer than Sardanapalus, and indeed a
Subnero. Nec(??) empty glory is no cooler a force when it comes to changing
clothes, even when manhood is left intact. Every passion is a heat; but when
it is fanned into affectation, it is already a burning from the fire of glory.
You have, then, from this tinder a great king seething, lesser only than
glory. He had conquered the Median nation, and was conquered by Median dress.
He put off the triumphal scale-armor and went away in captive trousers. He
covered the breast carved with the seals of scales with a transparent weave
and so stripped it, still panting from the work of war, and, that it might be
softer, he quenched it with silk fanning. It was not enough that the
Macedonian was swollen in spirit, unless a still puffier garment had also
delighted him; except that even the philosophers themselves, I think, affect
something of the kind. For I hear that there has been philosophizing in purple
too. If a philosopher in purple, why not also in a slipper? To be shod in
Tyrian unless with gold is by no means to go Greek. Someone will say, Yet
another both silk-clad and bronze-shod in the sandal has walked. Worthily
indeed, that something might tinkle under the raving garments, he walked with
a cymbal. But if already then and there Diogenes had been barking from the
tub, he would not have stopped at muddy feet, as Plato's couches know. He
would have carried Empedocles altogether into the inner rooms of Cloacina, so
that he who had raved himself a heavenly being might greet first his sisters,
then men, as a god.

Such dress, which turns away from nature and modesty, it would be right to
transfix with the eye, to point at with the finger, and to hand over with a
nod. In short, if anyone with a Menandrian flow drags a delicate garment on
the ground, let him hear at his side what the comic poet says: What a cloak is
that madman ruining? But now the brow of censorial attention has long been
scattered. As far as remark goes, promiscuity offers freedmen in equestrian
dress, the flogged in the dress of the free, the surrendered in that of the
free-born, bumpkins in that of city men, buffoons in that of the forum,
civilians in that of soldiers. The corpse-bearer, the pimp, and the trainer of
gladiators are clothed with you. Turn also to the women. You have to look at
what Caecina Severus pressed gravely on the senate: matrons without the stola
in public. Finally, by the resolutions of Lentulus the augur, she who had so
discharged herself was under penalty as for unchastity, since indeed certain
women had carefully unlearned the dresses that are marks and guardians of
dignity, as hindrances to the practice of pandering. But now, pandering upon
themselves, that they may be approached more plainly, they have forsworn the
stola and the under-tunic and the little sandal and the hairpiece, and even
the litters and chairs themselves, in which they used to be kept at home and
in secret even in public. But one puts out his own lights, another kindles
what are not his. Look at the she-wolves, the markets of the people's lusts,
and the tribades themselves. If it is better to take the eyes away from such
shames of chastity slain in public, still look even from on high and you will
already see matrons. And when the priestess of the latrines fans silk, and
consoles with necklaces a neck more unclean than the place, and thrusts into
bracelets (which even matrons would have usurped rashly from the gifts of
brave men) hands privy to all shameful things, and draws over an impure leg a
clean shoe or a red one, why do you not look at these, or likewise those
dresses that pretend religion stands by their novelty? When on account of an
all-white cult and on account of the mark of the fillet and the privilege of
the cap they are initiated to Ceres; when on account of a contrary affection
of dark clothing and a grim fleece over the head they are driven into the
frenzy of Bellona; when the ambition of a broader purple and the throwing-on
of Galatian red commends Saturn? When this very pallium, more peevishly
arranged, and the sandals, in Greek fashion, fawn on Aesculapius, how much
more then would you accuse that dress and press it with your eyes, even if
already simple and unaffected, still guilty of superstition? But when it first
clothes that wisdom which refuses the emptiest superstitions, then most surely
the pallium is an august garment above all spoils and robes, and a priestly
elevation above all caps and priestly peaks. Cast your eyes down, I advise
you. Revere the dress, the renouncer, for now, of one error of yours.

Still, you say, like this from toga to pallium? Why, what if from a diadem and
from a scepter? Did Anacharsis change otherwise, when he put philosophy before
the kingship of Scythia? Though there be no signs of those who have crossed to
the better, this dress has something it does. First, even as to the simple
putting of it on, it costs no tedium. There is no need of an artisan to shape
the wrinkles from the start the day before, to lead them down more sleekly,
and to assign the whole fiction of the contracted boss to guardian forceps.
Then at dawn he first snatches the tunic in by a girdle (it would have been
better to have woven it more moderate), looks the boss over again, reshapes
whatever has gone out of line, sends a part forward from the left, but draws
back from the shoulders the circuit from which the fold is born, now that the
boards are already failing, shuts out the right, still heaps it on the left
with another like tabul(??)to devoted to the back, and so clothes the man with
a pack. I will finally ask your conscience what you first feel yourself in a
toga: clothed, or loaded? Do you have a garment, or haul one? If you deny it,
I will follow you home. I will see what you hurry to do at once from the
threshold. Truly the laying aside of no other garment is congratulated except
the toga's. We say nothing of the shoes, the toga's own torture, the filthiest
protection of the feet, and a false one too. For whom would it not be better
in cold and heat to be stiff barefoot than in a shoe to be foot-bound? A great
defense for walking the Venetian cobblers have provided: womanish boots. But
for the pallium nothing is more ready, even if double, which is Crates's.
Nowhere is a mere covering imposed in the dressing, for the whole labor of it
is to cover loosely. That is allowed from one wrapping around, nowhere
inhuman, I take it; so it covers all of a man at once. Veiling the shoulder,
it lays it out or tucks it in. Otherwise it clings to the shoulder, props
nothing around, binds nothing around, worries nothing over the reliability of
the folds, easily governs itself, easily repairs itself. Even when it is laid
off, it is consigned to no torture for tomorrow. If there is any undertunic
beneath, the torment of the girdle is idle. If any shoe is put on, it is the
cleanest work, or the feet more bare, certainly more manly than in shoes.

This for the pallium for now, as far as you have attended it by name. Now
truly it also challenges on the score of business. I, it says, owe nothing to
the forum, nothing to the Campus, nothing to the senate-house. I keep no watch
for office, I occupy no platform, I wait on no praetor's hall, I do not smell
the gutters, I do not adore the lattices, I do not bruise the benches, I do
not confuse the laws, I do not bark cases, I do not judge, I do not serve as a
soldier, I do not reign: I have withdrawn from the people. In me I have a
single business for myself. I care for nothing else except not to care. You
would enjoy a better life more in withdrawal than in public. But you will
brand it as idle. Of course one must live for the fatherland and the empire
and the state. That was once the opinion. No one is born for another who will
die for himself. Certainly when it has come to the Epicuruses and the Zenos,
you call wise the whole school of quiet, who have consecrated it under the
name of the highest and only pleasure. Still it will be almost allowed to me
too to be of use in public. I am accustomed from any ledge or altar to speak
medicines for morals, which will confer good health more happily on public
affairs and on cities and on empires than your labors. For if I go on to the
sharp points with you, togas have harmed the republic more than breastplates.
Yet I am given to no vices. I spare no old sores, no scurf. I apply the
cautery to ambition, by which Marcus Tullius bought a citrus-wood orb for five
hundred thousand sesterces, by which Asinius Gallus pays twice as much for a
table of the same Mauretania. Ah, at what fortunes they have valued wooden
stains! likewise by which Sulla works dishes of a hundred-pound weight! I
truly fear that that scale is small, when Drusillanus, a slave of Claudius at
that, builds a five-hundred-pound appetizer-dish, perhaps necessary to the
tables written above, for which, if a workshop was built, a dining-room too
was owed. I dip the scalpel equally into that cruelty by which Vedius Pollio
used to throw slaves to be attacked by lampreys. Delighted, of course, by a
new cruelty of a land beast, toothless and clawless and hornless, it pleased
him to compel wild beasts from fishes, and those to be cooked at once, so that
in their entrails he himself too might taste something of his slaves' bodies.
I will cut off the gullet by which Hortensius the orator was first able to
kill a peacock for the sake of food; by which Aufidius Lurco first spoiled
bodies by fattening and by forced foods carried flavor into an adulterated
state; by which Asinius Celer brought down the relish of a single mullet for
six thousand sesterces; by which Aesopus the actor put together a dish of a
hundred thousand from birds of the same costliness, as being all the singing
and talking ones; by which his son after such a tidbit was able to hunger for
something more sumptuous. For he drank down a pearl, costly even by the name
itself, I believe so that he might not have dined more poorly than his father.
I am silent on the Neros and Apiciuses and Rufuses. I will give a purge to the
impurity of Scaurus and the dice of Curius and the drunkenness of Antony. And
remember that these, for now, out of many, were toga-wearers; such men are not
easy among the pallium. Who will draw out and lance these purulences of the
city, if not pallium-clad speech?

By speech, it says, you have persuaded me with the wisest medicine. But even
if eloquence is quiet, withdrawn by inability to speak or held back by modesty
(for even speechless philosophy is content with life), the dress itself
sounds. So, in short, a philosopher is heard while he is seen. From my meeting
I put vices to the blush. Who, when he sees his rival, does not suffer? Who
can look with his eyes on him on whom he cannot look with his mind? It is a
great benefit of the pallium, under whose recollection even wicked morals
blush. Let philosophy now see what it profits, for it is not alone with me. I
have other arts too, useful in public. From mine are clothed both the first
shaper of letters and the first who unknots the voice and the first who writes

disgrace of gladiators is led out in the toga. This, no doubt, will be the
indignity: From toga to pallium. But those things the pallium speaks. But I
already confer on it also the fellowship of the divine sect and discipline.
Rejoice, pallium, and exult! A better philosophy has already deigned to honor
you, since you began to clothe a Christian.
