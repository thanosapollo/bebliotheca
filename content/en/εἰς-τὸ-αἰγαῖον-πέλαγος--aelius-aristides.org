No one yet has sung a sea through to the end, neither poet nor prose writer,
but Homer says "violet-hued"

sea and "wine-dark sea," and Euripides "purple brine," and if anyone else has
said anything else of the kind. For the most part they even revile the sea,
calling it salty and heavy-roaring and the like. We, however, will leave for
the present the speech about the whole sea, how great a use it has for men and
of what kind, and for how great things the god made it; but to the Aegean we
shall pay the debt and hymn it, first because it has obtained the best of
situations, as one would praise first the situation of a city as well. For it
is seated most of all in the middle of the whole inhabited world and of the
sea, leaving toward the north the Hellespont and Propontis and Pontus, and
toward the south the rest of the sea, dividing Asia from Europe where the two
first stand apart after the Hellespont; and having on either shore the most
renowned and most civilized races, Ionia and the Aeolian land on the one side,
Hellas on the other. So this alone of seas would be worthy to call the middle
of all Hellas, if one set this one race of Hellenes on either continent. And
yet if the banks of rivers are in good repute for having trees and meadows,
surely it is right that the shores of the Aegean be held in very high repute,
adorned with such races, and that among the Hellenes who dwell beside it
themselves. Since the most temperate air, and that which is most tolerable
alike toward every season, would be this which stands over the Aegean, and is
seated most of all about these lands. This is clear by both accounts. For
those who claim that Attica lies in the fairest part of the sky, and those
again who claim Ionia, both agree, I suppose, that the best is about this
place as a whole. For inasmuch as it stretches along both shores, whichever of
them one sets down as prevailing, it has that one; and if again, according to
the other disputes, they should have to yield to one another and come to the
middle, as I said, the Aegean is that. So it has obtained the fairest of airs.

Such, then, is its situation, and in such a part of the whole. But its nature
is marvelous. For the size of the sea is among the first in repute and
stretched in every direction, if one should examine it exactly from beginning
to end everywhere, and for one sailing around it in a circle an untold number
of days will be spent. Yet it has not been stripped bare, nor does it, casting
the eye upon the boundless, bring dismay and helplessness; but there are many
seas of the Aegean, many gulfs, and another form of sea in another place. You
could even break off the voyage in the middle of the sea and meet with land
and cities and places like certain small continents washed round, and you
yourself could become steward of the voyage, and before you had crossed the
whole sea you could be carried back again, having equally accomplished your
aim. For it is not as with Homer's sea, that one must pass through a waste and
arrive at some inhabited place, so that the way is pleasing to none of the
gods because of the desolation; but here indeed even /the choruses of Nereids
unwind the fairest trace with the foot;/ because this is the most inhabited
and flourishing part of the sea, and there is as much to sail as one wishes,
and again as much to travel on foot as one does not wish to sail around, but
having gone through, putting in at another shore, to set out from there. For
this alone of seas is inhabited no less than the land itself; and it has
cities as far apart from one another as the lands between would also separate
the cities on the mainland. So through the sharpness of the sea and the
abundance of the whole voyage it is most formidable, yet through the respites
it is most gentle. And moving most swiftly it also most of all preserves the
divine, keeping it safe. For it must be awesome through strength, yet saving
through gentleness. And of seas it sounds most clearly about its own gifts and
offspring; just as the poets say the Pans and Satyrs sound on the mountains
and about the trees in the season

of the year, taking their pleasure, inasmuch as it has the most musical of the
gods as its own citizens and inhabitants, Apollo and Artemis, whom Zeus,
father of all, brought forth in this as the fairest part of the inhabited
world, that they might be perfect in music, and he led Leto through three
other islands into Delos, the middle one in the middle of the Aegean, making
Athena her guide of the way. And the natural musicality of the sea is clear
even to everyone, which from the very beginning sent up as it were another
chorus, that of the islands, which, having divided the sea, many leaning
toward one another, appear to sailors and passengers a spectacle more sacred
than any dithyrambic circle, a consolation in dangers and, when the voyage
goes as one would wish, a marvelous enchantment of the soul. For all are both
outside one another and in the middle, as when in the season of summer and
before the west wind comes down many boats ride at anchor for the hunt of
fish, and for one who has passed inside, both what is from the stern and what
is from the prow and either side all alike ends in an island, so that at first
one is at a loss which way one must even go through. And what the boats that
come to aid do, this these islands do for those storm-tossed: they meet them
as stretching out a hand and calling them to themselves. Such are the Aegean's
works of art, mixing the land together with the sea, as the spotted deer and
the skins of leopards have their colors and circles mixed, with which all the
sea here is shone round with brightness, while meadows on continents, full of
flowers, fall short, and whatever else one might think of this season and
these variegations; reasonably so. For all that belongs to the mainland is
here too in the lands in the middle, but the beauty of this is not present to
those. And as heaven is adorned with the stars, so also the sea of the Aegean
is adorned with the islands; so that even one who has no need of a voyage
would reasonably sail for this very thing, to be carried through the Aegean

to be conveyed; so this is the fairest both of seas and of all things on land.
So that the peculiar mark of beauty is also a token of the Aegean, and it
begins from the point, so to speak, where the islands begin for one crossing
the bare sea. And the Aegean alone it is not even possible to call
"unharvested," for it is not bare nor barren, but exceptionally rich in wine,
fertile also in grain and in all that the seasons bring forth, and rich in
fish and in game, of the sort Homer said the sea of the blessed must be,
having every use and every pleasure and spectacles, full of harbors, full of
sanctuaries, full of pipes and paeans and springs and rivers, being the nurse
of Dionysus, and equally dear to the Dioscuri and the Nymphs; furnishing happy
lives from every kind, profitable to its inhabitants and to merchants, and
saving to those to whom it is kindly. And as a fair body has fitting
beginnings and ends, so this sea alone, it seems, has both beginning and end
that is pleasing. For it begins from the first rank of the islands toward the
south, and ends at the Hellespontine strait, which, flowing around, it makes
the Chersonese worth seeing. So that, as the saying goes, the beauty extends
from head to foot. And of its outlets and gulfs it is not possible to say
either how many they are in number or of what kind in beauty. And though they
fear the Aegean sea most of all, they also most of all long to cross it a
second time, since men also cross this one for the sweetest reasons. Contests
and mysteries and the fair things of Hellas fill this expedition, and of the
lovers of beauty and the noble from everywhere this is the gatherer, and the
filler of the sweetest spectacles, serving the fairest things to the most
conspicuous of the gods. Let these things be sung to you from us, O dear
savior Aegean, in our music. And you, taking pleasure, save always both
yourself and those who sail with you.
