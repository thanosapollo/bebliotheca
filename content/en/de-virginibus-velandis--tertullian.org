* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Having already gone through the trouble that belongs to my own view, I will
show in Latin as well that our virgins ought to be veiled from the time they
have crossed the threshold of their years. Truth demands this, and no one can
prescribe against truth: not the stretch of times, not the patronage of
persons, not the privilege of regions. For from these, as a rule, custom takes
its start in some ignorance or simplicity, is hardened by succession into use,
and is then claimed against the truth. But our Lord Christ named himself
Truth, not Custom. If Christ is always, and before all things, truth is
equally everlasting and ancient. Let those look to themselves, then, to whom
what is old in itself is new. Heresies are refuted not so much by novelty as
by truth. Whatever tastes of opposition to truth will be heresy, even an
ancient custom. For the rest, a man is ignorant of a thing by his own fault.
And what is unknown ought to have been sought out as carefully as what is
recognized ought to be received.

The rule of faith is one altogether, alone unmoved and unreformable: that we
believe in one God almighty, maker of the world, and in his Son Jesus Christ,
born of the virgin Mary, crucified under Pontius Pilate, raised from the dead
on the third day, received into the heavens, sitting now at the Father's right
hand, coming to judge the living and the dead through the resurrection of the
flesh as well. While this law of faith remains, the rest of discipline and of
the way of life already admit the novelty of correction, as the grace of God
works and advances even to the end. For what sort of thing would it be, that
while the devil is always at work and adding daily to the devices of iniquity,
the work of God should either have stopped or have given up advancing? It was
for this that the Lord sent the Paraclete: because human smallness could not
take in everything at once, discipline should be guided little by little, set
in order, and led to its fullness by that vicar of the Lord, the Holy Spirit.
I have yet many things to say to you, he says, but you cannot bear them yet.
When that Spirit of truth has come, he will lead you into all truth, and will
announce to you the things that are coming. And earlier too he had spoken of
this work of his. What then is the Paraclete's administration, if not this:
that discipline is guided, that the scriptures are opened, that understanding
is recast, that there is progress toward what is better? Nothing is without
its season; all things wait for their time. So Ecclesiastes says, There is a
time for every matter. Look at creation itself, how it is brought on little by
little toward fruit. First it is a grain, and from the grain a shoot rises,
and from the shoot a little tree is forced out. Then branches and leaves
gather strength, and the whole that we call a tree is spread. Then the bud
swells, and from the bud the flower is loosed, and from the flower the fruit
is opened. That fruit too, crude for a while and shapeless, little by little
steering the course of its years, is trained into the mildness of its taste.

So too righteousness (for the same God is God of righteousness and of
creation) was first in its first steps, fearing God by nature. Then through
the Law and the prophets it moved on into infancy. Then through the gospel it
boiled up into youth. Now through the Paraclete it is being composed into
maturity. He will be, after Christ, the only master both to be named and to be
revered. For he does not speak from himself, but what is commanded by Christ.
He alone is the one who goes before us, because he alone comes after Christ.
Those who have received him put truth before custom. Those who have heard him
prophesying even now, not long ago, cover their virgins.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But I do not want, for the moment, to count this practice as truth's. Let it
be custom for a while, so that I may set custom against custom. Throughout
Greece and some of its barbarian lands, more churches hide their virgins. And
under this sky too the practice is found somewhere, so that no one may ascribe
that custom to Greek or barbarian heathenism. But I have put forward those
churches which the apostles themselves or apostolic men founded, and, I think,
before certain others. They therefore have the same authority of custom; they
set times and predecessors against us more than these later churches do. What
shall we observe? What shall we choose? We cannot spit out a custom that we
cannot condemn, since it is not foreign, because it is not of foreigners, with
whom we share the right of peace and the name of brotherhood. We and they have
one faith, one God, the same Christ, the same hope, the same sacraments of the
bath; let me say it once, we are one church. So whatever is our people's is
ours. Otherwise you divide the body.

Still, here, as is usual in all variously instituted, doubtful, and uncertain
matters, an examination should have been applied: which of two such different
customs better suited the discipline of God. And of course that one should be
chosen which shuts virgins in, known to God alone, who, besides that glory is
to be sought from God and not from men, ought to blush even at their own good.
You put a virgin to confusion more by praising her than by blaming her;
because the brow of sin is harder, having learned shamelessness from the sin
itself and in it. For that custom which belies virgins while it displays them,
no one would have approved except some such as the virgins themselves are. For
such eyes will want a virgin seen as the virgin has who wants to be seen. The
same kinds of eyes desire each other. It is of the same lust to be seen and to
see. It is as much the mark of a holy man to blush if he has seen a virgin as
of a holy virgin if she has been seen by a man.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But those most holy predecessors did not even choose to look between the
customs. Still, among us until very lately both customs were shared more
tolerably. The matter had been left to choice, so that each might cover
herself or put herself on show as she pleased, just as with marrying, which
itself is neither forced nor forbidden. Truth had been content to strike a
bargain with custom, so that under the name of custom it might enjoy itself
even in part. But when discernment had begun to advance, so that through the
license of either practice the mark of the better side was coming out, at once
that adversary of good things, and much more of good institutions, did his own
work. The virgins of men go about against the virgins of God, with a brow
quite bare, stirred into a rash boldness; and they look like virgins who can
ask something of husbands, not to say such a deed as this: that their rivals,
all the freer for being handmaids of Christ alone, may be given over to them.

We are scandalized, they say, because others walk otherwise than we do; and
they would rather be scandalized than be provoked to modesty. A scandal, if I
am not mistaken, is an example not of a good thing but of a bad, building up
toward sin. Good things scandalize no one except an evil mind. If modesty,
bashfulness, a scorn of glory that seeks to please God alone, are good, let
those who are scandalized at such a good recognize their own evil. For what if
the incontinent also say they are scandalized by the continent? Is continence
to be called back? And for fear that those with many marriages be scandalized,
is marriage to one to be refused? Why should these not rather complain that
the wantonness, the shamelessness of showy virginity, is a scandal to them?

For the sake of such market wares, then, are holy virgins to be dragged into
the church, blushing that they are recognized in the midst, trembling that
they are uncovered, summoned as if to a rape? For they are no less unwilling
to suffer even this. Every public exposure of an honorable virgin is a
suffering of rape; and yet to suffer force upon the flesh is the lesser thing,
because it comes of nature's office. But when the spirit itself is violated in
a virgin by the taking away of her veil, she has learned to lose what she used
to keep. O sacrilegious hands, that could drag off a dress dedicated to God!
What worse would any persecutor have done, if he had known that this had been
the virgin's choice? You have stripped a girl from the head, and already she
is not wholly a virgin to herself; she has been made another.

Rise, then, Truth; rise, and as if from patience break out! I do not want you
to defend any custom; for already even that custom under which you were
enjoying yourself is being stormed. Show yourself to be the one who covers
virgins. Interpret your own scriptures, which custom has not known. For if it
had known them, it would never have been.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But inasmuch as it is also custom to argue from the scriptures against the
truth, it is at once thrown against us that the apostle made no mention of
virgins where he laid down the rule about the veil, but named women only;
whereas, if he had wanted virgins too to be covered, he would have spoken of
virgins as well when he named women: just as, they say, in the place where he
treats of marriage he also declares what is to be observed concerning virgins.
And so they are not contained in the law of veiling the head, as not named in
this law; rather they are unveiled from this, because those who are not even
named are not commanded.

But we turn the same argument back. For he who knew elsewhere how to mention
both kinds, virgin I mean and woman, that is not-virgin, for the sake of the
distinction, in those places where he does not name the virgin, by making no
distinction, shows a sharing of condition. Otherwise he could have set a
difference here too between virgin and woman, just as elsewhere he says, The
woman and the virgin are divided. Therefore those whom he did not divide by
keeping silence he has joined into one. Nor yet, because there the woman and
the virgin are divided, will that division stand as a patron here as well, as
some would have it. For how many things said elsewhere have no force where
they are not said, unless the case is the same as elsewhere, so that it is
enough to have said it once? But that case of virgin and woman is far divided
from this kind. The woman and the virgin are divided, he says. Why? Because
the unmarried, that is the virgin, thinks of the things of the Lord, that she
may be holy in body and in spirit; but the married, that is the not-virgin,
thinks how she may please her husband. That will be the meaning of that
division, which has no place in this chapter, in which nothing is laid down
about marriage, or about the mind and thought of woman and virgin, but about
veiling the head.

Of this veiling the Holy Spirit, wanting no dispute, wanted the virgin too to
be understood under the one name of woman; whom, by not naming her in her own
right, he did not separate from the woman, and by not separating he joined her
to the woman from whom he did not separate her. Is it a novelty now, then, to
use the primary word and still understand the rest in that word, where there
is no need to distinguish the whole piece by piece? By nature a compact style
of speech is both pleasing and necessary, since a trailing speech is both
heavy and empty. So too we are content with general words, which take in
themselves the sense of the special ones.

So now of the word itself. The natural name is female. Of the natural name the
general is woman. Of the general the special is virgin, or married, or widow,
or however many names of years are added besides. The special is therefore
subject to the general, because the general is first; and the later to the
earlier, and the part to the whole. It is understood in that to which it is
subject, and it is signified in it, because it is contained in it. So neither
hand nor foot nor any of the members needs to be signified when the body is
named. And if you have said the world, there will be both heaven and what is
in it, sun and moon and constellations and stars, and earth and seas and the
whole count of the elements. You have named all when you have named that which
is made of all. So too by naming woman he has named whatever is woman's.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But since they use the name of woman in such a way as to think it fits no one
except her who has known a man, we must prove that the proper force of this
word belongs to the sex itself, not to a grade of the sex, so that virgins too
are commonly counted under it.

When this kind of second human being was made by God as a help for man, that
female is at once named woman, still happy, still worthy of paradise, still a
virgin. She shall be called woman, he says. You have therefore a name, I do
not say already common to a virgin, but proper to her, which from the
beginning a virgin received. But some will have it said, cleverly, of the
future: She shall be called woman, as if she were going to be this when she
had unsealed her virginity, since he also added, For this cause a man shall
leave father and mother and be joined to his woman, and they shall be two in
one flesh. Let them show us first, then, where that subtlety lives: if she was
named woman with a view to the future, what name she received in the meantime.
For she cannot have been without a name of her present quality. What sort of
thing is it, that she who for the future was to be called by a name already
marked out should in the present be named nothing? Adam set names on all the
animals, and on none from a future condition, but from the present
appointment, which any later condition would serve. Each was called what from
the beginning it was meant to be. What then was she called then? Yet as often
as she is named in scripture she is called woman before she was married, and
never virgin while she was a virgin. This name was then her only one, and that
when nothing had been said in a prophetic way.

For when scripture reports that the two were naked, Adam and his woman, this
too does not taste of the future, as if it had said his woman in a forecast of
a wife, but because even unmarried she was his woman, as being from his own
substance. This, he says, is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall
be called woman. From here, then, by a silent awareness of nature, the very
divinity of the soul has brought into the use of speech, without men knowing
it, as it has many other things which we shall be able to show elsewhere as
commonly done and said from scripture, that we call our women our wives,
though we also speak some things improperly. For the Greeks too, who use the
name of woman more for a wife, have other names proper to a wife. But I prefer
to count this use as a witness of scripture. For when two are made into one
flesh through the bond of marriage, flesh of flesh and bone of bones is called
the woman of him from whose substance she begins to be counted, having been
made a wife. So woman is not by nature the name of a wife, but wife by
condition is a name of a woman. In short, a woman can be spoken of who is not
a wife; but a wife who is not a woman cannot be spoken of, because she cannot
even be.

So when the name of the new female had been settled, which is woman, and what
she had been first had been set out, that is, when the name had been assigned,
he then turned to the prophetic reason, so as to say, For this cause a man
shall leave father and mother. The name is so far separate from the prophecy
as it is from the person herself, so that he has not said this of Eve herself,
but with a view to those future females whom he named in the mother-source of
the female kind. Otherwise Adam was not going to leave father and mother, whom
he did not have, for Eve. Therefore what was said prophetically does not
belong to Eve, because it does not belong to Adam either. For it was foretold
of the condition of husbands, who were going to leave their parents for a
woman, which could not fall on Eve, because it could not fall on Adam. If that
is how it is, it is plain she was not named woman on account of a future that
did not belong to her.

To this is added that he himself gave the reason of the name. For when he had
said, She shall be called woman, Because she was taken out of her man, he
says, and he himself still a virgin. But we shall speak of the name of man in
its own place. Let no one, then, take as prophecy a name that was drawn from
another meaning, especially since it is plain where she did receive a name
from the future: there, namely, where she is named Eve, now with a personal
name, because the natural one had gone before. For if Eve is the mother of the
living, see, she is named from the future; see, she is announced beforehand as
a wife and not a virgin. This will be the name of one about to marry; for from
a married woman comes a mother. So here too it is shown that she was not then
named woman from the future, who was afterward going to receive the name of
her future condition. Enough has been answered to this part.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Let us see now whether the apostle too keeps the form of this name according
to Genesis, counting it to the sex, calling the virgin Mary a woman just as
Genesis does Eve. For writing to the Galatians he says, God sent his Son, made
of a woman, who is certainly agreed to have been a virgin, though Ebion
resists. I recognize too that the angel Gabriel was sent to a virgin. But when
he blesses her, he ranks her among women, not among virgins: Blessed are you
among women. The angel too knew that a virgin is also called a woman.

But to these two points someone has seemed to himself to have answered
cleverly: that because Mary was betrothed, she was therefore pronounced a
woman both by the angel and by the apostle. For a betrothed woman is in a
manner married. Yet between in a manner and in truth there is difference
enough, at least in this place. For elsewhere, to be sure, it is to be taken
so. But now they did not pronounce Mary a woman as already married, but as
none the less a female, even if she had not been betrothed, as having been
called this from the beginning. For that from which the form descends must of
necessity prejudge. Otherwise, as far as this chapter goes, if she is here set
on a level with a betrothed woman, so that Mary was called woman not as a
female but as married, then Christ was not born of a virgin, because he was
born of a betrothed woman, who by that name will have ceased to be a virgin.
But if he was born of a virgin, though also betrothed, yet intact, acknowledge
that a virgin too, even an intact one, is called a woman. Here at least
nothing can seem to have been said prophetically, as if the apostle had named
a future woman, that is a married one, by saying made of a woman. For he could
not name a later woman, from whom Christ was not to be born, that is one who
had known a man, but that woman who was then present, who was a virgin and was
called woman after the proper force of this name, defended according to the
first form for a virgin and so for the whole kind of women.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Let us turn now to a review of the reasons themselves by which the apostle
teaches that a female ought to be veiled, whether the same reasons also fit
virgins, so that from this too a sharing of the name between virgins and
not-virgins may stand, while the same causes of the veil are found on both
sides.

If the man is the head of the woman, he is surely also of the virgin, from
whom comes that woman who has married, unless a virgin is some monstrous third
kind with a head of her own. If it is a shame for a woman to be shaved or
shorn, it is surely so for a virgin too. So let the world, God's rival, look
to it, if it pretends that cropped hair is a grace to a virgin, just as it is
allowed to a boy. Therefore she to whom it is equally unfitting to be shaved
or shorn is equally bound to be covered. If the woman is the glory of the man,
how much more the virgin, who is a glory to herself as well? If the woman is
from the man and for the man, that rib of Adam was first a virgin. If a woman
ought to have power upon her head, a virgin ought to have it all the more
justly, to whom belongs what is at issue in the cause.

For if it is on account of the angels, those, that is, whom we read of as
having fallen from God and from heaven through desire of females, who can
presume that such angels longed for bodies already stained and the leftovers
of human lust, so as not rather to have burned for virgins, whose bloom even
excuses human lust? For scripture too puts it so: And it came to pass, it
says, when men had begun to grow many upon the earth, and daughters were born
to them; and the sons of God, seeing the daughters of men, that they were
fair, took to themselves wives of all whom they chose. Here the Greek name of
women tastes of wives, because marriage is in view. When then it says
daughters of men, it plainly points to virgins, who would still be counted
with their parents (for married women are called their husbands'), though it
could have said wives of men; likewise not naming the angels adulterers but
husbands, while they take unmarried daughters of men, whom it had said above
were born, and so too signifying virgins. Born above, but here married to
angels. I do not know that they were anything else than born and then married.

So perilous a face, then, ought to be shaded, which has thrown
stumbling-blocks even as far as heaven, so that standing before God, to whom
it is guilty of the angels driven out, it may blush before the other angels
too, and may check that old bad liberty of its head, which is now not even to
be offered to the eyes of men. But even if those angels had sought women
already stained, so much the more ought virgins to have been veiled on account
of the angels, as so much the more the angels could have sinned on account of
virgins. If, moreover, the apostle also adds the prejudgment of nature, that a
woman's overflowing hair is an honor, because the hair serves as a covering,
this is surely a mark above all for a virgin, whose very adornment properly is
this: that, piled on the crown, it covers the very citadel of the head with a
circuit of hair.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

The contraries of all these, at any rate, bring it about that a man is not to
veil his head: namely because he has not by nature obtained a show of hair;
because it is no shame to him to be shaved or shorn; because the angels did
not go off course on his account; because he is the glory and image of God;
because his head is Christ. So when the apostle treats of man and woman, why
she ought to be veiled and he not, it is plain why he also kept silence about
the virgin: allowing, of course, the virgin to be understood in the woman by
the same reason by which he did not name the boy as to be counted in the man,
taking in the whole order of each sex under the names proper to each, woman
and man. So too Adam, still intact, is named man in Genesis: She shall be
called woman, he says, because she was taken from her man. Thus Adam was a man
before the meeting of marriage, just as Eve was a woman.

On both sides the apostle has spoken enough for the whole kind of each sex,
and briefly and fully, with so well-set a definition: Every woman, he says.
What is every, if not of every kind, every order, every condition, every
dignity, every time of life? since every is the whole and entire and lacking
in no part of itself. But a virgin is also a part of woman. Equally of the man
not to be veiled, Every, he says. See two different names, man and woman.
Every, each of them. Two laws bound over against each other, here of veiling,
there of baring. Therefore if, because it is said every man, the name of man
is common even to him who is not yet a man, an unbearded male, and if, the
name being common according to nature, the law of not veiling him who among
men is a virgin is common too according to discipline, why should it not be
prejudged in the same way that, woman being named, every virgin-woman is
contained in the fellowship of the name, so as to be contained also in the
sharing of the law? If a virgin is not a woman, neither is an unbearded youth
a man. If a virgin is not covered because she is not a woman, let the
unbearded youth be covered because he is not a man. Let there be equal leave
for the same virginity. As virgins are not forced to be veiled, so let boys
not be ordered to be unveiled.

Why do we partly acknowledge the apostle's definition as complete about every
man, and do not ask why he did not name the boy as well, but partly go aside
from it, though it is equally complete about every woman? If anyone, he says,
is contentious, we have no such custom, nor the church of God. He shows that
there had been some contention about this kind, to put out which he used the
whole shortness of speech: not naming the virgin, so as to show that there was
not to be doubt about her being veiled, and naming every woman, when he could
have named the virgin. So the Corinthians themselves understood him. To this
day, in fact, the Corinthians veil their virgins. What the apostles taught,
those who learned it approve.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Let us see now whether, as we have shown that the arguments from nature and
from the cause also fit a virgin, so too the rules of church discipline about
a woman look toward a virgin. A woman is not permitted to speak in the church,
nor to teach, nor to baptize, nor to offer, nor to claim for herself a share
in any manly function, still less in a priestly office. Let us ask whether any
of these is lawful to a virgin. If it is not lawful to a virgin, but she is
put under the same condition in all things, and is counted with the woman in
the necessity of humility, from where will this one thing be lawful to her
which is not lawful to every female? What prerogative does she earn against
her own condition, if she is a virgin and has set herself to hallow her flesh?
Is she therefore excused from the veil so that she may enter the church
notable and marked, so that she may show the honor of holiness in the liberty
of her head? She could have been more worthily honored by some prerogative of
manly rank or office.

I know well that somewhere a virgin not yet twenty years old has been set in
the order of widows. If the bishop owed her any relief, he could surely have
given it in some other way with the respect of discipline kept safe, so that
such a miracle, not to say a monster, should not now be pointed at in the
church: a virgin widow, all the more portentous because she does not even
cover her head as a widow, denying herself both ways, as a virgin who is
counted a widow, and as a widow who is called a virgin. But she sits there
uncovered by the same authority by which she sits there as a virgin, a seat to
which, besides sixty years, not only women of one husband, that is who have
been married, are at last chosen, but mothers too, and indeed those who have
brought children up, so that, built by the trials of all affections, they may
both readily know how to help the rest with counsel and comfort, and may none
the less have run through those things by which a female can be proved. So
true is it that nothing is permitted a virgin for honor from her place.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

So neither on the ground of any distinctions. For the rest, it is inhuman
enough if women, subject in all things to men, wear in front an honor-bearing
mark of their virginity, by which they are looked up to and stared at and
magnified by the brothers, while men, so many virgins, so many voluntary
eunuchs, walk in a good that stays hidden, wearing nothing that would make
them too illustrious. For they too will have to claim some badges for
themselves: either the feathers of the Garamantes, or the fillets of the
barbarians, or the cicadas of the Athenians, or the curls of the Germans, or
the tattoo-marks of the Britons; or let the opposite be done, and let them
lurk in the church with heads veiled.

We are sure the Holy Spirit could rather have granted some such thing to
males, if he had granted it to females, since besides the authority of sex it
would have been right that males be honored even on the ground of continence
itself. The more their sex is greedy and hot toward females, the more
laborious is the continence of the greater heat, and so the worthier of every
showing, if a showing of virginity is a dignity. For is not continence also
above virginity, whether of widowers, or of those who by consent have already
refused the common disgrace? For virginity stands by grace, but continence by
virtue. Not to desire what you have grown into desiring is a great fight. But
of a desire whose fruit you have never known, you will easily not desire,
having no adversary in the desire of the fruit. How then would God not rather
have granted some such thing to men in honor, either as to a nearer image of
himself, or as to one who toils more? But if nothing to the male, much more
nothing to the female.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But what we left aside above for the sake of the discussion that followed, so
as not to scatter its coherence, we will now clear by an answer. For when we
had taken our stand on the apostle's complete definition, that every woman is
to be understood of every time of life as well, it was ready to be answered
from the other side that therefore a virgin ought to be covered from birth and
from the first name of her years. It is not so, however, but from the time she
has begun to understand herself and to enter the sense of her own nature and
to go out from a child's virginity and to undergo that new thing which belongs
to another time of life. For the first of the race, Adam and Eve, so long as
they lacked understanding, went naked. But when they had tasted of the tree of
knowledge, the first thing they felt was that they must blush. So each marked
the understanding of their own sex by a covering.

But even if she is to be veiled on account of the angels, without doubt the
law of the veil will work from that time of life from which the daughters of
men could draw desire of themselves and undergo marriage. For a virgin ceases
to be one from the time she can not be one. And therefore among Israel it is
unlawful to hand one over to a man except after maturity has been attested by
blood; so before this sign the thing is unripe. Therefore if she is a virgin
so long as she is unripe, she ceases to be a virgin when she is recognized as
mature, and as not a virgin she is now applied to the law, just as she is to
marriage.

And the betrothed indeed have the example of Rebecca, who, when she was being
led, herself still unknown, to a betrothed still unknown, as soon as she knew
that he was the man she had seen from far off, did not wait for the struggle
of the right hand, or the meeting of a kiss, or the sharing of a greeting,
but, confessing what she had felt, that is that she was married in spirit,
denied she was a virgin by being veiled on the spot. O woman already of
Christ's discipline! For she showed that marriage too is done by look and
mind, as rape is; except that some still veil even a Rebecca. As for the rest,
that is those who are not betrothed, let the delay of parents, coming from
straitness or from scruple, look to itself; let the vow of continence itself
look to itself. It has nothing to do with years that are already running their
own stretches and paying their own dues to maturity. Another mother in secret,
nature, and another father in hiding, time, have married their daughter to
their own laws.

Look at that virgin of yours already married, her soul by waiting and her
flesh by change of form, for whom you are preparing a second husband. Already
her voice has grown out of its first use, and her limbs are complete, and
shame is clothed everywhere, and the months pay their tribute; and do you deny
she is a woman whom you say suffers a woman's things? If a man's joining makes
a woman, let them not be covered except after the very passion of marriage.
And yet even among the heathen they are led veiled to the husband. But if they
are veiled at betrothal, because they have been mixed with a male in body and
in spirit through the kiss and the right hands, through which they first
unsealed their modesty in spirit, through the common pledge of conscience by
which they contracted the whole confusion, how much more will time veil them,
without which they cannot be betrothed, and under whose urgency they cease to
be virgins even without betrothal? Even the heathen observe time, so that by
the law of nature they may render their rights to the years. For they send
females to business from twelve years, but a male from two more, judging
puberty by years, not by betrothal or marriage. She is called mother of a
household, though a virgin, and he father of a household, though unbearded.
Among us not even natural things are observed, as if the God of nature were
other than ours.

Recognize the woman too, recognize the married woman too, from the witnesses
of body and of spirit, which she undergoes both in conscience and in flesh.
These are the earlier tablets of natural betrothal and marriage. Set a veil
outside on her who has a covering inside. Let the upper parts too be covered
of her whose lower parts are not bare. Do you want to know what authority
years have? Set both before you, one packed early into a woman's dress, and
one who, advanced in maturity, lasts in virginity with her own dress: the
former will more easily be denied to be a woman than the latter believed a
virgin. So great is the witness of years that it cannot even be blocked by
dress.

What of this, that these of ours even by dress confess the change of years,
and as soon as they have understood themselves to be women they are led out
from among the virgins, laying aside from the head itself what they were: they
turn the hair and fix the lock with a more wanton pin, and with the hair
parted from the front they have openly professed womanhood. They seek beauty
from the mirror, and they soak a more fastidious face with washing, perhaps
they even touch it up with some drug, they toss the mantle about them on the
outside, they pack on a shoe of many shapes, they carry more gear down to the
baths. Why should I go after each thing? Their open preparations alone carry
about a whole womanhood, yet they want to play the virgin by the bareness of
the head alone, denying by one dress what they profess by the whole array.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

If they misuse dress on account of men, let them fill it out in this as well,
and as they veil the head among the heathen, let them at least in the church
hide their virginity, which they conceal outside the church. They fear
strangers: let them revere the brothers too. Or let them dare with a steady
face to be seen as virgins in the streets as well, as they dare in the
churches. I will praise their vigor if they have also marketed some of their
virginity among the heathen. The same nature outside as inside, the same
institution among men and before the Lord, stands in the same liberty. To what
end, then, do they thrust their good out of sight abroad, but publish it in
the church? I ask for a reason. Is it to please their brothers, or to please
God himself? If to please God himself, he is as fit to see whatever is done in
secret as he is just to repay what is done for him alone. In short he commands
that we trumpet none of those things that will earn a reward with him, and
that we not take them back from men as pay.

But if we are forbidden to do even the work of one small coin, or of any alms,
with the left hand knowing, how much darkness ought we to pour round ourselves
when we offer God so great an offering of our very body and our very spirit,
when we consecrate to him nature itself? Therefore what cannot be seen to be
done for God, because God does not will it to be done so, follows as done for
the favor of men, unlawful first of all, as lustful of glory. For glory is
unlawful to those whose proving consists in every humiliation. And if the
virtue of continence is conferred by God, why do you boast as if you had not
received it? If in fact you have not received it, what do you have that has
not been given you? And by this very thing it is plain it has not been given
you by God, that you do not offer it to God alone. Let us see, then, whether
what is human is firm and true.

They report a saying once spoken by someone when this question was first
tried: And how shall we draw the others to such a work? They will make us
happy, of course, if they are more, and not the grace of God or the merits of
each. Do virgins adorn the church for God, or does the church adorn, or
commend, virgins? He has therefore confessed that glory is at the root.
Further, where glory is, there is solicitation; where solicitation, there
compulsion; where compulsion, there necessity; where necessity, there
weakness. Deservedly, then, while they do not cover the head, so that they may
be solicited for glory's sake, they are forced to cover their bellies by the
ruin of weakness. For rivalry brings them out, not religion. Sometimes their
belly itself is their god, because the brotherhood readily takes virgins up.
And they not only fall, but they also draw after themselves a long rope of
sins.

For, brought forth into the midst and lifted up by their good being made
public, and heaped by the brothers with every honor and work of charity, so
long as they do not slip, when something has been admitted they think of as
much disgrace as they had honor. If an uncovered head is ascribed to
virginity, if any virgin has fallen from the grace of virginity, she remains
with head uncovered so as not to be betrayed, and then already walks in a
dress that is another's. Conscious of a womanhood now undoubted, they dare to
come to God with head bare. But a jealous God and Lord, who has said, Nothing
hidden that shall not be revealed, brings a good many even into public view.
For they will not confess unless they are betrayed by the cries of their own
infants. And in so far as they are more, will you not also hold them suspected
of more crimes?

I will say it, though I would rather not: it is a hard thing for a woman to be
made once for all who fears to be made, and who, already made, can still lie
that she is a virgin under God. What will she dare, again, about her own womb,
so that she may not be uncovered as a mother too? God knows how many infants
he has already helped both to be finished and to be brought through to birth
whole, after they had been fought against for some time by their mothers. Such
virgins always conceive most easily and give birth most happily, and children
indeed most like their fathers!

These outrages a forced and unwilling virginity admits. The very desire of not
hiding is not modest. It undergoes something that is not a virgin's: a zeal to
please, and men at that. Let her try with as good a mind as you like; she must
be put in peril by the publishing of herself, while she is struck by many
uncertain eyes, while she is tickled by the fingers of those who point, while
she is loved too much, while she grows warm among constant embraces and
kisses. So the brow is hardened, so shame is worn away, so it is loosed, so it
is learned already to want to please in another way.

:END:

But true and whole and pure virginity fears nothing more than itself. It does
not even want to suffer the eyes of women. It has other eyes of its own. It
flees to the veil of the head as to a helmet, as to a shield, to protect its
good against the blows of temptations, against the darts of scandals, against
suspicions and whispers and rivalry, even envy itself. For there is something
to be feared even among the heathen, which they call the evil eye, the more
unhappy outcome of praise and of too huge a glory. This we sometimes take as
the devil, for hatred of the good is his; sometimes we count it to God, for
the judgment of pride is his, who lifts the humble and brings down the lifted
up. The holier virgin will therefore fear, even under the name of the evil
eye, the adversary on this side and God on that, the envious temper of the
one, the censor's light of the other, and will rejoice to be known to herself
alone and to God. But even if she has become known to someone, she is wise if
she has blocked the step against temptations. For who will dare to press with
his eyes a closed face, a face that does not feel, a face, I might say,
severe? Any bad thought at all will be broken by that very sternness. She who
hides the virgin already denies even the woman.

In these things stands the defense of our view according to scripture,
according to nature, according to discipline. Scripture founds the law, nature
bears witness, discipline exacts it. Which of these does a custom of opinion
serve, or what color has the opposite view? God's is scripture, God's is
nature, God's is discipline. Whatever is contrary to these is not God's. If
scripture is uncertain, nature is plain, and from its witness scripture cannot
be uncertain. If nature is doubted, discipline shows what is more ratified by
God. Nothing is dearer to him than humility, nothing more acceptable than
modesty, nothing more burdensome than glory and the zeal to please men. Let
that, then, be scripture and nature and discipline to you which you find
ratified by God, as you are commanded to examine all things and to follow
whatever is better.

It remains also that we turn to them themselves, so that they may take these
things more willingly. I ask you, whether mother, or sister, or virgin
daughter, let me speak by the names of years, veil your head: if a mother, for
your sons; if a sister, for your brothers; if a daughter, for your fathers.
All times of life are in peril in you. Put on the armor of modesty, draw round
the rampart of bashfulness, build a wall for your sex, which shall neither
send your own eyes out nor let others' in. Fill up the dress of a woman, that
you may keep the standing of a virgin. Feign something of what is within, that
you may show the truth to God alone. And yet you do not feign a married woman.
For you have married Christ: you have handed your flesh over to him, you have
betrothed your maturity to him. Walk according to the will of your bridegroom.
It is Christ who commands even other men's betrothed and married women to be
veiled, and so much the more his own.

:END:

But we warn you too, women of the other chastity, who have fallen into
marriage, not to grow so far out of the discipline of the veil, not even in a
moment of an hour, that, because you cannot throw it off, you destroy it in
another way, walking neither covered nor bare. For some, with turbans and
woolen bands, do not veil the head but bind it, protected indeed in front, but
bare where the head properly is. Others are covered moderately with little
linens, I suppose so as not to press the head, not even let down as far as the
ears, and are covered only as far as the brain. I pity them if their hearing
is so weak that they cannot hear through a covering. Let them know that the
whole head is the woman. Its limits and bounds stretch as far as where the
dress begins. As much as unbound hair can occupy, so much is the region of the
veil, so that the neck too may be encircled. For it is the neck that ought to
be subjected, for whose sake power ought to be had upon the head. The veil is
their yoke.

The heathen women of Arabia will judge you, who cover not the head only but
the face so wholly that, with one eye left free, they are content to enjoy
half the light rather than to put the whole face on show. A woman would rather
see than be seen. And for that reason a certain Roman queen said they were
most unhappy, because they could love more than they could be loved; whereas
they are happy even by immunity from the other unhappiness, and the more
frequent one, that women can more easily be loved than love. And the modesty
of heathen discipline is in fact more unmixed and, so to say, more barbarous.
To us the Lord has even by revelations measured the stretches of the veil. For
an angel in dreams, striking the neck of a certain sister of ours as if in
applause, said, Elegant neck, and deservedly bare! It is well that you should
be unveiled from the head down to the loins, lest this liberty of the neck not
profit you either. And of course what you have said to one you have said to
all.

But how much chastisement will those also deserve who, among the psalms or at
any mention of God, keep on uncovered, and who, even when they linger in the
prayer itself, most easily set a fringe or a tuft or any thread at all on the
brain and think themselves covered? They so belittle their head in the lie.
Others, whose palm is plainly larger than any fringe and thread, misuse their
head no less, like a certain beast more than a bird, though winged, with a
small head, a drawn-out neck, the rest high-stepping. This creature, they say,
when it has to hide, thrusts away into the thicket its head alone, plainly the
whole of it, and leaves the rest of itself in the open. So, while it is safe
in the head, bare where it is larger, it is taken whole, head and all. Such
will those also be who are covered less than is useful.

It is necessary, then, at every time and in every place to walk mindful of the
law, ready and equipped for every mention of God; who, if he is in the breast,
will be recognized also on the head of women. To those who read these things
with good peace, who put usefulness before custom, may peace and grace from
our Lord Jesus overflow, together with Septimius Tertullianus, to whom this
little work belongs.
