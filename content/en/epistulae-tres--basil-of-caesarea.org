To Gregory the companion.

I recognized your letter, just as those who recognize the children of friends
from the likeness appearing in them toward those who begot them. For to say
that the construction of the place is not a great thing toward implanting some
impulse in your soul toward the life with us, before you should learn
something about the manner and the way of life, was truly your thought and of
your soul which sets nothing of the things here toward the blessedness laid up
for us in promises. But I am ashamed to write what I myself do in this remote
place, night and day. For I left the occupations in the city as occasions of
countless evils, but I have not yet been able to leave myself. But I am like
those who in the sea, from inexperience according to the voyage, are perishing
and nauseous; who are displeased at the size of the ship as providing much
tossing, and from there transferring to the skiff or the little boat,
everywhere are nauseous and at a loss; for the nausea and the bile go along
with them. Such then is also our case. For carrying around the indwelling
passions everywhere we are with similar tumults, so that we enjoy nothing
great from this solitude. What things one ought to do, however, and from where
it would have been possible for us to hold to the tracks of him who led the
way toward salvation (for if anyone, he says, wishes to come after me, let him
deny himself and take up his cross and follow me), these are they.

It is fitting to try to have the mind in quiet. For as an eye being
continuously turned about, and now being carried round toward the sides, now
being frequently turned toward the things above and below, it is not possible
to see clearly what lies before it, but the sight must be fixed upon the thing
seen, if it is going to make the vision clear; so also a human mind being
dragged about by countless cares according to the world is unable to gaze
clearly upon the truth. But him who is not yet yoked to the bonds of marriage,
raging desires and hard-to-restrain impulses and certain ill-starred loves
throw into confusion; but him who is already shut in with a yokefellow another
tumult of cares receives: in childlessness, desire of children; in the
possession of children, care of child-rearing; watching of a wife, care of a
house, superintendence of household slaves, the injuries according to
contracts, the wranglings toward the neighbors, the entanglements in the
law-courts, the dangers of commerce, the labors of farming. Every day comes
bearing its own darkening of the soul. The nights, taking over the daily
cares, deceive the mind in the same imaginings.

Of these there is one flight, the separation from the whole world. And
withdrawal from the world is not to come to be outside it bodily, but to tear
the soul away from sympathy toward the body and to become cityless, houseless,
without a hearth, without a companion, without property, without a livelihood,
without business, without dealings, ignorant of human teachings, ready to
receive in the heart the impressions that come to be from the divine teaching.
And readiness of heart is the unlearning of the teachings that previously
occupied it from evil custom. For neither is it possible to write on wax
without first smoothing away the characters lying in it; nor to deposit divine
doctrines in a soul without removing its preconceptions from habit. Toward
this then solitude furnishes us the greatest help, lulling our passions and
giving leisure to reason to cut them entirely out of the soul. For as the
beasts are easy to overcome when they have been soothed, so desires and angers
and fears and griefs, the venomous evils of the soul,


having been lulled by quiet and not being made savage by the continuous
provocation, become more easy to overcome by the power of reason. Let the
place then be such as ours also is, freed from mixing with men, so that the
continuity of the exercise not be interrupted by any of the things from
without.

And exercise of piety feeds the soul with the divine thoughts. What then is
more blessed than to imitate the choir of angels on earth? Immediately at the
beginning of day rushing into prayers and with hymns and songs honoring the
Creator; then when the sun has shone clearly, turning to works, prayer being
present with him everywhere, and seasoning the labors with the hymns, as with
salt; for the consolations of the hymns grant the cheerful and painless
condition of the soul.

Quiet then is a beginning of purification for the soul, neither of a tongue
speaking the things of men, nor of eyes looking around at the good complexion
of bodies and their symmetry, nor of hearing dissolving the tension of the
soul in listenings of songs made toward pleasure, nor in words of witty and
jester men, which especially is naturally able to dissolve the tension of the
soul. For a mind not being scattered upon the things outside nor being poured
out upon the world by the sense-organs returns to itself, and through itself
ascends toward the thought concerning God; and being both shone around and
shone upon by that beauty it takes forgetfulness even of nature itself;
neither being dragged down in the soul toward care of food nor toward anxiety
about coverings, but having leisure from earthly cares, it transfers all its
zeal upon the acquisition of the eternal goods: how temperance and courage may
be accomplished for it; and how justice and prudence and the remaining
virtues, as many as being subdivided under these generic ones, submit to the
earnest man to accomplish fittingly each of the things according to life.

And the greatest way toward the finding of what is fitting is the study of the
God-inspired Scriptures. For in these both the suggestions of the deeds are
found and the lives of the blessed men, handed down written, lie before us as
certain living images of the citizenship according to God, by the imitation of
the good works. And so, concerning whatever each perceives himself to be
lacking, spending time with that, as from some common infirmary, he finds the
suitable medicine for the ailment. And the lover of temperance continually
unrolls the history concerning Joseph and from him is taught the temperate
deeds, finding him not only holding himself toward pleasures with
self-control, but also being disposed habitually toward virtue. And courage he
is educated in from Job, who not only, when the opposite things of life had
fallen upon him, becoming poor from rich and childless from having fair
children in one turn of a moment, remained the same, preserving everywhere the
unhumbled thought of the soul. But neither was he provoked by the friends who
had come for consolation, trampling upon him and stretching the painful things
together with him. Again one looking how he might become both meek in the same
and high-spirited, so as to use anger against sin, but meekness toward men,
will find David noble in the deeds of valor according to war, but meek and
unmoved in the repayments of the enemies. Such also is Moses, being stirred
with great anger against those who sin against God, but with a meek soul
bearing the slanders against himself. And everywhere, as the painters,
whenever they paint an image from an image, looking frequently toward the
pattern, are eager to transfer the character from there toward their own
artwork; so also it is necessary for him who is eager to work himself perfect
in all the parts of virtue to look toward the lives of the saints as toward
certain moving and practical statues, and to make their good his own through
imitation.

Prayers again, succeeding the readings, take over the soul more fresh and more
vigorous, with the longing toward God


having been moved. And a good prayer is that which produces a clear thought
of God in the soul. And this is the indwelling of God, to have God established
in oneself through memory. For thus we become a temple of God, whenever the
continuity of the memory is not interrupted by earthly cares, whenever the
mind is not thrown into confusion by unexpected passions, but the God-loving
one, having fled all things, withdraws toward God, and driving out the things
that call us forth into vice spends time in the practices that lead toward
virtue.

And first of all it is fitting to be eager not to be ignorant concerning the
use of speech, but to ask without contentiousness, and to answer without love
of honor, not interrupting the one conversing, whenever he says something
useful, nor desiring to insert one's own word showily, setting measures for
speech and hearing; and to learn without shame and to teach without envy; and
if something has been taught by another, not to conceal it as the base of
women, who substitute bastards, but to proclaim with good will the father of
the word. And the middle tone of voice is to be preferred, so as neither to
escape the hearing by smallness, nor to be burdensome by the greatness of the
stretching. And having first examined in oneself what is to be said, thus to
make the word public. Affable in encounters, sweet in conversations, not
hunting the pleasant through wittiness, but having the gentle through kindly
exhortation. Everywhere thrusting away the harsh, even if it is necessary to
rebuke. For having first laid yourself down through humility, thus you will be
acceptable to the one needing the healing. And often useful to us also is the
prophet's manner of rebuke, who did not bring upon David when he had sinned
the sentence of condemnation from himself, but using a substitution of a
person, seated that one himself as judge of his own sin; so that he, having
first brought forth the judgment against himself, no longer finds fault with
the one who convicted him.

And there follows the humble and cast-down thought a gloomy eye and one bent
toward the earth, a neglected appearance, dry hair, a filthy garment. So that
the things which those who mourn do by practice, these appear to us of
themselves. A tunic drawn in to the body through a belt; the belt however
neither above the loins, for that is womanish; nor slack, so that the tunic
flows apart, for that is slovenly; and the walk neither sluggish, as to accuse
a dissolution of the soul, nor again vehement and hurried, as to hint at its
impulsive motions. The aim of clothing is one, to be a covering of flesh
sufficient toward winter and summer; and let neither the flowery be pursued in
color, nor the fine and soft in the making; for to look around at the good
complexions in clothing is equal to women's adornment, which they practice,
dyeing their own cheeks and hair with an alien flower. But indeed the tunic
ought also to have such thickness as not to need a partner toward warming the
one putting it on. And footwear the cheap according to worth, but filling the
need without lack.

And in a word, as in the garment it is fitting to consider the needful, so
also in food bread will fill the need and water will heal the thirst for the
healthy one and as many side-dishes from seeds as are able to preserve the
strength for the body toward the necessary needs. And to eat not displaying a
raging gluttony, but everywhere preserving the steady and meek and
self-controlled concerning pleasures; nor then having the mind idle in the
thought concerning God, but making the very nature of the foods and the
construction of the receiving body an occasion of doxology: how various kinds
of foods fitting the peculiarity of the bodies have been devised by the one
who manages all things. Prayers before food, to become worthy of the
provisions of God, both of those which he now gives and of those which he has
stored up toward the future. Prayers after food having thanksgiving of the
things given and request of the things promised. One hour of food appointed.
The same according to


period coming round; so that of the twenty-four hours of the day-and-night
this is the one spent upon the body; and the rest the ascetic is occupied in
the work according to mind.

And sleeps light and easy to shake off, naturally following the thinness of
the diet; and by practice being interrupted by the cares concerning the great
things. For to be overpowered by deep stupor, his limbs being loosened, so as
to furnish leisure for unseemly imaginings, puts those who sleep thus in a
daily death. But what dawn is to the others, this midnight is to the ascetics
of piety, the nocturnal quiet especially granting leisure to the soul, neither
of eyes nor of ears sending harmful hearings or sights upon the heart, but the
mind alone by itself being with God, and correcting itself by the memory of
the things that have been sinned, and setting limits for itself toward the
turning away from evil, and seeking the cooperation from God toward the
perfection of the things being pursued.

(These things from us to you, O dear head, narrations of brotherly love. And
do you yourself deign to repay us with your holy prayers, that we might be
delivered from the present evil age, and from unseemly men, and having been
set free from all sin, or rather having been separated from the enemy himself
and plotter against our life, we shall see with a pure heart the God of all
things in knowledge, through the grace of the Lord etc.)

150 {1To Amphilochius, as from Heracleides.}

1 I both remember the things once spoken by us toward one another, and I have
not forgotten both the things I myself said and the things I heard from your
nobility. And now a public life does not hold me. For even if I am the same in
the heart and I have not yet stripped off the old man, nevertheless at least
by the outward form and by making myself far from the affairs of life, I have
seemed henceforth as it were to have set foot upon the way of the citizenship
according to Christ. And I sit by myself, as those who are about to put out
into the open sea, looking out toward what is to come. For those who sail need
winds toward fair sailing, but we need the one who will take us by the hand
and will send us through safely through the salt waves of life. For I reckon
myself to need first a bit toward youth, then goads toward the course of
piety. And a provider of these is clearly a word, now training our disorder,
now raising up the sluggishness of the soul. Again I have need of other
medicines, so as to wash off the filth from habit. For you know that we, who
have been long accustomed to the marketplace, are unsparing of words, and
unguarded toward the imaginings constituted in the thought from the evil one.
And we have been overcome also by honor and we do not easily put away thinking
something of ourselves. Toward these things I reckon I need a great and
experienced teacher. Then however also for the eye of the soul to be restored,
so that, having taken away all the darkening from ignorance, as some rheum, it
is able to gaze upon the beauty of the glory of God, I judge not a small work,
nor to bring the benefit for a little. Which things I know accurately that
your learning also sees together, and desires that someone exist toward this
help; and if ever God should grant to arrive at the same with your seemliness,
clearly I shall learn more concerning the things I must take thought of. For
now from much ignorance I am not even able to recognize of how many things I
am in need; except at least that I have not at all repented of the first
impulse, nor does my soul slacken toward the aim of the life according to God,
which you were anxious about for me, doing well and fittingly for yourself,
lest turning back I become a pillar of salt, which a certain woman suffered,
as I hear. But still both the authorities from without


constrain me, as some deserter of the rulers seeking him. And most of all my
own heart holds me, witnessing to itself those things which I have said.

And since you mentioned covenants, and promised to accuse, you made me laugh
in this dejection of mine, that you are still a rhetor and do not stand away
from the cleverness. For I think, if I do not altogether as one ignorant miss
the truth, that there is one way leading toward the Lord, and that all who
journey toward him travel together with one another and go according to one
covenant of life. So that having gone away where am I able to be separated
from you and not live with you and serve God with you, to whom we have fled in
common? For our bodies will be set apart by places, but the eye of God clearly
looks upon both in common, if indeed also my life is worthy to be overseen by
the eyes of God. For I read somewhere in Psalms, that the eyes of the Lord are
upon the righteous. For I pray both for you and for everyone who chooses
similarly to you both to be together even in the body, and every night and day
with you to bend the knees toward our Father who is in the heavens, and if any
other worthily calling upon God. For I know that the communion in the prayers
brings much gain. But if, as often as it is possible for me in a different
little corner, thrown aside, to groan, the lying will altogether follow me, I
am not able to fight against the word, but already I condemn myself as a liar,
if I uttered anything such according to the old indifference, which sets me
under the judgment of the lie.

And having come near Caesarea, so as to become acquainted with the affairs,
and not having endured to approach the city itself, I fled to the nearby
poorhouse, so as to learn there concerning the things I wished. Then when the
most God-beloved bishop had come according to custom I reported concerning the
things your learning commanded us. And the things he answered it is neither
possible for us to keep in memory, and it exceeds the measure of a letter; but
as in a heading concerning propertylessness he said that the measure is this,
that each should restrict the possession for himself to the last tunic. And he
furnished us the proofs from the Gospel: one, as of John the Baptist having
said: he who has two tunics, let him give to him who has not; and another, as
of the Lord having forbidden the disciples not to have two tunics. And he
added to these also this: if you wish to be perfect, go, sell your belongings,
and give to the poor. And he said that the parable of the pearl also bears
toward this; that the merchant who found the precious pearl, having gone, sold
all his belongings and bought that one. And he added to these, that one must
not even permit to himself the distribution of the money, but to the one who
has been entrusted to manage the things of the poor. And this he confirmed
from the Acts. That selling the things belonging to them, bringing them they
laid them at the feet of the apostles, and from those it was distributed to
each, according as anyone had need. For he said that the discernment of the
one truly in need and of the one asking according to greed needs experience.
And he who gives to the one being afflicted has given to the Lord, and from
him he will receive the wage. But he who has provided to the one going about
has thrown it to every dog, burdensome indeed through shamelessness, but not
pitiable through want.

And concerning how we must live day by day, he had already said few things as
toward the greatness of the subject; nevertheless I wished you to learn from
that one himself. For it is not reasonable for me to make disappear the
accuracy of the teachings. And I prayed sometime with you to reach him, in
order that, having kept the things said accurately in memory, you might also
by your own understanding discover in addition the things lacking. For of that
one I remember from the many things I heard, that the teaching concerning how
the Christian must live does not need so much a word as the daily example. And
I know that, if the bond of the care of your aged father did not hold you, you
yourself would not have preferred anything else to the meeting with the
bishop, nor would you have


advised me, leaving this one, to wander into solitudes. For the caves and the
rocks await us, but the benefits from the men do not always remain for us. So
that, if you endure me advising, you will shape the father to permit you a
little to withdraw from him and to meet a man who both from the experience of
others and from his own understanding knows many things and is able to furnish
them to those who approach him.

173 {1To Theodora the canoness.}

1 The not being persuaded that our letters are altogether put into the hands
of your love makes us slow toward writing, but that by the wickedness of those
serving they first meet countless others; and especially now with the affairs
throughout the world so disturbed. Wherefore I wait in a way to be blamed and
to be demanded the letters by force, so as to use this very thing as a proof
of the delivery. And both writing and being silent we have one work in our
hearts, to keep the memory of your seemliness, and to pray the Lord to grant
you to finish the course of the good citizenship as you chose. For truly it is
no small contest to bring on the things that follow for one who confesses the
promise. For to choose the citizenship according to the Gospel is of everyone,
but also to lead the observation even as far as the smallest things, and to
overlook none of the things written there, this has been accomplished by very
few of those who have come into our knowledge; so as both to use a trained
tongue and an eye trained according to the will of the Gospel, and to work
with hands according to the aim of well-pleasing, and to move feet and to use
each of the members thus as our Creator arranged from the beginning; the
seemly in the deportment, the guarded in the meetings with men, the sufficient
in foods, the unsuperfluous in the possession of the necessary things. All
these things, small indeed when simply thus spoken, but needing a great
contest toward being accomplished, we have found upon the truth itself. And
indeed also the perfect in humility, so as neither to remember the distinction
of ancestors, nor, if some advantage is present in us from nature either
according to body or according to soul, to be lifted up by this, nor to make
the opinions from without concerning us an occasion of lifting up and
inflation, these things hold to the evangelical life. The strenuous in
self-control, the labor-loving in prayers, the sympathetic in brotherly love,
the communicative toward those in need, the cast-down of the thought, the
contrition of the heart, the sound of the faith, the even in gloominess, the
thought of the fearful and inexorable tribunal never leaving us, toward which
we all indeed are hastening, but very few remember it and are anxious about
the outcome from it.
