* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

After Didius Julianus was killed, Severus, a man of Africa, obtained the
empire. His city was Leptis, his father Geta; his forebears were Roman knights
before citizenship was given to all. His mother was Fulvia Pia; his
great-uncles Aper and Severus were of consular rank; his father's father was
Macer, his mother's father Fulvius Pius. He himself was born on the sixth day
before the Ides of April, in the second consulship of Erucius Clarus and the
consulship of Severus.

In his first boyhood, before he was grounded in Latin and Greek letters, in
which he later became most learned, he played no game among the children
except sitting as a judge. He would sit with the rods and axes borne before
him, the boys standing round in order, and give judgment. In his eighteenth
year he declaimed in public. Afterward he came to Rome for his studies, sought
the broad stripe from the deified Marcus, and received it, with the support of
his kinsman Septimius Severus, already twice consul.

When he had come to Rome he found a host who at that very hour was reading the
life of the emperor Hadrian, and he seized on this as an omen of coming
fortune. He had another omen of empire too: invited to an imperial dinner, he
came in a cloak when he should have come in a toga, and he was given a
governor's toga of the emperor himself. That same night he dreamed that he
clung to a she-wolf's teats as Remus or Romulus. He also sat, not knowing it
was forbidden, in the imperial chair a servant had set out by chance. While he
slept in a stable a snake wound about his head and went away without harm,
when his household woke and cried out.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

His youth was full of wildness, and at times of crimes. He stood trial for
adultery and was acquitted by the proconsul Julianus, whom he succeeded in the
proconsulship, had as colleague in the consulship, and likewise succeeded in
the empire. He performed the quaestorship with care, omitting the military
tribunate. After the quaestorship he was allotted Baetica, and from there he
went to Africa to settle his household after his father's death. But while he
was in Africa, Sardinia was assigned him in place of Baetica, because the
Moors were laying Baetica waste. So, after serving as quaestor in Sardinia, he
received a post as legate to the proconsul of Africa. In that office a certain
townsman of his from Leptis, a man of the people, embraced him as an old
comrade while the rods were borne before him; Severus had him beaten with
clubs under this proclamation of the herald:

/Do not, as a man of the people, rashly embrace a legate of the Roman people./

From this it came about that even the legates sat in a carriage, who before
had walked on foot. Then in a certain African city, when he was anxious and
consulted an astrologer, and the astrologer, the hour being set, saw a huge
destiny, he said to him:

/Give me your own nativity, not another's./

And when Severus swore it was his own, the man told him all that later came to
pass.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

He earned the tribunate of the people by the emperor Marcus's decree, and he
held it with the utmost severity and vigor. He then married Marcia, of whom he
said nothing in the history of his private life, though later in his reign he
set up statues to her. He was designated praetor by Marcus not among the
emperor's candidates but in the crowd of competitors, in the thirty-second
year of his life. Then he was sent to Spain and dreamed first that he was told
to restore the temple of Augustus at Tarraco, which was already falling; then
that from the peak of a very high mountain he looked down on the world and on
Rome, the provinces singing together to lyre or flute. Though absent he gave
games. Next he was set over the Fourth Legion, the Scythica, near Massilia.
After this he went to Athens for studies and sacred rites and for the works
and antiquities. There, having suffered certain wrongs from the Athenians, he
became their enemy, and as emperor he avenged himself by cutting their
privileges. Then he received the province of Lugdunum as legate. When, after
losing his wife, he wished to marry another, he asked for the nativities of
the women he might wed, being himself most skilled in astrology; and when he
heard that there was a woman in Syria whose nativity was that she should be
joined to a king, he sought her as wife, Julia of course, and received her
through the help of friends. By her he at once became a father.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

He was loved by the Gauls for his severity, his honor, and his self-restraint
as no one else had been.

Then he governed the Pannonias with proconsular power. After this he obtained
Sicily by lot as a proconsular province. At Rome he received a second son. In
Sicily he was charged as if he had consulted seers or Chaldeans about the
empire, but he was acquitted by the prefects of the guard, to whom he had been
given for hearing, Commodus now coming into hatred, and the accuser was
crucified. He held his first consulship with Apuleius Rufinus, Commodus naming
him among a great many. After the consulship he was idle for about a year;
then, with Laetus supporting him, he was set over the German army. Setting out
to the German armies he bought spacious gardens, though before he had had only
a very small house at Rome and one farm in Venetia. And already in these
gardens, when he was dining on the ground with his sons at a spare meal, and
fruit was set out, the elder son, then five years old, divided it with a too
open hand among his little playmates. When his father rebuked him and said,

/Divide more sparingly, for you do not possess a king's wealth,/

the five-year-old boy answered:

/But I shall possess it./

When he had gone to Germany he conducted himself in that command so as to add
still more to a fame already high.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

So far he served as a soldier while still a private man. Then, when it was
heard among the German legions that Commodus had been killed and that Julianus
was ruling amid the hatred of all, he was named emperor against his will at
the urging of many, at Carnuntum on the Ides of August. He also gave the
soldiers as many sesterces as no prince had ever given. Then, after securing
the provinces he left behind him, he pressed his march to Rome, all yielding
wherever he went, since already the Illyrian and Gallic armies, under
compulsion from their commanders, had sworn allegiance to him. For he was
received by all as the avenger of Pertinax. At the same time, at Julianus's
instance, Septimius Severus was named an enemy by the senate, and envoys were
sent to the army with the senate's words, to order the soldiers, at the
senate's command, to leave him. When Severus heard that envoys had been sent
by the authority of a consenting senate, he was at first afraid; afterward he
so managed, by bribing the envoys, that they spoke for him before the army and
went over to his side. When Julianus learned this he had a decree of the
senate passed on sharing the empire with Severus, whether in good faith or by
a trick is uncertain, since he had already sent certain known killers of
generals to murder Severus, as he had also sent men to kill Pescennius Niger,
who had himself taken up the empire against him at the instance of the Syrian
armies. But Severus, having escaped the hands of those Julianus had sent to
kill him, sent a letter to the praetorians giving the signal either to desert
or to kill Julianus, and his order was obeyed at once. For Julianus was killed
in the Palace, and Severus was invited to Rome; and so, as had befallen no man
before, Severus was made victor by a mere nod, and he pressed on to Rome still
under arms.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

After Julianus was killed, while Severus still remained in camp and tents as
if coming through enemy country, the senate sent a hundred senators as envoys
to congratulate him and to sue for pardon. They met him at Interamna and
greeted him armed, armed men standing round, after they had been searched lest
they have any iron. The next day, when all the palace household came to meet
him, he gave the envoys seven hundred and twenty gold pieces each and sent
them ahead, granting leave if any wished to remain and return with him to
Rome. He at once also made Flavius Juvenalis prefect of the guard, whom
Julianus too had taken as his third prefect.

Meanwhile at Rome there was a huge panic of soldiers and citizens, because
Severus was coming armed against those who had judged him an enemy. To this
was added that he learned Pescennius Niger had been named emperor by the
Syrian legions. He intercepted Niger's edicts and letters to the people and
senate through those who had been sent, lest they be posted to the people or
read in the senate-house. At the same time he also thought of putting Clodius
Albinus in his place, to whom the power of a Caesar seemed already to have
been decreed at Commodus's instance. But fearing those very men of whom he
judged rightly, he sent Heraclitus to hold Britain and Plautianus to seize
Niger's children. When Severus had come to Rome, he ordered the praetorians to
meet him unarmed, in their undergarments only; then he called them thus to the
tribunal, armed men posted all around.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Then he entered Rome armed, with armed soldiers, and went up to the Capitol;
from there he went on in the same dress to the Palace, the standards he had
taken from the praetorians being borne before him not upright but trailing.
Then throughout the whole city the soldiers stayed in temples, in porticoes,
and in the Palatine houses as if in stables. Severus's entry was hateful and
terrible, since the soldiers seized what they had not bought and threatened to
lay the city waste. The next day, attended not only by armed soldiers but by
armed friends, he came into the senate. In the senate-house he gave an account
of his taking of the empire and alleged that Julianus had sent known killers
of generals to murder him. He also forced a decree of the senate that an
emperor should not be allowed to kill a senator without consulting the senate.
But while he was in the senate, the soldiers in a mutiny demanded ten thousand
sesterces each from the senate, citing the example of those who had brought
Augustus Octavian to Rome and received as much. And though Severus wished to
check them and could not, he nevertheless dismissed them, once they were
quieted, with an added bounty.

Then he led a censor's funeral for the image of Pertinax and enrolled him
among the gods, adding a flamen and Helvian brethren, who had been the Marcian
brethren. He also ordered himself to be called Pertinax, though later he
wished that name abolished as an omen.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Next he paid his friends' debts. He dowered his daughters and gave them in
marriage to Probus and Aetius. When he offered his son-in-law Probus the
prefecture of the city, Probus refused and said it seemed less to him to be
prefect than son-in-law of the prince. He made both sons-in-law consuls at
once and made both rich. Another day he came to the senate and, after they
were accused, gave Julianus's friends over to outlawry and death. He heard a
great many cases. Judges accused by the provincials he punished heavily when
the charges were proved. He so provided for the grain-supply, which he had
found very low, that at his death he left the Roman people a seven years'
allowance.

He set out to settle the East, still saying nothing openly of Niger. Yet he
sent legions to Africa, lest Niger seize Africa through Libya and Egypt and
press the Roman people with a lack of grain. He left Domitius Dexter prefect
of the city in place of Bassus, and he set out within thirty days of coming to
Rome.

Leaving the city he suffered a huge mutiny of the army at Saxa Rubra over the
place for marking the camp. His brother Geta also met him at once, and he
ordered him to govern the province already entrusted to him, though Geta hoped
for something else. Niger's children, brought to him, he held in the same
honor as his own. He had indeed sent a legion to occupy Greece and Thrace
first, lest Pescennius seize them. But Niger already held Byzantium. Wishing
also to seize Perinthus, Niger killed a great many of the army, and for that
he was named an enemy along with Aemilianus. When he invited Severus to a
share of power, he was scorned. Severus did, however, promise Niger a safe
exile if he wished it; but he did not pardon Aemilianus. Then Aemilianus,
beaten at the Hellespont by Severus's generals, fled first to Cyzicus and from
there to another city, where he was killed by their order. Niger's forces too
were routed by the same generals.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

When he heard this, Severus sent a letter to the senate as if the business
were finished. Then he met Niger and killed him near Cyzicus and carried his
head about on a pike. After this he sent Niger's sons, whom he had kept in the
state of his own children, into exile with their mother.

He sent a letter to the senate announcing the victory, and he put none of the
senators who had been of Niger's party to death except one. He was more angry
with the people of Antioch, because they had laughed at him while he governed
in the East and had also helped Niger with food. In the end he took many
things from them. He also took the right of citizenship from the people of
Neapolis in Palestine, because they had long been in arms for Niger. He
punished many harshly, outside the senatorial order, who had followed Niger.
He also visited many cities of that party with injuries and losses, and he
killed those senators who had served with Niger under the name of generals or
tribunes.

Then he did more around Arabia, the Parthians also being brought under his
power, and the Adiabeni too, all of whom had sided with Pescennius. For this,
when he returned and a triumph was voted, he was named Arabicus, Adiabenicus,
and Parthicus. But he refused the triumph, lest he seem to triumph for a civil
victory, and he declined the name Parthicus as well, lest he provoke the
Parthians.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

As he was returning to Rome after Niger's civil war, another civil war was
announced, that of Clodius Albinus, who had rebelled in Gaul. For this reason
Niger's sons were later killed with their mother. He therefore at once judged
Albinus an enemy, and those who had written or written back to him too mildly.
And as he went against Albinus, on the way at Viminacium he named his elder
son Bassianus Caesar, adding the name Aurelius Antoninus, in order to push his
brother Geta from the hope of empire which Geta had conceived. He added the
name Antoninus to his son because he had dreamed that an Antoninus would
succeed him. Hence some think Geta too was called Antoninus, that he also
might succeed in the empire. Others think he was called Antoninus because
Severus himself wished to pass into the family of Marcus.

At first indeed Severus's generals were beaten by Albinus's men. Then,
anxious, when he consulted, he learned from Pannonian augurs that he would be
the victor, but that his adversary would neither come into his power nor
escape, but would die near water.

Many friends of Albinus at once deserted and came over; many generals were
taken,

on whom Severus inflicted punishment. Meanwhile, after many things done in
various ways in Gaul, Severus first fought most fortunately against Albinus at
Tinurtium. There indeed he ran a huge danger by the fall of his horse, so that
he was thought dead from a blow of a leaden ball, and so that another emperor
was already almost chosen by the army. At that time, when he had read the acts
passed in praise of Clodius Celsinus, who was of Hadrumetum and a kinsman of
Albinus, Severus was angry with the senate, as if the senate had granted this
to Albinus, and he voted that Commodus should be enrolled among the gods, as
if by this sort of thing he could avenge himself on the senate. He first
proclaimed Commodus a god among the soldiers and wrote it to the senate,
adding a speech of his victory. Then he ordered the bodies of the senators who
had been killed in the war to be scattered. Then, when Albinus's body was
brought, almost still half alive, he ordered the head cut off and carried to
Rome, and he followed this with a letter. Albinus was beaten on the eleventh
day before the Kalends of March.

The rest of his body he ordered to be laid out before his own house and to be
seen for a long time. Besides, he himself rode over Albinus's corpse, and when
the horse shied he urged it on and loosed the reins so that it should trample
boldly. Others add that he ordered the same body thrown into the Rhone, and
the bodies of his wife and children as well.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

When countless men of Albinus's party had been killed, among them many leading
men of the state and many distinguished women, the goods of all were
confiscated and increased the treasury. Then many nobles of Spain and Gaul
were killed as well. In the end he gave the soldiers as much pay as no prince
had given. From this confiscation he also left his sons as much as no emperor
had left, since he had made a great part of the gold through Gaul, through
Spain, and through Italy imperial property. Then first the stewardship of
private affairs was established. Many indeed who after Albinus still kept
faith with him were overcome by Severus in war. At the same time it was also
announced that the Arabian legion had gone over to Albinus.

So, having harshly avenged Albinus's revolt, killing very many and wiping out
his family as well, he came to Rome angry at both people and senators. He
praised Commodus in the senate and in an assembly, called him a god, and said
he had displeased only the infamous, so that it was clear he was raging quite
openly. After this he spoke of his own clemency, though he had been most cruel
and killed the senators written below.

and so killed him. Then he threw Narcissus, the strangler of Commodus, to the
lions. Besides he killed many men of obscure station, not counting those whom
the violence of battle consumed.

After this, wishing to recommend himself to men, he transferred the posting
service from private persons to the treasury. Then he had Bassianus Antoninus
named Caesar by the senate, with the imperial insignia decreed. Then, stirred
by a rumor of a Parthian war, he set up statues himself to his father, mother,
grandfather, and former wife. Plautianus, once his closest friend, he so hated
once he knew his life, that he named him a public enemy and, taking down his
statues throughout the world, marked him with a heavy insult, angry above all
because Plautianus had set his own statue among the images of Severus's
kinsmen and connections. He remitted to the Palestinians the penalty they had
earned on Niger's account. Later he came back into favor with Plautianus and
entered the city as if in an ovation and went to the Capitol, though in time
he killed him too. He gave the toga of manhood to Geta, his younger son, and
joined the daughter of Plautianus as wife to the elder. Those who had called
Plautianus a public enemy were banished. So there is always a change of all
things, as if by a law of nature. Then he designated his sons consuls. He
greatly honored his brother Geta. Then he set out to the Parthian war, after
giving a gladiatorial show and a largess to the people. In the midst of this
he killed many on true or pretended charges. Most were condemned because they
had joked, others because they had been silent, others because they had said
many things with double meaning, such as

/See, an emperor truly of his name, truly Pertinax, truly Severus./

* Caput 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

It was indeed common talk that Septimius Severus was seeking a Parthian war,
led by a lust for glory, not by any necessity. At last, having shipped the
army from Brundisium, he came by a continuous journey into Syria and drove the
Parthians back. But afterward he returned to Syria so as to prepare himself
and carry war against the Parthians. Meanwhile, at Plautianus's instance, he
hunted down the remnants of Pescennius's party, so that he even attacked some
of his own friends as if they were plotting against his life. He also killed
many as if they had consulted Chaldeans or seers about his safety, suspecting
especially anyone fit for empire, since he himself still had sons who were
little children, and he believed or heard that this was said by those who were
forecasting empire for themselves. In the end, when some had been killed,
Severus excused himself and after their death denied that he had ordered what
was done. Marius Maximus says this especially of Laetus. When his sister from
Leptis came to him, speaking Latin with difficulty, and the emperor blushed
much for her, he gave her son the broad stripe and many gifts to her, and
ordered the woman to return to her country, and with her son too, who died a
short time later.

:PROPERTIES:

So, as summer was already ending, he entered Parthia, drove the king, and came
to Ctesiphon and took it near winter time, because in those regions wars are
better waged in winter, though the soldiers lived on the roots of plants and
from that contracted diseases and sicknesses. Therefore, though he could not
go farther, the Parthians blocking the way and the soldiers' bowels also
running from unaccustomed food, he nevertheless held on and took the town and
put the king to flight and killed a great many and earned the name Parthicus.
For this the soldiers also named his son Bassianus Antoninus, who had already
been called Caesar and was in his thirteenth year, a partner in the empire.
They named Geta too, the younger son, Caesar, calling him Antoninus as well,
as most writers relate. For these namings he gave the soldiers a most lavish
donative, granting all the plunder of the Parthian town, which the soldiers
were seeking. From there he returned to Syria as victor, and when the senators
offered him a Parthian triumph he refused it because, afflicted with a disease
of the joints, he could not stand in the chariot. He did, however, grant that
his son should triumph; the senate had decreed him a Jewish triumph, because
things had also been well done in Syria by Severus.

Then, when he had passed Antioch, he gave the toga of manhood to his elder son
and designated him consul with himself, and at once they entered the
consulship in Syria. After this, having given a more abundant stipend

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

he went to Alexandria. On the way he established very many rights for the
Palestinians. He forbade becoming Jews under a heavy penalty; he enacted the
same also concerning Christians. Then he gave the Alexandrians the right of
councilors, who without a public council lived as they had before under the
kings, content with one judge whom Caesar had given. He changed many other
rights for them as well. Severus himself always afterward showed that this
journey had been pleasant to him because of the worship of the god Serapis,
because of knowledge of antiquities, and because of the strangeness of animals
and places. For he carefully inspected Memphis and Memnon and the pyramids and
the labyrinth.

And since it is long to follow lesser things, these are the splendid deeds of
his: that after Julianus was beaten and killed he discharged the praetorian
cohorts; that against the soldiers' will he enrolled Pertinax among the gods;
that he ordered the decrees of Salvius Julianus to be abolished, which he did
not obtain. In the end he seems to have had the surname Pertinax not so much
from his own wish as from the spare ways of his character. For he was held
more cruel from the endless slaughter of many, and when one of his enemies had
come to him as a suppliant and said to him

/What would you have done?/

he was not softened by so prudent a saying and ordered him killed.

He was besides eager to wipe out factions. He almost

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

never left an encounter except as victor. He subdued Abgarus, king of the
Persians. He brought the Arabs under his power. He forced the Adiabeni to pay
tribute. Britain, which is the greatest glory of his reign, he fortified with
a wall drawn across the island to the Ocean at both ends. From this he also
received the name Britannicus. Tripolis, from which he came, he made most
secure by crushing the most warlike peoples, and he gave the Roman people a
free and most plentiful daily oil forever.

He was as implacable toward offenses as he was of singular judgment in raising
up all who were industrious. He was devoted enough to philosophy and to the
studies of speaking, and overly eager for learning as well. Everywhere an
enemy of robbers, he himself wrote a trustworthy account of his private and
public life, excusing only the fault of cruelty. Of him the senate judged
thus, that he either ought not to have been born or ought not to have died,
because he seemed both too cruel and too useful to the state. At home,
however, he was less cautious, who kept his wife Julia, infamous for
adulteries and even charged with conspiracy. The same man, when, lame in the
feet, he delayed a war, and the soldiers took this hard and had made his son
Bassianus, who was with him, Augustus, ordered himself lifted and carried to
the tribunal. Then he ordered all the tribunes, centurions, generals, and
cohorts by whose instance this had happened to be present, then his son, who
had received the name Augustus, to be stood forth; and when he ordered
punishment on all the authors of the deed except his son, and was asked for
pardon, all prostrate before the tribunal, touching his head with his hand he
said:

/At last you feel that the head rules, not the feet./

It is his saying, when fortune had led him by many steps from a low place
through the offices of letters and of war to the empire:

/I have been all things, and nothing avails./

:PROPERTIES:

He died at Eboracum in Britain, after subduing the peoples who seemed hostile
to Britain, in the eighteenth year of his reign, cut off by a most heavy
disease, already an old man. He left two sons, Antoninus Bassianus and Geta,
on whom too he set the name Antoninus in honor of Marcus. He was laid in the
tomb of Marcus Antoninus, whom of all emperors he so honored that he even
enrolled Commodus among the gods and thought the name Antoninus should
thereafter be written for all, as if it were Augustus. He himself, at the
instance of his children, who had given him a most ample funeral, was enrolled
among the gods by the senate.

His chief public works still standing are the Septizonium and the Baths of
Severus. Also the Septimian Baths in the region across the Tiber at the gate
of his name, whose aqueduct falling at once denied them public use.

The judgment of him after death was great among all, most of all because for a
long time no good came to the state from his sons, and afterward, when many
invaded the state, the Roman empire was plunder for robbers.

He used such scanty clothes that even his tunic had scarcely any purple, while
he covered his shoulders with a shaggy cloak. He was most spare of food,
greedy of his native pulse, at times fond of wine, often without meat. He
himself was handsome, large, with a long beard, a grey and curly head, a face
that inspired reverence, a clear voice, but with something of an African
accent even to old age; and much loved after death, either when envy was laid

I remember reading in Aelius Maurus, freedman of Phlegon the freedman of
Hadrian, that Septimius Severus rejoiced beyond measure when he was dying,
because he was leaving two Antonini to the state with equal power, after the
example of Pius, who left Verus and Marcus Antoninus as sons by adoption to
the state; and this the better, because that man was giving adopted sons, this
man sons begotten by himself, as rulers of the Roman state. He had received
Antoninus Bassianus from a former marriage and had begotten Geta of Julia. But
that hope deceived him much; for murder denied the one to the state, his own
character the other. And that holy name remained well in almost no one for

These were the signs of his death: he himself dreamed that he was snatched to
heaven by four eagles and a jeweled chariot, some huge human shape flying
before, I know not what; and as he was snatched he counted out the numbers
eighty and nine, beyond which years he did not live even one year, for he came
to the empire an old man. And when he had been set in a huge circle in the
air, he stood long alone and forsaken; but when he feared he would fall
headlong, he saw himself called by Jupiter and placed among the Antonines.

On a day of circus games, when three little Victories of plaster had been set
as usual with palms, the middle one, which held a globe inscribed with his
name, struck by the wind, fell from the podium still standing and came to rest
on the ground; but the one inscribed with Geta's name collapsed and was wholly
shattered; the one that bore Bassianus's title, having lost its palm, barely
stood in a whirl of wind. After the wall at Luguvallum in Britain had been
seen, when he was returning to the nearest lodging, not only as victor but
with peace founded forever, turning in his mind what omen might meet him, a
certain Ethiopian from the soldiers, famous among jesters and always noted for
his jokes, met him with a crown made of cypress. When he, angry, ordered him
removed from his sight, touched by the omen of his color and of the crown, the
man is said to have said in jest:

/You have been all things, you have conquered all things; now, victor, be a
god./

And when, coming into the city, he wished to make a sacrifice, he was first
led to the temple of Bellona by the error of a rustic soothsayer; then dark
victims were brought. When he had rejected this and was withdrawing to the
Palace, by the negligence of the attendants the black victims followed the
emperor even to the threshold of the Palatine house.

There are famous works of his through very many cities. But that was great in
his life, that at Rome he restored all the public temples that were falling by
the fault of time, almost never writing his own name, yet everywhere keeping
the titles of the founders. Dying he left a seven years' grain-allowance, so
that seventy-five thousand pecks a day could be spent; and so much oil that
for five years it would suffice not only for the uses of the city but for all
Italy that needs oil.

His last words are said to have been these:

/I received the state troubled on every side; I leave it at peace even among
the Britons, an old man and lame in the feet, leaving a firm empire to my
Antonines if they are good, a weak one if they are bad./

Then he ordered the watchword given to the tribune

/Let us labor,/

because Pertinax, when he was taken up into the empire, had given the
watchword

/Let us be soldiers./

Then he had resolved to double the royal Fortune which used to attend princes
and be set in their bedchambers, that he might leave the most sacred image to
each of his sons; but when he saw himself hard pressed at the hour of death,
he is said to have ordered that Fortune be placed on alternate days in the
bedchambers of his sons the emperors. Bassianus scorned this before he
committed the murder of a brother.

His body was received from Britain all the way to Rome with great reverence of
the provincials; though some say there was only a little golden urn containing
Severus's remains, and that this was laid in the tomb of the Antonines,
Septimius having been burned where he ended his life.

When he made the Septizonium he thought of nothing else than that his work
should meet those coming from Africa. Had not, in his absence, his statue been
set in the middle by the prefect of the city, he is said to have wished to
make an entrance to the Palatine houses, that is the royal hall, from that
side. This even Alexander later wished to do, but he is said to have been
forbidden by the soothsayers, since when he inquired of this he did not obtain
a favorable omen.
