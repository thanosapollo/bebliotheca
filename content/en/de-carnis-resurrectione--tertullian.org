* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The confidence of Christians is the resurrection of the dead. Believing that,
we are. Truth compels us to believe this; God opens the truth; but the crowd
mocks, thinking nothing remains after death. And yet they make offerings to
the dead, and indeed with the most lavish duty according to their customs,
according to the seasons of foods, so that they presume those whom they deny
feel anything also to desire. But I will rather laugh at the crowd, then too,
when it most cruelly burns the very dead whom afterward it most greedily
feeds, by the same fires both earning favor and giving offense. O piety
playing from cruelty! Does it sacrifice or insult, when it burns to the
burned? Plainly at times even the wise join their opinion with the crowd. That
nothing is after death is the school of Epicurus. Seneca too says all things
are ended after death, even death itself.

But it is enough if a philosophy no less, that of Pythagoras and Empedocles
and the Platonist, on the contrary proclaim the soul immortal, nay still more
nearly even affirm it returnable into bodies, though not into the same, though
not into human only, so that Euphorbus is listed into Pythagoras, Homer into a
peacock. Certainly they have pronounced a bodily relapse of the soul, more
tolerably with the quality changed than denied, the truth at least struck,
though not entered. Thus the world, even when it errs, is not ignorant of the
resurrection of the dead.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But if even with God there is some sect more akin to Epicureans than to
prophets, we shall know what the Sadducees hear from Christ. For it was
reserved for Christ to lay bare all things formerly hidden, to set right
things doubted, to fill out things tasted beforehand, to present things
preached, and certainly to prove the resurrection of the dead not only through
himself but also in himself. Now however we are being prepared against other
Sadducees, partners of their opinion. Thus they acknowledge a half
resurrection, of the soul alone of course, having despised the flesh, just as
they have also despised the Lord of the flesh. In short, none others envy
salvation to the bodily substance than heretics of another divinity. And
therefore, compelled also to arrange Christ otherwise, lest he be held to be
the Creator's, they have first gone astray in his very flesh, either
contending that it is of no truth according to Marcion and Basilides, or of a
peculiar quality according to the heresies of Valentinus and Apelles. And so
it follows that they shut out the salvation of that substance whose partner
they deny Christ to be, sure that it has been equipped with the highest
prejudgment of resurrection if the flesh has already risen in Christ. For this
reason we too have sent ahead a volume ON THE FLESH OF CHRIST, in which we
both prove it solid against the vanity of a phantasm and vindicate it as human
against a peculiarity of quality, whose condition has inscribed Christ both
man and son of man. For by proving him fleshy and bodily we likewise also shut
them out by prescribing that no other god is to be believed besides the
Creator, while we show such a Christ, in whom God is recognized, as is
promised by the Creator. Shut out next concerning God the author of the flesh
and concerning Christ the redeemer of the flesh, they will now also be
convicted concerning the resurrection of the flesh. Congruently, of course.
And we say that debate with heretics is to be entered almost in this way. For
order always demands to be drawn from the principal things, so that it may
first be established concerning him by whom that which is in question is said
to have been arranged. And so even the heretics, from consciousness of
weakness, never treat the matter in ordinary order. For sure how they labor in
insinuating another divinity against the God of the world, known naturally to
all from the testimonies of his works, certainly also prior in the sacraments
and more manifest in the preachings, under the pretext of a more urgent cause,
that is of human salvation itself to be sought before all things, they begin
from the questions of resurrection, because the resurrection of the flesh is
more hardly believed than one divinity; and so they season a treatment
stripped of the forces of its own order and loaded rather with scruples, by
those who cheapen the flesh, little by little toward the sense of another
divinity from the very shaking and changing of hope. For each one, thrown down
or moved from the grade of that hope which he had taken up with the Creator,
is now easily turned aside to an author of another hope even to be suspected
of his own accord. For through diversity of promises a diversity of gods is
insinuated. Thus we see many ensnared, while they are first crushed concerning
the resurrection of the flesh before they crush concerning the unity of
divinity. Therefore as far as concerns the heretics, we have shown with what
wedge they are to be met by us. And they have already been met, each under his
own title, concerning the one God indeed and his Christ against Marcion,
concerning the flesh of the Lord however even against four heresies, to
prepare especially for this question; so that now it is to be digested
concerning the resurrection of the flesh alone, as if it were uncertain even
with us, that is with the Creator. For there are also many untrained, and very
many doubtful in their own faith, and more who are simple, who will have to be
instructed, directed, fortified, because by this side too the unity of
divinity will be defended. For as, when the resurrection of the flesh is
denied, it is shaken, so when it is vindicated it is made firm. The salvation
of the soul however I believe to be without debate. For nearly all heretics,
in whatever way they will it, yet do not deny it. Let some one Lucanus look to
it, not sparing even this substance, dissolving it according to Aristotle and
substituting something else for it, some third thing that will rise, neither
soul nor flesh, that is not a man, but a bear perhaps, as Lucanus is. He too
has from us the fullest pen on the whole state of the soul, which, defending
it first as immortal, we acknowledge the failing of the flesh alone and assert
its restoring most of all, having been brought back into the ordinary body of
the matter, if we have postponed anywhere things briefly touched because of
the inrush of causes. For as it is customary for some things to be tasted
beforehand, so also it is necessary that they be deferred, provided both that
things tasted beforehand are filled out in their own body and that things
deferred are rendered under their own name.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

It is indeed also permitted to have a taste from common senses in the things
of God, but for a testimony of the true, not for a help of the false, which
may be according to the divine arrangement, not against the divine. For some
things are known even by nature, as the immortality of the soul with most, as
our God with all. I will use therefore even the sentence of some Plato
pronouncing, Every soul is immortal; I will use also the conscience of the
people attesting the God of gods. I will use also the rest of the common
senses which preach God as judge: God sees, and, I commit it to God. But when
they say, Dead is dead, and, Live while you live, and, After death all things
are ended, even death itself, then I shall remember both that the heart of the
crowd has been reckoned ash by God and that the very wisdom of the world has
been pronounced foolishness. Then if the heretic too shall have fled to the
vices of the crowd or the talents of the world, Depart, I will say, from the
heathen, heretic; although you are all one who invent God, yet while you do
this in the name of Christ, while you seem a Christian to yourself, you are
other than the heathen; give him back his senses, because neither is he
instructed from yours. Why do you lean on a blind guide, if you see? Why are
you clothed by the naked, if you have put on Christ? Why do you use another's
shield, if you have been armed by the apostle? Let him rather learn from you
to confess the resurrection of the flesh than you from him to deny it; because
if it ought to be denied even by Christians, it would suffice for them to be
instructed from their own knowledge, not from the ignorance of the crowd. So
far will he not be a Christian who shall deny it which Christians confess, and
shall deny it with those arguments which a non-Christian uses. Take away
finally from heretics what they taste with the heathen, so that they may set
up their questions from the scriptures alone, and they will not be able to
stand. For the common senses are commended by simplicity itself and by
fellow-feeling of sentences and familiarity of opinions, and they are thought
so much the more faithful because they define things naked and open and known
to all. Divine reason however is in the marrow, not on the surface, and for
the most part rival to things manifest.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And so the heretics begin at once from there, and from there they
preconstruct, then they interweave, from where they know minds are easily
taken, from the favorable communion of senses. Or do you first or more hear
anything else from a heretic than from a heathen? and not immediately and not
everywhere the abuse of the flesh, against its origin, against its material,
against its fall, against its whole exit, unclean from the beginning from the
dregs of earth, more unclean next from the slime of its own seed, frivolous,
weak, criminal, burdensome, troublesome, and after the whole indictment of
ignobility falling into its origin earth and the name of a corpse, and from
that name too about to perish into no name thence already, into the death
already of every word? This then, snatched from your sight and touch and
memory, do you, a wise man, wish to persuade, that it is at some time to
receive itself into the whole from the corrupted, into the solid from the
void, into the full from the emptied, into something at all from nothing, and
that too with fires and waters and the bellies of beasts and the craws of
birds and the milts of fishes and the proper gullet of the times themselves
giving it back? But will the same be hoped for which has fallen away, so that
the lame and the one-eyed and the blind and the leper and the paralytic may
return, so that it may not please to have gone back to the former state? or
whole, so that they may fear to suffer such things again? What then of the
consequences of the flesh? Again all things necessary to it, and first of all
foods and drinks, and swimming for the lungs, and seething for the intestines,
and no shame for the shameful parts, and labor for all the members? Again
ulcers and wounds and fever and gout and death to be wished for again? These
of course will be the vows of flesh to be recovered, to desire again to escape
from it. And we indeed say these things somewhat more decently for the shame
of the pen. For the rest, how far even their foul speech may go you may
experience in meetings, of heathen as of heretics.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Therefore since both the untrained still taste from the common senses, and the
doubtful and the simple are again disturbed through the same senses, and
everywhere this first ram is aimed against us by which the condition of the
flesh is shaken, necessarily the condition of the flesh first will be
fortified by us too, the vituperation driven off by praise. Thus the heretics
provoke us to play the rhetorician also, as even to philosophize. This futile
and frivolous little body, which they do not shudder finally to call evil,
even if it had been the work of angels, as it pleases Menander and Marcus,
even if the construction of some fiery angel equally, as Apelles teaches, the
patronage of a second divinity would suffice for the authority of the flesh.
We know angels after God. Now whoever that highest god of each heretic is, not
undeservedly would I also derive the dignity of the flesh from him by whom
there had been a will of producing it. For he would certainly have forbidden
it to be made rather than have known it was being made, if he had not willed
it to be made. Thus even according to them the flesh is equally a thing of
God. Nothing of a work is not his who has suffered it to be. It is well
however that both the more numerous and the harder doctrines yield the whole
fashioning of man to our God. How great this one is you know well enough, you
who have believed him unique. Let the flesh now begin to please you, whose
craftsman is so great. But the world too, you say, is the work of God, and yet
the fashion of this world passes, with the apostle also as author; nor will a
restoration of the world be prejudged on that account because it is the work
of God. And certainly if the universe is unreformable after its departure,
what of a portion? Plainly, if the portion is equated to the universe. For we
appeal to distance. First indeed, that all things were made by the Word of
God, and without him nothing. The flesh however has stood both by the Word of
God for the sake of form, lest anything be without the Word. For Let us make
man, he sent ahead first, and further, by hand, for the sake of preference,
lest it be compared to the universe. And God fashioned, he says, man. The
reason of a great difference without doubt, according to the condition of
things of course. For lesser were the things that were being made than he for
whom they were being made, if indeed they were being made for man, to whom
they were soon assigned by God. Rightly therefore as servants by command and
empire and vocal power alone had all things come forth. Against this man, as
their lord, was built up by God himself for this, that he might be able to be
lord while he is made by the Lord. Remember however that man is properly said
of the flesh, which first occupied the name of man: And God fl(??)xed man,
clay from the earth. Already man, who is still clay. And he breathed into his
face the breath of life, and man became, that is the clay, into a living soul,
and God placed man, whom he fashioned, in paradise. So far is man first a
figment, then whole. This I would commend, so that whatever at all has been
provided and promised by God for man, you may know due not to the soul alone
but also to the flesh, if not from partnership of kind, certainly at least
from privilege of name.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

I will therefore pursue the purpose, if I can yet vindicate as much for the
flesh as he conferred who made it, already then glorying that that littleness,
clay, came into the hands of God, whatever they are, blessed enough even if
only touched. For what if by no further work the figment had at once stood
from the touch of God? So great a thing was being done, by which that material
was being built up. Therefore it is honored as often as it suffers the hand of
God, while it is touched, while it is plucked, while it is drawn down, while
it is fashioned. Think over God wholly occupied and given to it, with hand,
sense, work, counsel, wisdom, providence, and first of all with that very
affection which was dictating the lineaments. For whatever the clay was being
pressed into, Christ was being thought, the man to be, because both clay and
flesh are the Word, because also earth then. For so the preface of the Father
to the Son: Let us make man according to our image and likeness. And God made
man, that of course which he fashioned, he made him according to the image of
God, namely of Christ. For the Word also is God, who, being set in the figure
of God, did not think it robbery to be equal to God. Thus that clay already
then putting on the image of Christ to be in flesh was not only the work of
God but also a pledge. What does it now do toward darkening the origin of the
flesh to toss about the name of earth, as of a sordid, as of a lying element?
when, even if another material had been suitable for striking out man, the
height of the craftsman ought to be thought over, who both by choosing it had
judged it worthy and by handling it had made it. The hand of Phidias works the
Olympian Jupiter from ivory, and it is adored, and it is no longer the god of
a beast and indeed of the most tasteless, but the highest divinity of the
world, not because the elephant, but because Phidias so great: would the
living God, the true God, not have purged any cheapness of material by his own
working and healed it from every weakness? Or will this remain, that man has
more honorably fashioned God than God man? Now even if clay is a scandal, it
is already another thing. I now hold flesh, not earth; though even flesh may
hear: You are earth, and into earth you shall go. The origin is reviewed, the
substance is not recalled. It has been granted that something is more generous
than its origin and happier by change. For gold too is earth, because from
earth; yet earth only so far, from which it is gold, a far other material,
more splendid and more noble from a more obsolete matrix. Thus it was also
permitted to God to refine the gold of flesh from the filth of clay, whatever
you think it, the census having been excused.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But the authority of the flesh may seem more diluted, because the divine hand
did not handle it too properly, as it did the clay. When it handled the clay
for this, that afterward flesh might become from clay, it was certainly doing
the business of the flesh. But I would still wish you to learn when and how
the flesh flowered from clay. For those tunics of skin which Adam and Eve,
stripped of paradise, put on will not, as some will, themselves be the
reforming of the flesh from clay, since somewhat earlier both Adam recognized
a transmission of his own substance in the woman already as flesh (This now is
bone from my bones, and flesh from my flesh), and the very tasting of the male
into the woman was supplied with flesh, to be supplied with clay, I suppose,
if Adam was still clay. The clay therefore has been obliterated and swallowed
into flesh. When? when man was made into a living soul from the breath of God,
a vapor of course fit to bake the clay somehow into another quality, as into
something roasted, so into flesh. Thus it is also permitted to a potter by a
tempered blast of fire to rebody clay into a stronger material and to bind
another species from another, fitter than the former and already of its own
kind and name. For even if it is written, Shall the clay say to the potter?
that is man to God, even if the apostle says, In earthen vessels, yet both is
man clay, because clay before; and flesh is pottery, because from clay through
the vapor of the divine blast, which afterward the tunics of skin, that is
skins drawn over, clothed. So far, if you have drawn off the skin, you have
stripped the flesh. Thus that which today is made a spoil, if it be drawn off,
this was a garment when it was built over. Hence also the apostle, calling
circumcision a stripping of the flesh, has affirmed the skin a tunic. These
things being so, you have both clay glorious from the hand of God and flesh
more glorious from the breath of God, by which equally the flesh both laid
down the rudiments of clay and took up the ornaments of the soul. You are not
more diligent than God, so that you indeed do not enclose Scythian and Indian
gems and the shining grains of the Red Sea with lead, with bronze, with iron,
not even with silver, but bury them in the most choice and moreover most
laborious gold, and likewise for wines and the most precious unguents first
take care of the fitness of vessels, and equally match the dignity of
scabbards to swords of perfect rust-color; but God has committed the shadow of
his soul, the breeze of his spirit, the work of his mouth to some cheapest
hilt, and by placing it unworthily has of course condemned it. But has he
placed it, or rather inserted it and mixed it with the flesh? with so great a
concretion indeed that it can be held uncertain whether the flesh carries the
soul around, or the soul the flesh, whether the flesh appears of the soul, or
the soul of the flesh. But it is more to be believed that the soul is borne in
and rules, as nearer to God. This too overflows to the glory of the flesh,
that it both contains what is nearest to God and renders it possessor of the
very rule. For what use of nature, what fruit of the world, what savor of the
elements does the soul not feed on through the flesh? why not? through which
it is supported by every instrument of the senses, sight, hearing, taste,
smell, touch? through which it has been sprinkled with divine power,
accomplishing nothing not by speech, even when it is sent ahead silently? For
speech too is from the organ of flesh. Arts through the flesh, studies,
talents through the flesh, works, businesses, offices through the flesh, and
so the whole living of the soul is of the flesh, so that for the soul not to
live is nothing other than to turn aside from the flesh. Thus even to die
itself is of the flesh, whose also it is to live. Further if all things
through the flesh lie under the soul, they will lie under the flesh too.
Through what you use, with that you must use. Thus the flesh, while it is
reckoned minister and servant of the soul, is found partner and coheir. If of
temporal things, why not also of eternal?

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And these suffrages indeed I would have procured for the flesh as from the
public form of the human condition. Let us see now from the proper form also
of the Christian name how great a prerogative with God this frivolous and
sordid substance has: although it would suffice for it that no soul at all can
obtain salvation unless while it is in the flesh it has believed; so far is
the flesh the hinge of salvation. From which when the soul is chosen for God,
it is itself which causes that the soul can be chosen. Of course the flesh is
washed, that the soul may be unstained; the flesh is anointed, that the soul
may be consecrated; the flesh is signed, that the soul too may be fortified;
the flesh is overshadowed by the imposition of the hand, that the soul too may
be illuminated by the Spirit; the flesh is fed with the body and blood of
Christ, that the soul too may be fattened from God. They cannot therefore be
separated in the reward whom work joins. For even the sacrifices pleasing to
God, I mean the conflicts of the soul, fasts and late and dry foods and the
squalors appendices of this office, the flesh restores from its own
inconvenience. Virginity too and widowhood and modest concealment of marriage
in secret and the one knowledge of it are burned to God from the goods of the
flesh. Come now, what do you think of it, when for the faith of the name,
drawn out into the midst and publicly exposed to hatred, it contends, when it
is wasted in prisons by the foulest exile of light, by want of the world, by
squalor, by stench, by insult of food, not free even of sleep, since even
bound to the very couches and torn by the very straw, when already even in the
light it is mangled by every machine of torments, when finally it is spent in
punishments, having striven to render Christ a return by dying for him, and
indeed often by the same cross, not to speak of through more atrocious devices
of penalties too. Truly that most blessed and most glorious flesh which can
bring forth before Christ the Lord for so great a debt, so that it owes him
this only, that it has ceased to owe him, the more bound because set free.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Therefore to weave it again, that which God built with his own hands according
to the image of God, which he animated from his own breath according to the
likeness of his own liveliness, which he set over the inhabiting, the fruit,
the rule of his whole working, which he clothed from his sacraments and
disciplines, whose purities he loves, whose chastisements he approves, whose
passions he prices to himself, shall this not rise, so often God's? Far be it,
far be it that God should destine to eternal destruction the work of his
hands, the care of his talent, the sheath of his breath, the queen of his
working, the heir of his liberality, the priest of his religion, the soldier
of his testimony, the sister of his Christ. We know a good God: the only, the
best we learn from his Christ; who, commanding love after his own toward the
neighbor, will himself also do what he has commanded. He will love the flesh
in so many ways nearest to himself, even if weak, but Power is perfected in
weakness, even if feeble, but They do not desire a physician except those who
are ill, even if unhonorable, but To the more unhonorable we give greater
honor, even if lost, but I, he says, have come, that I may save what has
perished, even if sinful, but I prefer, he says, the salvation of the sinner
to his death, even if condemned, but I, he says, will strike and I will heal.
Why do you cast up to the flesh those things which wait for God, which hope in
God, which are honored by him, which he helps? I would dare to say, if these
things had not happened to the flesh, kindness, grace, mercy, every beneficent
force of God would have been empty.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

You hold the scriptures by which the flesh is darkened; hold also those by
which it is illustrated. You read when it is pressed down; drive your eyes
also then when it is revealed. All flesh is grass. Isaiah did not pronounce
this only, but also, All flesh shall see the salvation of God. He is noted in
Genesis, God saying, My spirit shall not remain in these men, because they are
flesh. But he is also heard through Joel: I will pour out of my spirit upon
all flesh. Do not know the apostle either from one style only, by which he
often stings the flesh. For even if he denies that anything of good dwells in
his flesh, even if he affirms that those who are in the flesh cannot please
God, because it lusts against the spirit, and if he sets any other things so
that not the substance of the flesh but its act is dishonored, we shall say
indeed elsewhere that nothing ought to be cast up properly to the flesh except
for the bruising of the soul, which subjects the flesh to itself by ministry.
But meanwhile even in those letters Paul is, when he carries the marks of
Christ in his body, when he forbids our body to be violated as the temple of
God, when he makes our bodies members of Christ, when he warns to lift up and
magnify God in our body. Therefore if the ignominies of the flesh expel its
resurrection, why should not the dignities rather bring it in? Since it is
more congruent to God to bring back into salvation what he has at times
rejected than to give over into perdition what he has also at some time
approved.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Thus far concerning the proclamation of the flesh against its enemies and
nonetheless its most friendly. For no one lives so carnally as those who deny
the resurrection of the flesh. For denying they despise both punishment and
discipline. Of whom also the Paraclete through the prophetess Prisca
luminously: They are fleshes, and they have hated the flesh. Which if so great
an authority fortifies as can patronize it to the merit of salvation, must we
not also review the potency and power and licence of God himself, whether he
is so great who can rebuild and restore the tabernacle of the flesh collapsed
and swallowed and snatched away in whatever ways? Or has he also promulgated
some examples of this right of his in the public of nature, lest any perhaps
still thirst to know God, who is not to be believed by another law than that
he is believed able to do all things? Plainly among the philosophers you have
those who defend this world unborn and unmade, but much better that nearly all
heresies, agreeing that the world is born and made, ascribe the condition to
our God. Therefore be confident that he has brought forth this whole from
nothing, and you know God by trusting that God is so able. For some weaker
also prefer first to believe this of matter. They will rather that the
universe was dedicated by him from matter lying under, according to the
philosophers. Further even if it were so held in truth, yet since he would be
said to have brought forth from the reformation of matter substances far other
and species far other than matter itself had been, I would no less defend that
he had brought them forth from nothing, if he had brought forth those things
which had not been at all. For what does it matter whether something is
brought forth from nothing or from something, provided that what was not comes
to be, when even not to have been is to have been nothing? Thus also to have
been, on the contrary, is to have been not-nothing. Now even if it matters,
yet both applaud me. For whether God has worked all things from nothing, he
will be able also to press out from nothing the flesh brought forth into
nothing: or whether he has modulated other things from matter, he will be able
also to call forth from another the flesh drained away wherever. And certainly
he is fit to remake who has made, by how much more it is to have made than to
have remade, to have given a beginning than to have given back. Thus you may
believe the restitution of the flesh easier than its institution.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Look now also at the very examples of divine power. Day dies into night and is
buried everywhere in darkness. The honor of the world is put to funeral, every
substance is blackened. All things are sordid, silent, stunned; everywhere
there is a justitium, a rest of things. Thus lost light is mourned. And yet
again with its cult, with its dowry, with the sun, the same both whole and
entire revives for the whole world, killing its death, night, tearing up its
burial, the darkness, existing heir to itself, until night too revives with
its own pomp as well. For the rays of the stars also are rekindled, which the
morning kindling had extinguished; the absences of the constellations also are
brought back, which a temporal distinction had taken away; the mirrors of the
moon also are reornamented, which the monthly number had worn down. Winters
and summers revolve, springs and autumns with their forces, manners, fruits.
Indeed even to the earth there is a discipline from heaven to clothe trees
after spoils, to color flowers anew, to set herbs on again, to exhibit the
same seeds which have been consumed, and not to exhibit them before they have
been consumed. A wondrous reason: from a defrauder a preserver; that it may
give back, it intercepts; that it may keep, it destroys; that it may make
whole, it vitiates; that it may even enlarge, it first boils down. Since it
restores things richer and more cultivated than it has exterminated; truly
with destruction as interest, and injury as usury, and damage as gain. Let me
have said it once, the whole condition is recurrent. Whatever you have met,
has been: whatever you have lost, nothing is not again. All things return into
status when they have withdrawn; all things begin when they have ceased;
therefore they are finished, that they may come to be. Nothing perishes except
into salvation. This whole revolving order of things therefore is a witnessing
of the resurrection of the dead. God prescribed it by works before letters,
preached it by forces before voices. He sent nature ahead for you as a
teacher, about to send prophecy too under, so that you may more easily believe
prophecy as a disciple of nature, so that you may at once admit, when you have
heard what you have already seen everywhere, nor doubt that God is also the
resuscitator of flesh, whom you know as the restorer of all things. And
certainly if all things rise again for man, for whom they have been procured,
and further not for man unless also for the flesh, what sort of thing is it
that it itself should perish wholly, for the sake of which and for whom
nothing perishes?

Such meanwhile are the lineaments of the divine forces, God having worked no
less by parables than spoken; let us come also to his very edicts and decrees,
by which we now especially order this division of the matter. For we have been
started from the authority of the flesh, whether it is such that, collapsed,

the cause, whether there is some so worthy as to vindicate the resurrection of
the flesh as necessary and certainly in every way due to reason, because it
remains to say, even if the flesh is capable of being restored, even if the
divinity is fit for restoring, yet a cause of restitution will have to
preside. Receive therefore the cause too, you who learn with God one as good
as also just, good from his own, just from ours. For unless man had failed, he
would have known God only as good from the property of nature. But now he also
suffers him as just from the necessity of a cause, yet even in this itself
good, while also just. For by helping the good and punishing the bad,
exhibiting justice, he bestows both sentences on the good, hence vindicating
this, thence remunerating that. But with Marcion you will learn more fully
whether this is the whole of God. Meanwhile such is ours, deservedly judge,
because lord, deservedly lord, because author, deservedly author, because God.
Hence also that I-know-not-who of the heretics is deservedly not judge, for he
is not lord, deservedly not lord, for he is not author; I already do not know
if he is God, who is neither author, which God is, nor lord, which an author
is. Therefore if it is most congruent to God and Lord and author to destine a
judgment upon man concerning this very thing, whether he has taken care to
acknowledge and observe his Lord and author or not, that judgment too
resurrection will discharge. This will be the whole cause, nay the necessity
of resurrection, most congruent of course to God, the destining of judgment;
concerning whose arrangement you may inspect whether divine censure presides
for judging both human substances, both the soul and the flesh. For what it
will be congruent to judge, this it will also befit to resuscitate. We say
first that the judgment of God is to be believed full and perfect, as now last
and thence perpetual, as so also just, while it is not less in anything, as so
also worthy of God, while in proportion to so great a patience of his it is
full and perfect; and so that the fullness and perfection of judgment consist
only from the presentation of the whole man, that the whole man further
appears from the concretion of both substances, and therefore that he is to be
exhibited in both whom it is necessary to judge as whole, who unless whole has
of course not lived: such therefore as he has lived, such to be judged,
because he is to be judged concerning that which he has lived. For life is the
cause of judgment, to be discharged through as many substances as it has also
functioned through.

Come now, let our adversaries first split the texture of flesh and soul in the
administration of life, so that they may thus dare to split it also in the
remuneration of life. Let them deny the society of works, so that they may
deservedly be able also to deny the wage. Let the flesh not be partner in the

recalled, if it alone departs. But indeed it no more departs alone than it
alone has run that from which it departs, this life I mean. But so far does
the soul not transact life alone, that we do not even take thoughts, though
alone, though not led through to effect by the flesh, away from the college of
the flesh, since indeed what is done in the heart is done by the soul in the
flesh and with the flesh and through the flesh. This species of flesh finally,
the citadel of the soul, the Lord also taxes for the bruising of thoughts: Why
do you think evil in your hearts? and: Who has looked at a woman to lust, has
already committed adultery in his heart. So far even without work and without
effect is thought an act of the flesh. But even if in the brain, or in the mid
distinction of the eyebrows, or wherever it pleases the philosophers, the
principality of the senses has been consecrated, which is called HTEMONIKON,
the whole thinking-place of the soul will be flesh. Never is the soul without
flesh, as long as it is in the flesh. It does nothing not with it, without
which it is not. Inquire still whether thoughts too are administered through
the flesh, which through the flesh are recognized outwardly. Let the soul roll
something: the countenance works the indication, the face is the mirror of all
intentions. Let them deny the society of deeds to that to which they cannot
deny the society of thoughts. And they indeed enumerate the delinquencies of
the flesh: therefore as sinner it will be held to punishment. We however also
oppose the virtues of the flesh: therefore having worked well it will also be
held to a reward. And if it is the soul that acts and drives in all things, it
is the obedience of the flesh. God may not be believed either an unjust judge
or an inert one; unjust, if he bars from rewards the partner of good works,
inert, if he separates from punishments the partner of bad, when human censure
is held so much the more perfect as it also demands the ministers of each
deed, neither sparing nor grudging them, that they may not share the fruit
with the authors either of penalty or of grace.

But when we distribute empire to the soul, obedience to the flesh, it must be
looked out lest they also subvert this by another argumentation, so as to wish
to place the flesh thus in the office of the soul, not as a minister, lest
from this they be compelled to acknowledge it a partner. For they will say
that ministers and partners have a choice of ministering and of partnering and
a power of their own will in both, being men of course themselves too;
therefore they share merits with the authors, to whom they accommodate work of
their own accord: but the flesh, tasting nothing, sensing nothing through
itself, having neither to will nor not to will of its own, appears rather in
the place of a vessel to the soul, as an instrument, not as a ministry. And so
judgment of the soul alone presides, how it has used the vessel of the flesh,
but the vessel itself is not subject to sentence, because neither is a cup
condemned if someone has mixed poison in it, nor is a sword sentenced to the
beasts if someone has worked robbery with it. Already therefore the flesh is
innocent from that part by which bad works will not be reckoned to it, and
nothing hinders it from being saved in the name of innocence. For although
good works are not reckoned to it either, as neither bad, yet it more befits
the divine kindness to set innocents free. For the beneficent owes; but it
belongs to the best even to offer what is not owed. And yet a cup, I do not
say a poisoner's, into which some death has belched, but one infected with the
breath of a harlot or an archigallus or a gladiator or an executioner, I ask
whether you condemn it less than their very kisses? we are also accustomed to
dash one clouded with our own filth, or not mixed to our mind, that we may the
more be angry with the boy. But a sword drunk with robberies, who will not
banish from the whole house, not to say from the chamber, not to say from the
office of his own head, presuming of course that he will dream nothing else
than the mockeries of souls, urging and unsettling the bedfellow of their own
blood? But indeed a cup well conscious to itself, and commended from diligence
of ministry, will also be adorned from the crowns of its drinker or honored
with a sprinkling of flowers. And a sword well bloody from war and a better
homicide will weigh its praise by consecration. It is possible therefore to
fix a sentence even upon vessels and instruments, so that they themselves too
may share in the merits of lords and authors; so that I may have satisfied
this argumentation too, although diversity of things is vacant from the
example. For every vessel or instrument comes into uses from elsewhere, a
material wholly foreign from the substance of man. The flesh however, sown
from the beginning of the womb, conformed, born together with the soul, is
mixed with it even in every operation. For even if it is called a vessel with
the apostle, which he commands to be treated in honor, yet by the same it is
called the outer man; that clay of course which first was cut with the title
of man, not of a cup or a sword or any vessel. For it has been called a vessel
by the name of capacity, by which it takes and contains the soul, but man from
the communion of nature, which presents it not as an instrument in operations
but as a ministry. Thus the ministry too will be held to judgment, even if it
tastes nothing of its own, because it is a portion of that which tastes, not
furniture. This the apostle too knowing, that the flesh does nothing through
itself which is not reckoned to the soul, nonetheless judges the flesh a
sinner, lest by this, that it seems to be driven by the soul, it be believed
freed from judgment. Thus also when he enjoins some works of praise upon the
flesh, Glorify, he says, and lift up God in your body, sure that this effort
too is done by the soul, yet for that reason he commands them also to the
flesh, because he also promises the fruit to it. Otherwise neither would
reproach have fitted toward one alien to the fault nor exhortation toward one
foreign to the glory; for both reproach and exhortation would be vacant toward
the flesh, if the wage too were vacant which is taken in the resurrection.

Some simpler favorer of our opinion will think the flesh is also for this
reason to be presented to judgment, because otherwise the soul cannot take the
passion of torment or of refreshment, as being incorporeal; for this the crowd
thinks. We however both profess here that the soul is corporeal and prove it
in its own volume, having its own kind of substance of solidity, through which
it can both sense something and suffer. For even now that souls are tormented
and cherished with the dead, though naked, though still exiles of the flesh,
the example of Lazarus will prove. I have therefore given the adversary to
say: Therefore that which has its own corpulence will suffice of itself for
the faculty of passion and sense, so that it does not need the presentation of
the flesh. Nay, it will need to that extent, not because it cannot sense
something without the flesh, but because it is necessary that it sense also
with the flesh. For as much as it suffices of itself for acting, so much also
for suffering. For acting however it suffices less of itself. For it has of
itself only to think, to will, to desire, to arrange. For accomplishing
however it waits for the work of the flesh. Thus therefore also for suffering
it demands the society of the flesh, so that it may be able to suffer as fully
through it as it could not fully act without it. And therefore of those things
for which it suffices of itself it meanwhile hangs the sentence, of
concupiscence and thought and will. Further if these were enough for the
fullness of merits, so that deeds also were not required, the soul would
suffice wholly for the perfection of judgment, to be judged concerning those
things for the doing of which it alone had sufficed. But since deeds also are
bound to merits, but deeds are administered through the flesh, already it does
not suffice that the soul be cherished or crucified without the flesh for the
works also of the flesh, even if it has a body, even if it has members, which
likewise do not suffice for it for sensing fully, just as neither for acting
perfectly. Therefore according to whatever way it has acted, according to that
it also suffers with the dead, first tasting judgment, as it first brought in
the admission; yet waiting also for the flesh, so that through it it may also
compensate the deeds to which it committed the thoughts. Finally this will be
the reason of the judgment destined to the last end, so that by the exhibition
of the flesh every divine censure can be perfected. Otherwise that would not
be delayed to the end which even now souls pluck with the dead, if it were
destined for souls alone.

Thus far I would have dealt with prestructions for fortifying the senses of
all the scriptures which promise a relapse of the flesh. For which, since so
many authorities of just patronages procure, I mean the honors of the
substance itself, then the forces of God, then the examples of them, then the
reasons of judgment and its necessities, the scriptures will of course have to
be understood according to the prejudgments of so many authorities, not
according to the talents of the heretics, coming from unbelief alone, because
it is held incredible that a substance withdrawn by destruction be restored,
not because it is either unmeritable for the substance itself, or impossible
for God, or unfit for judgment. Plainly incredible, if it has not even been
preached from divinity; except that even if it had not been preached by God,
it ought of itself to have been presumed, as therefore not preached, because
prejudged by so many authorities. But when it also sounds with divine voices,
so far is it from being understood otherwise than those things desire by which
it is persuaded even without divine voices. Let us see therefore this first,
under what title this hope has been posted. One edict of God, I think, hangs
with all, The resurrection of the dead. Two words, expedited, cut off, wiped
clean. I will meet them themselves, I will discuss them themselves, to what
substance they addict themselves. When I hear that resurrection hangs over
man, I must ask what of him has been allotted to fall; since indeed nothing
will expect to rise except what has first fallen. Who does not know that the
flesh falls through death can also not know it standing through life. Nature
pronounces the sentence of God: You are earth, and into earth you shall go.
And he who has not heard, sees. No death is not a ruin of members. This lot of
the body the Lord also expressed, when clothed with the substance itself,
Destroy, he says, this temple, and I will resuscitate it in three days. For he
showed whose it is to be destroyed, whose to be dashed, whose to lie, whose
also to be raised and resuscitated; although he also carried around a soul
trembling even unto death, but not falling through death; because the
scripture too had said, Of his body. And so far is it the flesh which is
overthrown by death, that thence from falling it is announced a cadaver. The
soul further does not fall even by the word, because it does not collapse by
habit. Nay it is itself which brings ruin upon the body when it has been
breathed out, as it is itself which will raise it from the earth when it has
entered. That cannot fall which will raise when it has entered; that cannot
collapse which dashes when it has gone out. I will say more tightly, the soul
does not even fall into sleep with the body, nor is it even then laid low with
the flesh. For it is stirred in dreams and tossed; but it would rest, if it
lay, and it would lie, if it fell. Thus it does not fall into the truth of
death which does not collapse even into its image. The following word now, Of
the dead, equally inspect to what substance it sits; although in this matter
we admit that mortality is sometimes assigned to the soul by heretics, so
that, if a mortal soul is about to obtain resurrection, it may be a
prejudgment that the flesh no less mortal will also share resurrection. But
now the property of the word is to be vindicated to its own lot. Already
indeed by this very thing, that resurrection is of a falling thing, that is of
the flesh, the same will also be in the name of the dead, because resurrection
is of a falling thing, which is called of the dead. Thus also through Abraham,
father of faith, we learn a man of divine familiarity. For asking a place for
burying Sarah from the sons of Chet, Give me therefore, he says, a possession
of a sepulchre with you, and I will bury my dead, the flesh of course. For he
would not have desired space for burying a soul, even if the soul were
believed mortal, even if it deserved to be called Dead. But if Dead is a body,
of bodies will be the resurrection, when it is said of the dead.

the texture of flesh and soul. He will therefore suffer neither of those

even a little. But if the flesh is a little, then neither the flesh, because
not even a little, nor anything, because not anything. Nay if he shall not
also resuscitate the flesh on the last day, already he will suffer not a
little of man to perish, but for so great a part I would say almost the whole.
Pressing further, This is the will of the Father, that everyone who looks at

through which the Son was looked at, and to the soul, through which he was
believed. Therefore, you will say, the thing will have been promised to those

says, are those who have not seen and believe, when, even if resurrection of
the flesh were denied them, it would certainly have fitted the happier. For

But he also commands that he rather is to be feared who kills body and soul in

immortal by nature, which cannot be killed by men, and mortality is of the

kind of materials it is fitted and varied, which is seen, which is held, which
finally is killed by men. Thus I shall also admit the body of a wall as
nothing other than cement, than stones, than bricks. If anyone brings in some

by man, and it will have been said of that. Likewise if a body of the soul is

which as it shall be killed in gehenna, if it has not more feared to be killed
by God, so also it shall be made alive into eternal life, if it has preferred
rather to be slain by men. Likewise if anyone shall snatch the killing of
flesh and soul in gehenna toward the destruction and end of both substances,
not toward punishment (as of things to be consumed, not as of things to be
punished), let him remember that the fire of gehenna is preached eternal unto
eternal penalty, and thence let him acknowledge the eternity of the killing as
therefore more to be feared than the human as temporal. Then he will also
believe eternal substances whose killing in penalty is eternal. Certainly when
after resurrection the body with the soul is to be killed by God in gehenna,
enough will be established concerning both, both concerning carnal
resurrection and concerning eternal killing. Most absurd otherwise, if the
flesh resuscitated is therefore killed in gehenna, that it may be finished,
which it would also suffer not resuscitated. For this of course it shall be
remade, lest it be that to which not-being has already happened. Supporting us
for the same hope he also subjoins the example of sparrows, that of two the
one does not fall to the earth without the will of God, so that you may
likewise believe that the flesh which has fallen to the earth can also rise
through the will of the same God. For even if this is not permitted to
sparrows, yet we stand before many sparrows, by this, that falling we rise,
whose hairs of the head finally, affirming all numbered, he of course promises
saved. For what reason would have reduced to number those about to perish?
unless because this is, That everything which the Father has given me, I may
not lose anything from it, that is not a hair, as neither an eye nor a tooth.
For the rest whence shall there be weeping and gnashing of teeth, unless from
eyes and from teeth? the body of course having been killed even in gehenna and

then to stand at the right or left, and to eat of the tree of life, are the

after which they will not marry, but having been resuscitated. For they shall

through a change yet of a resuscitated substance. For the rest it would not
even be asked whether we would marry or die or not again, if the restitution
of that substance were not most of all called into doubt which properly
functions both with death and with marriages, that is of the flesh. You have
therefore the Lord confirming against the heretics of the Jews what is also
now denied among the Sadducees of the Christians, a solid resurrection.

has eternal life, and shall not come into judgment, but shall pass from death
to life. And so, setting the word as maker-alive, because the word is spirit

hearing and ruminated by understanding and digested by faith. For a little

the resurrection of the flesh, as if it were not permitted that there be
something which, even if it profits nothing, yet something else can profit it?
The spirit profits; for it makes alive. The flesh profits nothing; for it is
made dead. And so according to us he has rather placed the proposition of
both. For by showing what profits and what does not profit, he equally
illuminated what profits what, the spirit of course as maker-alive of the
flesh made dead. For the hour shall come, he says, when the dead shall hear
the voice of the Son of God, and those who have heard shall live. What dead,
unless the flesh? and what the voice of God, unless the word? and what the
word, unless the spirit, deservedly about to resuscitate the flesh, which he
himself was made, both from death, which he himself suffered, and from the
sepulchre, into which he himself was brought? Finally when he says, Do not
wonder, that the hour shall come, in which all who are in monuments shall hear

are in monuments, unless bodies and flesh, because neither are the monuments
themselves anything other than stalls of corpses, since indeed even the old
men themselves, that is sinners, that is dead through ignorance of God, whom
the heretics argue are to be understood as monuments, are openly preached
about to proceed from monuments into judgment. For the rest how shall
monuments proceed from monuments?

The apostolic instruments also witness resurrection. For the apostles too had

the nations by its very novelty, and an unbelief worthy of so great a thing
began to twist faith with questions, then the apostle too through almost the
whole instrument took care to corroborate the faith of this hope, both showing
that it is and that it has not yet been transacted, and, concerning which

nor is the flesh without the soul man, which after the exile of the soul is
inscribed a corpse. Thus the word man is somehow a clasp of two substances
joined, under which word they cannot be unless cohereing. Further the apostle
prefers the inner man to be understood not so much as the soul as mind and
spirit, that is not the substance itself, but the savor of the substance.
Since indeed writing to the Ephesians that Christ dwells in the inner man he
signified that the Lord is of course to be intimated to the senses. Finally he
added: Through faith, and in your hearts, and in love; setting faith indeed
and love not as substantive of the soul, but as conceptive; but by saying in
hearts, which are substantive of the flesh, he has already also reckoned the
inner man himself to the flesh, whom he has set in the heart. Inspect now how
he has alleged that the outer man indeed is corrupted, but the inner is
renewed day by day. Do not affirm that corruption of the flesh which it would
suffer from the day of death detected into perpetuity, but that which in the
space of this life before death and up to death it will experience by
vexations and pressures, torments and punishments, for the sake of the name.
For the inner man too will have here of course to be renewed through the
supply of the spirit, advancing by faith and discipline day by day, not there,
that is not after resurrection, where we shall not of course have to be
renewed day by day, but once unto the sum. Learn from what follows: For what
is for the present, he says, the temporal and light thing of our pressure
shall through excess unto excess accomplish for us an eternal weight of glory,
not looking at the things which are seen, that is passions, but at those which
are not seen, that is wages. For the things which are seen are temporal, but
those which are not seen are eternal. For he affirms that pressures and
injuries, by which the outer man is corrupted, as light and temporal, are
therefore to be despised, preferring the weight of invisible eternal wages and
of glory in compensation of the labors by suffering which here the flesh is
corrupted. So far that is not the corruption which they ascribe to the outer
man as a perpetual destruction of the flesh for expelling resurrection. Thus
also elsewhere, Since indeed, he says, we suffer together, so that we may also
be glorified together: for I reckon that the passions of this time are not
worthy toward the future glory which is to be revealed in us. Here too he
shows the inconveniences lesser than their rewards. Further if we suffer
together through the flesh, whose it properly is to be corrupted by passions,
of the same also will be what is promised for co-suffering. And so far, about
to ascribe to the flesh the property of pressures, as also above, he says, But
when we had come into Macedonia, our flesh had no remission, then so that he
might also give co-suffering to the soul, In all things, he says, pressed:
outwardly fights, of course warring down the flesh, inwardly fear, of course
afflicting the soul. So far even if the outer man is corrupted, not as losing
resurrection, but as sustaining vexation, he will be understood to be
corrupted from this not without the inner. Thus of both it will also be to be
glorified together, as also to suffer together. According to the colleges of
labors the fellowships of rewards also must of course run down.

rise and by that change about to come over shall take up an angelic habit. Or

they are about to obtain life. But in the dead, you say, this mortal will
already have been swallowed. Not of course in all. For how many will it be
permitted to find even of yesterday, corpses so recent that nothing in them
can seem swallowed? For you of course think swallowed nothing other than
intercepted, than abolished, than snatched from every sense, which has ceased
to appear in every kind. Nor will it be established that even the most ancient

certainly through time, is it by life, is it by the over-garment, is it by the

substance, yet not also by age, they so produce as one, while neither is
prior. It is more right that we be wholly either old or new; for how we can be
the other we do not know. But the apostle plainly notes the old man. For put

to the oldness of some substance. For he does not command that we put down the

works, not bodies, concerning which here too he subjoins, Putting down a lie

But be angry, and do not fail. Let the sun not set upon your anger. Nor give
place to the devil. Let him who was stealing steal no longer, nay rather let
him labor by working with the hands, so that he may have to impart to the
needy. Let every foul word not proceed from your mouth, but that which is best

do not sadden the holy spirit of God, in which you have been sealed unto the

defend that both the oldness of man and the newness pertain to a moral, not to

been old according to the former conversation is the same also said to be
corrupted thus according to the concupiscence of seduction, just as also old
according to the former conversation, not according to the flesh, through
perpetual destruction. For the rest as saved in the flesh as the same, as

Such an apostle you may recognize everywhere, so condemning the works of the
flesh that he may seem to condemn the flesh, but lest anyone so think,
procuring from the supply of other or even coherent senses. For even saying
that those who are in the flesh cannot please God he at once recalls from a

being in the flesh he was showing they were not in the works of the flesh; and
so that those finally cannot please God, not who would be in the flesh, but
who would live carnally, but that those please God who, placed in the flesh,
walk according to the spirit. And again he says the body indeed is dead, but
because of delinquency, as the spirit is life because of justice. But opposing
life to death set in the flesh he has without doubt also promised life there
from justice where he has determined death from delinquency. For the rest he
has opposed life to death in vain, if it is not there where is also that
itself to which he has opposed it, to be shut out of course from the body.
Further if life shuts death out from the body, it cannot accomplish that
unless it penetrates there where is what it shuts out. And why do I more
knottily, when the apostle more absolutely? For if, he says, the spirit of him
who raised Jesus dwells in you, he who raised Jesus from the dead shall also
raise your mortal bodies because of his spirit dwelling in you; so that even
if anyone has presumed the soul a mortal body, since he cannot deny the flesh
with this, he is compelled to acknowledge also a resuscitation of the flesh
according to a communion of the same status. From what follows you may still
learn that the works of the flesh are condemned, not itself. Therefore
brothers, he says, we are debtors not to the flesh unto living: If you have
lived according to the flesh, it will be that you die, but if by the spirit
you have mortified the acts of the flesh, you shall live. Further that I may
answer to each several things, if salvation is promised to those set in the
flesh, yet living according to the spirit, already the flesh is not adverse to
salvation, but the operation of the flesh. But the operation of the flesh
having been shut out, which is the cause of death, the flesh is already shown
saved, lacking a cause of death. For the law, he says, of the spirit of life
in Christ Jesus has manumitted me from the law of delinquency and of death,
certainly which he sent ahead as dwelling in our members. Therefore our
members will not now be held by the law of death, because not of delinquency
either, from which they have been manumitted. For what was invalid of the law,
in which it was weakened through the flesh, God having sent his Son in the
likeness of the flesh of delinquency, and through delinquency condemned
delinquency in the flesh, not the flesh in delinquency; for neither shall the
house be condemned with the inhabitant. For he said that sin dwells in our
body. But delinquency having been condemned the flesh has been set free, as,
it not condemned, it is bound by that law of death and of delinquency. Thus he
also called the sense of the flesh death, then also enmity toward God, but not
the flesh itself. To whom then, you will say, shall the sense of the flesh be
reckoned, if not to the substance itself? Plainly, if you have proved that the
flesh tastes anything of its own. But if without the soul there is sense of
nothing, understand that the sense of the flesh is to be referred to the soul,
sometimes reckoned to the flesh, because it is administered because of the
flesh and through the flesh. And therefore he says delinquency dwells in the
flesh, because the soul too, by which delinquency is led in, is a tenant of
the flesh, made dead indeed, but not in its own name, but in that of
delinquency. For also elsewhere, How, he says, even now as if living in the
world do you pass sentence? not writing to the dead, but to those who ought to
cease to live worldly.

which has been sown, they shall be provoked to the exhibition of the thing
itself. Either let them show the soul sown after death, that is dead, that is
dashed in the ground, scattered, dissolved, which has not been decreed upon it
by God, let them propose its corruption and dishonor, weakness, so that it may
also be of itself to rise into incorruption and into glory and into power. But
indeed in Lazarus, the principal example of resurrection, the flesh lay in
weakness, the flesh almost rotted into dishonor, the flesh meanwhile stank
into corruption, and yet Lazarus the flesh rose, with the soul indeed, but
uncorrupted, which no one had bound with linen bonds, no one had placed in a
sepulchre, no one had already sensed to stink, no one had seen sown for four
days. The whole habit, the whole exit of Lazarus the flesh of all too
experiences today, but the soul of none. In which therefore the pen of the
apostle appears, of which it is established he speaks, that will also be both
an animal body, when it is sown, and spiritual, when it is raised. For that
you may so understand he still stretches out the hand, equally from the
authority of the same scripture weaving again that the first man Adam was made
into a living soul. If Adam is the first man, but flesh is man before the
soul, without doubt the flesh will have been made into a living soul. Further
when it was a body into a soul, it was of course made an animal body. What
would they wish it called than that which it has been made through the soul,
than that which before the soul it was not, than that which after the soul it
will not be, unless when it rises? For the soul having been received it is
again made an animal body, so that it may become spiritual. For that does not
rise unless what it was. Thus from where it befits the flesh to be called an
animal body, from there it in no way befits the soul. For the flesh is a body
before an animal body. For it was made an animal body afterward, having been
animated. But the soul even if a body, yet because it itself is a body not
animated, but rather animating, cannot be called an animal body, nor become
what it makes. For as an accident to another it makes that animal: but not an
accident to another, how shall it make itself animal? As therefore before an
animal body the flesh receiving the soul, so also afterward spiritual putting
on the spirit. The apostle arranging this order deservedly also distinguishes
it in Adam and in Christ, as in the heads of the distinction itself. And when
he calls Christ the last Adam, recognize from here that he has worked with all
the forces of doctrine toward the resurrection of the flesh, not of the soul.
For if the first man Adam too is flesh, not soul, who finally was made into a
living soul, and the last Adam Christ is therefore Adam because man, therefore
man because flesh, not because soul; and so he subjoins, Not first what is
spiritual, but what is animal, afterward what is spiritual, according to both
Adams. Does it seem to you to distinguish an animal body and a spiritual body
in the same flesh, whose distinction he has prestructured in both Adams, that
is in both men? From what substance do Christ and Adam bring forth between
themselves? From flesh of course; though also from soul. But by the name of
flesh both are man; for flesh is first man. From that they could also admit an
order, so that one might be reckoned the first, the other the last man, that
is Adam. For the rest diverse things cannot be arranged into an order, from
substance at least. For from place or time or condition perhaps they can. Here
however from the substance of flesh they have been called first and last, as
also again the first man from earth and the second from heaven; because even
if from heaven according to the spirit, yet man according to the flesh. And so
when an order befits the flesh in both Adams, not the soul, so that the first
man into a living soul, the last into a making-alive spirit have been
distinguished, equally their distinction has prejudged a distinction for the
flesh; so that it has been said of the flesh, Not first what is spiritual, but
what is animal, afterward what is spiritual, and so the same is also to be
understood above: Both what is sown an animal body, and what rises a spiritual
body, because not first what is spiritual, but what is animal, because the
first Adam into a soul, the last Adam into a spirit. The whole concerning man,
the whole concerning flesh, when concerning man. What then shall we say? Does
not the flesh even now have the spirit from faith? so that it is to be asked
how it is said that an animal body is sown. Plainly the flesh has also here
received the spirit, but an earnest, but of the soul not an earnest, but the
fullness. And so also for this reason it has been named an animal body from
the name of the greater substance, in which it is sown, about to be likewise
through the fullness of the spirit further spiritual, in which it is
resuscitated. What wonder, if it has been more called from that from which it
has been stuffed than from that from which it has been sprinkled?

Although therefore by proving that the flesh shall rise we by this itself
prove that no other shall rise than that concerning which it is treated, yet
the several questions and their causes also demand their own encounters,
though already cut from elsewhere. We shall therefore interpret more fully
both the force and the reason of change, which commonly supplies a presumption
of another flesh about to rise, as if to be changed is to cease wholly and to
perish from the former. But change is to be discerned from every argument of
perdition. For change is one thing, perdition another. Further not another, if
the flesh shall be so changed that it perishes. But it shall perish changed,
if it itself has not remained in the change, which shall have been exhibited
in the resurrection. For as it perishes, if it does not rise, so also if it
does indeed rise, but is withdrawn into a change, it equally perishes. For
equally it shall not be, as if it had not risen. And how inept, if it rises
unto this, that it may not be, which could have not risen, nor be, because it
had already begun not to be. Diverse things shall not at all be mixed, change
and perdition, diverse of course in works. This destroys, that changes. How
therefore what has been lost has not been changed, so what has been changed
has not been lost. For to have perished is wholly not to be what it has been;
to have been changed is to be otherwise. Further while it is otherwise, it can
be that itself; for it has to be what does not perish. For it has suffered
change, not perdition. And so far can something both be changed and
nonetheless be itself, that even the whole man in this age is in substance
himself, yet is changed in many ways, both in habit and in corpulence itself
and in health and in condition and in dignity and in age, in study, in
business, in craft, in resources, in seats, in laws, in manners, and yet loses
nothing of man, nor is so made another that he ceases to be the same, nay is
not even made another, but something else. This form of change even divine
documents witness. The hand of Moses is changed, and indeed after the likeness
of a dead one, bloodless and whitened, cold, but also, heat having been
received and color poured back, it is the same flesh and blood. Afterward the
face of the same is also changed with an uncontemplable brightness. But Moses
was likewise he who was not being seen. Thus Stephen too had already put on an
angelic height, but not other knees had fallen to the stoning. The Lord also
in the withdrawal of the mountain had even changed the garments with light,
but had kept the lineaments recognizable to Peter; where also Moses and
Elijah, one in an image of flesh not yet received, the other in the truth of
flesh not yet dead, taught that the same habit of body perseveres even in
glory. From which example instructed Paul too, Who has transfigured, he says,
the body of our humility, conformable to the body of his glory. But if you
also defend transfiguration and conversion into a passing of each substance,
then Saul too converted into another man has gone out from his body, and Satan
himself when he is transfigured into an angel of light loses his quality. I do
not think so. Thus also unto the event of resurrection it shall be permitted
to be changed, converted, reformed with the salvation of the substance.
