Most of those concerned with speeches, men of Greece, are accustomed either,
having chosen the occupations upon the fictions, to practice speaking the
whole time, and to fall short of speaking by as much as they would if they did
nothing of the things that contribute to this, or, having despised these,
themselves to spend their leisure on no more serious matter than these, on
that concerning strife and on disturbing those who come to speech with them,
from which they pain those they meet, but they themselves neither appear
better, I suppose, nor are they able to make those men better. Already one of
the notable and of the ancient sophists prided himself at the beginning of his
writing that he at least would do no such thing nor play the clever, but would
discourse about these two things, both the concord toward one another and the
war toward the barbarians, and he set up a very noble hypothesis of the
speeches, as I at least am judge, and a fitting one. But I no longer see an
occasion to speak about the war toward the barbarians. For since they have all
paid the penalty in a mass, I think it will be a concern for the gods and the
leaders, and, to speak with the gods themselves, I do not think the contest
will be great, seeing that of the whole inhabited world and of what is already
securely held a small part is now standing opposed through folly, and what
remains, concerning the concord toward one another, I will discourse as well
as I am able. For I think that the use of this continuous occupation and
practice concerning speeches belongs especially to such contests. For it is
not so that we may never say anything of the useful that we always practice,
but whoever both is able and dares to say what is needed, this man is the one
who also shows that the practice has not been made in vain nor at random. I
knew this too, then, that it is as it were a prop for many and

a refuge, always to praise those present and in whatever they say, and that
many even of those who run away from other cities speak ill of those to these
in which they happen to be, especially if some difference already exists
between them toward one another. But I would not be able to praise praises
that come about unrestrainedly, unless some use were also present and that the
hearers depart better from what is possible, nor, in as much as one must also
gratify the speech with justice, would I court the city before the eyes, and
flee from deeming the others worthy of what is reasonable, nor in this very
first thing would I set the cities apart, as the many and paltry do, but I
will praise all as far as it is reasonable for each, and I will begin these
speeches from this. For I think that for one wishing to effect a common
friendship for the cities toward one another it is not fitting to speak well
of some and ill of others, but to mention all with fair speech, so that first
all, having become more pleased, may receive the counsel more eagerly, and
then already something of the things that contribute to concord may be
accomplished. For if, being praised toward the common, you receive it and none
of you reckon the fair speeches of the others as a dishonor to himself, but
each of you rejoice in one another's as in your own, first, having begun from
the hearth, you will display the concord, and then you yourselves will also
little by little be accustomed both to speak well of one another and to think
the things that are advantageous to you all in common. But to have come as one
who will counsel concord, and at the same time to shrink from praising you,
seems to me at once a kind of cowardice, and at the same time to be of one who
is destroying the hypothesis, and one of two things, either of one who has
undertaken badly, or of one who does not know in what manner he must go
through concerning it.

First then let us consider concerning the whole constitution, into which all
we who contribute here likewise, greater and lesser, by how much it is
believed to excel the others both among the ruling judges and among the others
almost, so to speak, all. For neither so many cities in all does any other of
all provide, nor indeed

the greatest ones of such quality. And further beauties of lands of every kind
and symmetry of seasons and ways of life of every kind and the whole position,
as it is seated in the navel of the whole empire, crowned by many and mild
nations and races about it from land and sea; and it dwells with the best
order and one could accuse it neither of arrogance nor of too much slavery,
but in a middle form it is governed most beautifully of those under the sun.
And it has come to this of worth that, so great being the whole land which
both the Phasis and the Nile have divided toward the upper place, and this
having been called Asia collectively by Greeks from the beginning, this
portion by the sea now, having taken away the name from the mainland, has made
it its own, so it has prevailed to be instead of all the rest. And besides
these things the reverence and humanity from the leaders and the concession in
all things has made you seem better or equal in honor to the many of those
ruled. So how is it not reasonable that all be proud of what they have, and
all think thus as of their own and common goods, and in no way to be at
faction among themselves, concerning things about which no one of those
outside is at faction that they do not prevail in all? Come then, let me also
go over the cities now competing about the primacy and from whom the greatest
beginning of the strife happens, not of themselves, as I at least would say,
erring more than of the others as many as have been divided about these. For
according as each happens to be attached, he wishes to abuse the others on
behalf of this one as far as he is able. But I myself would neither do this
nor do I think that whoever else does it has sense, but I will walk the whole
contrary way and will adorn the cities as well as I am able, using summaries
of the praises as is reasonable.

And I will begin from the one receiving us now and which provides the
council-chamber for the speeches about these things. That it once ruled those
about this place and that later these very things on which we pride ourselves
had their beginning from there I think I will let go, and what immediately to
the eyes

received votes; in return for which I at least would not accept all the

reasonable, if we also become better in the other things, to be worse about
this city, but not better in the things done, and that the advances and

one, but as much as comes to fair speech and the grace from speeches, not to
assign to that one among the first of cities?

Come then let us also mention the third as it were libation; I mean third in
number, not in rank now; let no one hunting a word drag the speech to the
contrary for me. Greatest then already it is possible to say this very thing
about her, that these two cities being, and so many again being of the things
said about them, something is left also about this one at once to say that is
different and not worse said. And I think that all as many as are within the
pillars of Heracles and the river Phasis, claiming Ephesus as their own, would
think rightly, this by the community of the harbors, this by all the other
receptions. For all are conveyed as to their own fatherland and no one is so
ignorant nor so strongly going against the evident, who would not concede that
the city is both a common treasury of Asia and a refuge of need; nor yet is
anyone so fond of blaming who would find fault with the boundaries of that
city. For it is much always as one goes into the interior, and much upon the
sea itself, and everywhere sufficient to provide all that a city needs, and to
suffice for all ways of life, as many as men are able to live and choose. And

say even the whole contrary of this, that of those wishing to save their own
ambition it is to associate mildly with the others, but of those abusing what

all in succession, but of those who constructed them and from whom we have
received each, some in the same times, others later born. But to counsel well
and to be temperate is of everyone, I suppose, who wishes this to be present
for himself. And just as we judge a house to have been constructed best not
whichever has stones as most beautiful, but whichever is inhabited most with
one mind, so also cities we must think dwell best whichever know how to think
the same. For faction is terrible everywhere and tumultuous and similar to
consumption. For it draws all the powers and having grown on sucks and empties
and does not stop before it has worn out completely, having used the sick
themselves as servants against themselves.

But indeed when this form is also most beautiful of every nation and this the
most firm salvation, it is clear I suppose that cities both three together and
more together of those contributing to the same reasonably hold to this. For
surely what is advantageous in common to all those in the nation, these things
do not have a different account in some of them. For it is not if they claim
the primacy that this makes the faction any better, but here indeed one would
find even the excess. For if what not even one would say belongs even to
chance people [nor to the most paltry], these things those who claim to excel
will think fitting for themselves, and will not guard themselves as far as
they are able, how does the accusation not take an addition, or how is it not
one thing that they wish, another that they construct? For they wish to be
well thought of as best, I suppose, but they would thus appear disposed
equally with the worst. And I think that just as everyone would think generals
ought especially to be better than the soldiers, and at any rate not worse, so
also the cities to which the pre-deliberation is assigned ought not to think
worse than the paltrier and the many. Just as then good order and ornament is
not suitable for the soldiers and dishonorable or useless for generals, but if
the many will present themselves in the ordered, the leaders must first
themselves lead this very thing, since that at least is impossible, when
generals are at faction and disturbed toward one another, for an army to
become of one mind, so also of the cities one must think it especially fitting
to be temperate whichever instead of the common form are for all those in the
race. And I wonder if, when war has come, everyone would think it his gain to
be in danger with as many and most noble allies as possible, but when it is
possible without danger to enjoy what exists, if it will exist with others, he
will reckon it a loss, and will not even consider that, by as much as he
thinks himself worthy of greater things, by so much more it is reasonable also
to counsel that the affairs of the others be good; since to be in front of
those worth nothing is no dignity, but the contest toward the notable is safe
on both counts. For

from Homer's things. For since the poet is most common, it is not worse to use

cities he has adorned different ones in a different way, but all as far as was
possible, nowhere letting go whatever even small thing one of them had to
honor. For light, they say, is the grace of fair speech. So that one must not
even deprive the enemies of such a thing, if they have done some good; that
they belong to some of the gods Pindar, I think, says is an exhortation to
Heracles. So by nature a divine thing is the matter of fair speech, and not
only when we are about to begin the rites does it have its occasion, but also
in most of human affairs. And that those using it as far as is possible would
do what is pleasing to the gods is a token both this voice of the herald and
that of the priests, coming about whenever we ask the gods for the good
things. So when this form of speeches is fitting for the asking of the good
things, it is reasonable I suppose that those honoring it once for all would
think they fare better and save a matter of the better portion. And if again
this is so, how is faction not to be fled also according to this? Since there
is nothing more contrary to fair speech than this evil; for one thing is
opposed to two most beautiful things. For it allows neither to say the best
nor to do them, but it brings blasphemies instead of fair speech, and ill
fortune instead of good fortune. Thus where there is fair speech, there is
also good action, and where faction, neither is saved.

to do this trusting in himself, as he himself at least would not be thought
less in this, but he who makes spite toward others his own gain, apart from
the other vice, makes himself suspect also concerning the things about which
he contends, as having nothing

Thus both for a nation in common and for the cities that stand in front and
for each city by itself friendship toward one another and concord is of great
goods, and the contrary, faction, is by nature a cause of the extreme evils.
For it is not possible, using a difficult mind, to experience easy affairs.
And if because no war is now present someone thinks these things nonsense, he
himself does not know that he is talking nonsense. For it is thus, I think:
whenever war is present, faction ruins the affairs of the war, and when peace
exists it is an enemy to the goods of the peace. For it is by nature always to
harm, and the harm meets the occasions. And most absurd of all to have
greatest thanks to the leaders, because they keep you unaffected by the evils
of war, but yourselves to choose willingly this which is a much greater evil
than war. For faction is so much harder than war, as war is set right from
concord, but peace is destroyed by faction, and of war often there is nothing
disagreeable to enjoy, but of peace nothing useful, if it uses faction. And
indeed faction dissolves peace, but war does faction in a way; so much is war
more choiceworthy than faction; and a beginning, where there is faction, there
is also war even toward themselves, but where there is lawful war, the danger
