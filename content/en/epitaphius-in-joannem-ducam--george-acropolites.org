* Epitaph on John Doukas

Of kyr George Acropolites, an epitaph for the emperor of blessed memory kyr
John Doukas.

1 Alas, alas, even the great emperor has died, alas, alas, the far-famed John
and most mighty, the name fearful and dreadful to enemies, but gracious to us
and longed for. Alas, alas, the most secure tower of the Romans has been
thrown down, more fortified than the walls of Semiramis, in which all things
were fenced round and were kept unassailable against the adversaries. The
cosmic pillar has been shaken and has crashed down, which raised the empire of
the Romans to ethereal height and made it more conspicuous to all. The center
of the circle of the Ausones has vanished, from which lines were sent out
toward cohesion, both holding fast and gathering the things of the saving
circumference, and casting far the enemy goads leaping against us. The famous
and bright sun has been quenched, through whom we escaped stones of stumbling,
through whom there was a sweet illumination for us, through whom we also made
our walking decently as in day, from whom grace-gushing rays flowed,
illuminating all the land of the Romans, under which the thoughts of the
adversaries were also reduced to ashes; whom the empty boasts of shameless
speakers of other tongues could not, as a moon's body running under, diminish
even a little, nor did a cloud's mist, the plots of deceitful opponents,
overshadow.

2 Alas, alas, for I must lead the discourse back to the dirge. Alas for so
great a suffering, healers of such a disaster for us! A tree has fallen, the
high-reaching and broad-leaved, in which the things of the whole were
refreshed being sheltered, neither roasted by solar burnings nor chilled by
the frostings from snows. Come then, O Romans present, do we ourselves truly
still remain in life, the emperor having flown from us, who bore in us the
account of a soul or the place of life? Or do we seem to live, but in truth
have died? For there is also a death among humans not yet appearing, but truly
being and known to those who examine, and by one myth it both makes a stone
from humans and does not hesitate to declare that this one weeps. For a heavy
passion having entered in truly turns a human nature to stone and makes it
insensible in other things, clinging only to the pain and wishing to weep
continually.

3 Is it then so, O Romans, that that most mighty one and of emperors ever the
most magnificent in works has himself also, as one of humans, gone down under
the earth and been wrapped in a cold slab, having exchanged for this the most
brilliant of stones and pearls? The one great in strength, the one unmatched
in understanding, the one tested in many battles and having bound on very many
victories, whose trophies are beyond the sand of the sea and the multitude of
stars not numbered, some by land, some by sea, whom the snorting Italian
feared, whom the warlike Persian shuddered at, whom the blood-loving Paeonian
dreaded and Mysian and Dalmatian cowered, whose spear they have feared even as
far as the mountain of Gadeira, whose blade even those who are champions
against all used to tremble at? Has he then thus become, with the rest, a
waste of Charon and a work of the death-dealing sword? And has even Pluto's
dart vaunted itself over him too? Who would give me a Stentorian voice, who
will widen my mouth and make it more wide-gaping, that I may pour forth a
voice worthy of the suffering and measured to the greatness of the disaster?
Who would lend me a Homeric tongue or the Herodotan muse, that I may come a
little near in discourse to the emperor's exploits, and may equal to the
suffering the lamentation from words? But these things would perhaps be
impossible not only for us who are so low-lying concerning discourses but even
for those very high in thought; but who would furnish me, after Jeremiah, seas
of tears and a heavy-roaring sound of groanings, that I may lament the emperor
fittingly?

4 We ought then, O reverend senate, and all Romans, not with lamentations only
to receive the emperor dead, nor only to pour groanings as a libation or to
tear cheeks or to rend hairs or to wail greatly and tear-stained or to put on
black as image and symbol of the suffering; not thus only then ought we to
have granted him the due rite, but if it were holy even to pour out our souls
together with him as he departed and for those long ago given soul and given
life through him to appear dead with him. For truly the affairs of the Romans
had died long before and been made dead, truly seized by the Italian disease,
from when, having fallen in from an assault upon the queen of cities, which
held the account of a head for all the empire of the Romans, they brought her
over together with their dominion. When then they had enslaved the queen and
taken the lady captive, from there they subjected all her attendant without
trouble. This then has become the common disaster for the Romans by the
infinite judgments of God, into which not even Paul has been able to stoop, he
who was raised to the third heaven and from there taken away into paradise.
Suddenly then the more divine Jerusalem came to desolation, and, to speak the
prophet's word, as a hut in a cucumber-field and as a terebinth that has cast
off its leaves. But God raised up that great emperor, I mean Theodore
Laskaris, who took hold of the ship of the Romans as it was being tossed and
sat upon the tillers and received her steering from God and from his own most
dexterous nature. First then he himself stood against the many and great*** to
thrust back with much, and manfully fought the Aparctias winds and the
boat-sinking Caecias. Not much time, and he too then was taken by the Better,
and the command of the merchant-ship he entrusted to the one dexterous for
these things and skilled and of whom he had taken trial from many. And he,
having received the vessel very smallest, and such as not simply a hollow ship
but not even a dromon or a boat to be reckoned and appear, but a tiny little
craft both truly being and so called, brought it to so great a size of height
and of widening, that not a thirty-oared or forty-oared, the things
much-fabled among the ancients or rather to speak things unable toward
construction, but a thousand-oared or ten-thousand-oared he constructed her.

5 But why do I chatter with metaphors and, troping, weigh down the hearings,
when it is possible to make the affairs clear plainly and to set them before
the eye? This emperor then, the great, having received the hegemony from the
emperor and father-in-law, having embraced a just lot and a fitting allotted
portion but not having seized a plunder, which has happened to many others,
and a hegemony so great as not even sufficient to be far-famed as a beginning,
let alone the renowned empire of the Romans, which in breadth and in greatness
differed much from the others, for this was squeezed by small and
easily-circumscribed boundaries and was circled by weaker fences and these
toward the east only, since the Italian was reaping the greater part of it.
Having received such a hegemony and having reckoned with himself that the
imperial dignity does not become for the well-minded a matter of luxury nor an
account of delicacy nor a provocation of slackness, but a starting-point of
long contests and a cause of more conspicuous works, of wars and of the
following trophies, which God awards to those who fight unhesitatingly upon
just affairs, having thought such things then and having raised his spirit to
height and having swollen his reasoning, he works nothing small and lowly nor
ignoble and of the things not very paradoxical, but something youthful and
noble and not even turned over into a bare thought by those of that time.

6 For the Italians being unconquerable even by nature, and from what they had
worked against us puffed up far and having become more warlike, and reaping as
we said the greater part of the east, for Scamander was subjected to them and
Adramyttion also yielded, and their stations were pitched as far as Pergamon,
and the lands of Pegae and of the Lentianoi paid tribute, and outside these
not even Lopadion remained, and if someone going a little beyond from there
even saw Nicomedia and all the country around assigned to them as subject, the
eastern things being thus disposed then, and all the western things enslaved
and reduced to slavery by themselves and by the Bulgarians and by the Romans
who had been stung out of the just yoke and had broken off from the servitude,
as all know, such then being the affairs and able thus to furnish cowardice
not only to those having a fraction such as he received of the rule, but even
to those conspicuous in wealth and cities and armies, he suffered nothing
human, he was not contracted, he did not cower, he did not fear the
beastliness of the Italian and his sharpness toward battles just as of a boar
leaping upon a sword, nor did he suppose the cowardice that had seized the
Romans from them as a hindrance, but having despised all and set himself
against all and having thought himself alone a counterweight to the affairs in
place of all, he embraces the battle against the Italians and sends away the
peace as profiting nothing; for with the just, war is better than peace. For
he thought it terrible and a spirit of an ignoble soul to see with eyes the
former rule and belonging to him from of old being farmed by aliens and
reaped, and to reckon it as a domestic garden-plot.

7 From there he joins battle with the Italians, and God shields the just, and
the preludes of that man's courage and nobility are displayed; for the
Italians are defeated utterly and are conquered with a victory such as the sun
seeing has rejoiced. Ah, what solar light the Romans then beheld, what grace
of the day, having thus routed the foreign race and having spoiled all their
baggage and having led up simply all as captives and bound them with the cord
of slavery and been filled with infinite spoils of every kind and filled with
every sort of joy. When these things then happened, both the high-necked one
was falling and the one raising the brows was being slackened from the empty
boast, and the puffing had been thrown down and the snorting appeared both
empty and powerless.

8 From there the things in us were being rekindled and warmed, those being
chilled and made dead, and it was being raised little by little with heights
not to be despised. The Italian then was driven out of the Roman lands and
towns, having undergone in each of these a strong battle, in the peaks of
winters, when it rains much and fields are sprinkled with snowfalls, when
water is frozen very much and by the excess of the cold both farmer and
shepherd are condemned to be wholly idle and the one has been shut in the
dwellings, the other has spent time in caves not even able to gaze at the air,
and again in the middles of the summers, when every human wishes to linger by
springs and the shadings from trees, being cooled by artificial winds from
fans. But that unwearied one, adamantine in the body and unyielding toward the
contrary qualities of the air, and unflinching in the soul toward the
movements and the onsets of the opponents, was softened in nothing toward
these nor did he give up the intensity toward the much of the time and the
heaviness of the toil. Nor was a season marked out for him for the battle,
thinking every season suitable to run risk for his own lot and to contend for
what had been entrusted to him profitably by the Better, but what another
would have thought an end was his beginning, and what another having
accomplished would have reckoned to suffice, he himself supposed as if nothing
had happened. For he had not yet succeeded in setting the trophy in this
battle, and a pretext of another battle and of other contests was being
introduced; and immediately the war-cry sounded and the breastplates gleamed
and the spears bristled and the shields were held forth and the joining of the
battle at once, he fighting in front and running risk before the many. For the
spear of this man anticipated the things of the others, and the horse both
leapt over and flew over those of the others, and the enemy was wounded by his
hands, and again a victory and again a great trophy.

9 Thus the things of his battle being linked one to another and thus the
things of his victory being applauded. What wonder then, if being so great in
courage, and such in prudence, and in both unmatched among all, and so
enduring toward the warlike things, he magnified the boundaries of the Romans
to so great a breadth, of which how great the magnitude and how great the
course, lift your eyes in a circle and see, both every Roman and the
sojourning foreigner. The eastern lot was all subjected to the emperor, having
been freed from the Italian hand, and all the things in a circle of this have
been tamed as never yet. For the Persian loves quiet, he who has battles as a
delight, the one swift to leap on and to spoil and to flee; his bow has been
unstrung and his arrow has been broken for him and his spear has been worn out
together, he having suffered much in various oppositions and having lost many
of his own and having destroyed the more conspicuous. Let my discourse also be
carried over toward the opposite parts, I mean the western. To whom would so
great a blessedness of the Romans and the far-famed thing ever have come into
mind? What mind supposed that such good things would come to be for us? All
the west falls at the emperor's feet and bends the knee and has proclaimed him
master. The Triballian marks for us the things of the west, or rather not he
himself, if indeed he is even registered as a slave and as a servant bows
under the emperor and is compelled as a tributary to give military force. The
Mysian from the other side perhaps describes the boundaries, but not even he
describes them; for in truth this one too is arrayed with those under the
emperor. Small about the west is the thing still gasping which he had not yet
succeeded in subduing, I mean the things about the Peloponnese and the Euripus
and as many as are still tyrannized by the Italians and subjected.

10 Such are that man's contests, such are that man's successes. These things
persuade the Romans to lament, these things force them to groan and to send
forth tears from the eyes in streams, remembering the former disasters and the
things from whose trial one would not so have hoped to escape, and in later
things the brilliant and much-spoken-of exploits, from which the things of
prosperity and this great blessedness have entered in upon us and the lying
upon the tongue of all as being set above the others in enrichments, in good
seasons, in successes, and it has happened that all who are hostile have
feared and been contracted, thinking the Romans to be more courageous than all
humans, well trained under that most noble both soldier and general in the
warlike things and having practiced not to tremble at an opponent, and to
tumble among swords if there should be need, fighting not over their own only
if it should happen, but even reaching after the things of others the more
daringly.

11 Thus it is a debt for Romans to mourn the emperor, thus an inescapable due
to lament their own master. And I too ought, O you present, having long ago
been able to speak as a rhetor and having had the tongue sharpened in
speaking, not with such discourses to receive the emperor nor thus to repay so
many benefactions; I do not mean these common and public good seasons of which
the enjoyment too is common, I mean recoveries of cities and raisings-up or
rebuildings of others, fallings of enemies, and enslavements of some, nor the
freedom and good condition of all Romans, for whom the disease of slavery
among the adversaries is the thing to be reproached, nor the widening of the
race or heightening or however one may wish to call it; I say nothing from
these common things, but already something other and more beloved. For from
that man we had received the discourses and what we now are we had become,
having been reared up to the emperor and by his commands and expenses educated
in the things of letters. I ought then as I said not with such discourses but
with others to dignify him, that is not with dirge-making but with
praise-making, not with wailing voices but with speeches that speak well, nor
to meet him with tear-stained faces, but with bright and glad looks. Thus we
ought to have made the discourses and thus to have repaid him. But these
things the time then took away together with the confusion of the affairs; for
long ago these things bound my tongue, how and for what cause it is not
possible to say; at any rate then it bound it. But now the emperor's departure
looses this badly and tears the bonds unhappily, and I a most unhappy rhetor
working out the recitation before you hearers heavy-mourning and black-clad.

12 But O what am I to suffer? How would I be able to speak out the greatness
of the suffering that has seized the Romans? How would I set the event beside
in discourse? For if when one good soldier has fallen or a general the whole
army laments and bewails the misfortune, how, so great an emperor and such in
virtue having exchanged living, whose infinite victories and exploits against
enemies long books composed by the wise would have been hard to interpret
toward the exactness, how then, such a disaster having met the Romans, will
not every one bewail with boundless wailings and groan something deep and wail
a wailing sound and howl pitiably and tear cheeks with the nails, and pluck
hairs of the head, and sprinkle ash of it, and rend a garment and put on
sackcloth and howl everything mournful? For world-wide disasters and
ecumenical sufferings and wholly common destructions we have drawn into mind
attending to the histories, certain terrible earthquakes that have happened
and collapse of greatest sections of earth and boilings-out of sea and
drownings of humans and again violent burnings and combustions of greatest
towns; for every element wars against the human, both fire and water and the
air, having stood out of the natural boundaries and overflowed the accustomed
order; and inroads of foreign races have happened to others, and the
captive-taking has occurred and enslavements of men, and women with suckling
infants have seen the slavish day; and again downfalls of violent rains have
happened among some, from which the whole is flooded. Terrible then are these
and truly most difficult and more grievous than natural death; for nature
knows this and the thing happening is not new, but those an unordered motion
of elements has played or unstable fortune has dramatized.

13 Many painful things then happen to humans through these, some more broadly,
some more particularly and partially; but nothing so painful comes upon as for
a man who is a leader to be lost, skilled in the rule, fair in all things, the
form, the strength, the courage, the justice, the prudence, and besides these
the nobility-and-goodness and gentleness, which make the ruler hold the
account of a father to those under the hand. But the marks of that man's
courage, of the firm and enduring and standing as the square, of the sharper
spirit or of fire when a season should call and a peak of season presses, and
in the same way also the symbols of the prudence, which has been set above all
the virtues or rather better to say which has given form to the virtues and
has persuaded them to be and to be named virtues, the characters and the
images of all these as is possible for us and as the season gives have been
embroidered before; but since we have also remembered nobility-and-goodness
and gentleness, here of the discourse the tears leap forth for me as I reckon
his sympathy concerning those who have stumbled, and I think you all too will
lament more in these things. For turn them over in mind: has some human died
even upon greatest causes at the emperor's hands? And this would be far-famed
even of one who had accomplished the rule for few years, but of one who has
been distinguished upon these, how much wonderful and extraordinary. For who
has known anyone from that man having broken off the soul violently, I do not
say then upon moderate or even a little greater stumblings, but upon very
greatest matters, upon very malicious and deceitful ones, upon rebellions? And
the last and first of the evils, upon tyrannies themselves, for as soon as he
was caught who had planned a tyranny against him, O justice and virtue! the
who against whom!, and whoever then he was who had contrived this
deceit-stitching, if only he ate bread and drank water and was named among
mortals, as soon as then, as I said, he was caught he has at once been pitied,
either having had a nose cut off or a hand cut away or, the greatest, having
had eyes dug out; the first of this drama these, but many of the
fellow-knowers did not even know the wrath nor undergo the anger, but at once
they were looked on with suspicion and were released. And these things
happened not once, but even twice and many times. And that man thus used to
let go the terrible and murderous accursed ones who had been seen evil about
him, whom he benefited most and to whom the gifts were infinitely-fold, but
God judged the judgments well. For the emperor used to pity, for he knew he
was being sheltered by the Better, but He used to scourge and strike, the one
with incurable diseases and long-timed confinements to bed, and another even
with sudden death, him who all the time had the body healthily, another who
was together with many about battle, having withdrawn him alone and having set
him as a mark of a worthless man with an arrow-shot, and simply to speak He
has sent all utterly to the recess of the earth; thus God knows to vindicate
those who hope upon that man, even if these for a time let them off from the
charges.

14 And the other things of his cheerfulness and meekness, who would boast to
hymn them out? And David the most meek, and may this one be gracious, I think
has been conquered by that man alone. For someone used to sin, and the
maltreatment was as far as words or moderate blows, the more terrible. And he
used to endure even harsher words brought forth from the bold-mouthed,
thinking them as noises dissolving into air, and he used to bear more
unordered actions, having come to know that mercy is counterweighed by God to
those who pity and that the humane is measured back to us for the humane.
These are also that man's symbols of meekness, these the proofs of the gentle
heart, in which God delights, in which all the subject people rejoices.

15 I for my part, O Romans present who have been gathered by the suffering and
have prepared yourselves toward dirges, have been struck by the many and great
goods of the emperor and the abyss of the virtues and the infinite stream of
the exploits. But I am wholly unable toward a circuit-description, and I wish
to speak out something of his masterpiece-works and to weave in something of
the nobility-and-goodness, but there happens to be inexperience even in both,
and in things in which another would have had much to say, in these I myself
suffer the speechless, and the much of the flood causes the failing of water,
as in the narrow-mouthed of vessels if water be brought on in a mass it does
not then find an entrance. Thus also it happens upon some greatest subjects,
which meet the writers with both boundless quantity and monstrous greatness.
Titus the Caesar is wondered at because in a time of drought, all the army
being present, having dismounted from the horse he stretched the hands to
heaven saying "Reverence these, God, which have not poured human blood, and
send rain upon earth," and immediately they say it rained violently upon
themselves. Joseph also of Israel is praised having become grain-giver of
Egypt and having foreseen the season and having traded the season. But the
hands of our emperor I think the divine would have reverenced more strongly.
For a moderate time described the autocracy of Titus, but an age not small
came to know his own strong-rule, in all of which he has not become a cause of
human gore nor has the earth had gore stained from his hands. Perhaps then not
rain only if he wished did he draw down from clouds, but he even split a rock
and waters at once flowed through and he went through sea as through firm* dry
land, the Mosaic armies being fulfilled. And he imitated the grain-giving of
Joseph, except not thus nor upon shameful-gain takings; for for free he used
to grant the greater part to those in need, to whom he also forever used to
lavish the mercy.

16 To all then he used to open humane inward parts, but most to those
paralyzed in the joints and mutilated in the bodies, whose tear would
altogether have become for that man a cleansing bath and a wiping of bodily
filth. What is the wonderful and exceptional of Noah? Is it not the saving of
the ark in a world-wide flood? For this man was there not an ark greater and
more honorable, the whole land of the Romans, having been saved from a cosmic
and all-bearing surge, and having lifted above the hard things according to
multitude the clean animals, the genuine offspring of this land, and not only
so but having brought in together with the clean also multitudes of unclean
and about to bring them out from a drowning of the appearing fleshly, and the
more also about to save them from the intelligible? Do you not see the
infinite multitude of the Scythians? These both from the harm expected from
enemies he himself freed and with the bath of baptism he cleansed from the
soul's defilement; and now they are sheep of Christ, being wolves before,
being led under Christ the first shepherd and arch-shepherd toward a place of
green and of salvation, and dogs too in a season of those being moved barking
against the inheritance of the master and of their own flocks. Are these
things not worthy of wonder? Are they not near those Mosaic signs and wonders,
through which God was glorified? And the things of the Jews, will someone
lament them? Whom, so great a nation and clinging tightly to the ancient
commandments and affecting the shadow and the letter, he so easily and beyond
all hope more quickly transferred toward the grace and the truth, having
persuaded with words the unyielding and having softened with gifts the
hard-minded, having despised not only the yearly tributes being many, but even
having given in addition many-fold gifts.

17 These things if someone wished to hymn out part by part, would ten tongues
have sufficed him, as many as the poet prayed to be rich in? I for my part
think that not even a hundred or more than these being would have spoken well
according to the worth. But these things thus, if someone went toward an
encomium, the hard-to-accomplish, and perhaps even impossible. But for me the
thing put forward is not a discourse of well-speaking, but altogether of
dirges and wailings; for we have not arrived to encomize, but we have come
together to groan with the discourses. Yes then we must both lament and weep
the emperor. Is it then, O multitude of the Romans, lawful for us to weep
everywhere and forever and to have no time of relief from the suffering? Not
to cast a word of joy upon the soul nor to drip some small dew of pleasure
upon the heart, of which there is satiety of all things, as it seems to poetry
and to truth? And if of the sweet things, much more of the painful. And how
would this come to be, someone would say, or how would we appear forgetful of
so great a disaster? If you should be at a loss toward this, I myself bring on
the solution. For I myself will console the suffering for you, I myself will
work the suffering of the disaster lighter, having prepared and mixed for you
a drink of soul-leading toward consolation. There is a drug known to poetry,
which Helen daughter of Tyndareus received as a favor from some Egyptian
woman; "this," he says, "if someone should ever chance to drink, not on that
day would a goad of grief prick his heart, not even if father or mother or
brother lie dead before the eyes." What then such a drug would mean, Homer
would know and those who revel in his poems. I, O Romans, knowing you all thus
swallowed down by the sea of the painful meeting that has come upon you and
baptized by the stream of grief or even swept aside, would myself lead you out
toward refreshment and would mix out for you a drug not able for a day only to
stop those who drink from the heart-pain according to the rhapsody, but even
for all time, but even for whole ages measured alongside the lives of each.

18 Perhaps you seek the drug and turn over in mind what this would be? It is a
discourse. For the rational, discourses are the drugs as the herbs for the
bodies. And this is not new nor unknown to you, even if it should be spoken in
a newer manner. You, O Romans, think the emperor to have died; but he has not
died, but even again remains living. Are you at a loss about the discourse and
how this has come to be you cannot conjecture? Truly our emperor lives. I do
not mean the life inborn with the intellectual soul, according to which all of
us having died do not die, but I mean the life here appearing to the eyes. Or
do you not see the emperor himself when you behold that man's son? Is not this
one here that man himself? Has he not been made like him outright in bodily
characters, in psychic properties? Is he not an ensouled image of that man,
not a sculpted work of the same nature? Did he not rule together with the
father from birth, was he not upon the same tillers together with the
steersman of the cosmic merchant-ship, did he not take hold together of the
reins of the ecumenical chariot and accomplish the contests? He himself then
is that emperor of ours, so that it is not only not necessary to mourn, but
even needful to rejoice, the paradox. For that he arranged the things of the
rule thus and led the things of his own allotted portions to such increase and
became full of good days, and that the unyoking of the soul was not forced,
their harmony having been slackened by natural accounts, and that he handed
over the power and the rule to such a son, fair to look upon, and better to
understand, and simply to speak most fitted to rule in virtue, and that the
affairs happened thus, so that the transfer of the hegemony was almost not
even known to the subjects, how would these things not rather become causes of
pleasure than of grief? For a man who has died upon a son both fair and good
even the word of the people declares not to have died.

19 Rejoice then, tribes of the Romans, rejoice and be glad in the midriffs and
gladden the folds of the heart with sweeter discourses. For we have been
blessed with such an emperor, I will speak the word boldly, as the things of
the Romans would never have been fortunate in. Now then if ever there is hope
for the Romans, that the things of the good season be increased and the things
of those who gladden be established; now a world-wide gladness will come to
be, a deep peace having come to dwell in the land, peace from foreigners,
peace even from natives, all living mildly and being content with their own;
one will not wrong the other, he will not make a plunder of the neighbor; for
each will rest under his pine, to speak the prophecy, he will not have
judgment stolen or dragged aside by a gold-piece that weights the
even-balanced yoke of justice. For this man knows to bring forth unbribed
words in judgments, he will become a plumbline of straight-justice as an
undeviating canon of good law, not being bent back by injustice. A contractor
will not harvest farmers, writings will not empty the houses of the poor; for
this man knows other writings, through which what is under the hand will be

foolish, but to the most reasonable, nor to the more beastlike, but to the
most humane; for a plunderer and destroyer of a rule will not lay hand on, a
human having as food the harm of humans and as drink their tears, who
luxuriates in the neighbor's evils and rejoices in plunderings and insults
nature abusing the season, but a reverend man both most reasonable and most
gentle and having the regard of God, to speak the poetry, will be preferred,
the one adorned with understanding, the one thick with education, and the one
holding to the just and the true.

20 For not from horsemanship or archery or from sending a dart well-aimed or
brandishing a spear well-natured or holding forth a shield dexterously nor
from certain swifter movements of the small being worthy has the emperor just
now set out, which things have long been thought by him as in the part of
games, even if they have been worked out to the peak, but from discourses and
books and philosophy itself, but from learnings and from the topmost knowledge
of the things that are. There is then every necessity that the thing
much-spoken come to be. For one of the more ancient skilled men also said that
then the cities will cease from the evils and the good things will walk about
in them, when emperors will philosophize or the philosophers will be emperors.
And in this man both have happened; for being emperor he has philosophized to
the peak and being about to become a philosopher he has reigned more strongly.
Do you not see this man more conspicuous than Homeric Agamemnon, whom that one
introduces like in eyes and head to Zeus who delights in thunder, in waist to
Ares, in chest to Poseidon? Nor would I say him to be like a kingly man, but
according to the Pythia I am in doubt whether to name him god or human; but it
conquers rather that he both is and is called a god. This man from philosophy
has been shown godlike just as from race an emperor, and from nature strong,
and from virtue courageous, and from the affairs all-best.

21 If then we should wish to weep the emperor as being a pillar for the Romans
raising their spirits to height, behold another pillar for us, and perhaps
even a pillar of fire leading the new Israel into a land of promise, into a
land flowing with springs of milk and honey, from which we were badly cast
out; if as a tower guarding all within and saving them without danger, behold
a tower both more fortified and more secure. For grant me to speak the true;
for a father will not be vexed hearing the better things of the son in names,
whom he well came to know as greater in the affairs; for it is a long time
since the father used to declare this one better than all humans and than
himself. The emperor's declaration then persuades me and the clearness of the
affairs to bear such witness concerning the emperor; since then you have the
word firm because you do not even doubt the prediction, let my sequence hold
to the former things. If then we should seek the one who has passed as a tree
covering all around, as from the painful things shading those under him,
behold another tree more conspicuous by as much as it also is leafy with
greener leaves and heavy with fruits and being arranged toward a coming
fruit-giving; in this one's shadows we shall rest. If then we should long for
that man as a center holding and bearing the things in a circle, behold for us
a clearer center, able to lead round greater circles upon circles and others
upon others according to the infinite. If we had supposed that man as a cosmic
sun and had come to know ourselves illuminated under him, already also another
sun both brighter and purer, having with the rays of knowledge to shine round
not the land of the Romans only but even all the ends of the inhabited world.
If we lament the one departing for the sympathetic, let us wonder at the one
remaining for the more sympathetic. If we bewail that man for the moderate and
humble-minded, let us long for this one for the more moderate. Of none then, O
Romans, of the former fair things do we fall short, but rather we have met
with these better and more honorable, having leapt over from prosperity into a
greater prosperity.
