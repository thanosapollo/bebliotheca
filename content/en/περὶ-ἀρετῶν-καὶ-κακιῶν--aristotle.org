Things that are noble are praiseworthy, and things that are shameful are
blameworthy. Of the noble the virtues take the lead, of the shameful the
vices. Praiseworthy too are the causes of the virtues, what attends on the
virtues, what comes of them, and their works; blameworthy are the opposites.
Taking the soul as threefold after Plato, the virtue of the reasoning part is
prudence, of the spirited part gentleness and courage, of the appetitive part
temperance and self-control, and of the whole soul justice, liberality, and
greatness of soul. Vice of the reasoning part is folly, of the spirited part
irascibility and cowardice, of the appetitive part intemperance and
incontinence, and of the whole soul injustice, illiberality, and smallness of
soul.

Prudence is a virtue of the reasoning part, that prepares what tends toward
happiness. Gentleness is a virtue of the spirited part, by which people become
hard to move by anger. Courage is a virtue of the spirited part, by which they
are hard to dismay by fears about death. Temperance is a virtue of the
appetitive part, by which they become without appetite for the enjoyments of
base pleasures. Self-control is a virtue of the appetitive part, by which they
hold down with reason the appetite that rushes toward base pleasures. Justice
is a virtue of soul that distributes what is according to desert. Liberality
is a virtue of soul that spends readily on what is noble. Greatness of soul is
a virtue of soul by which one can bear good fortune and ill fortune, honor and
dishonor.

Folly is a vice of the reasoning part, a cause of living badly. Irascibility
is a vice of the spirited part, by which people become easily moved to anger.
Cowardice is a vice of the spirited part, by which they are struck with panic
by fears, and most of all those about death. Intemperance is a vice of the
appetitive part, by which they choose the base pleasures. As for incontinence,
there is nothing; but you can define it thus. Incontinence is a vice of the
appetitive part, by which they let appetite, thrusting them toward the
enjoyments of base pleasures, be carried away by unreason. Injustice is a vice
of soul by which people become grasping beyond desert. Illiberality is a vice
of soul by which they reach after gain from every quarter. Smallness of soul
is a vice of soul by which they are unable to bear good fortune and ill
fortune, honor and dishonor.

Of prudence it is to deliberate, to judge goods and evils and all things in
life that are to be chosen or avoided, to use well all the goods one has, to
keep company rightly, to see the right moments, to use both word and deed with
quick wit, and to have experience of all that is useful. Memory, experience,
and quickness of mind each either come from prudence or attend on prudence; or
some of them are as it were joint-causes of prudence, such as experience and
memory, and others as it were parts of it, such as good counsel and quickness
of mind. Of gentleness it is to be able to bear accusations and slights in
measure, not to rush quickly to punishments, and not to be easily moved to
angers, but unbitter in character and uncontentious, having quiet and
steadiness in the soul. Of courage it is to be hard to dismay by fears about
death, to be of good heart in terrors, to be bold toward dangers, to choose
rather to die well than to be saved shamefully, and to be a cause of victory.
Further, of courage it is also to toil and to endure and to choose to play the
man. Attending on courage are also boldness, high spirit, confidence, and
daring, and further love of toil and endurance. Of temperance it is not to
admire the enjoyments of bodily pleasures, to be without appetite for every
enjoyment of shameful pleasure, to fear even a just license, and to be intent
upon one's life alike in small things and in great. Attending on temperance
are good order, seemliness, shame, and caution. Of self-control it is to be
able to hold down with reason the appetite that rushes toward base enjoyments
and pleasures, to endure, and to be patient of natural want and pain. Of
justice it is to be distributive of what is according to desert, to preserve
ancestral customs and the lawful usages, to preserve the written laws, to
speak truth in what is at stake, and to keep agreements. First among justices
is that toward the gods, then toward daimons, then toward country and parents,
then toward the departed; among which is piety, being either a part of justice
or attendant on it. Following justice are also holiness, truth,
trustworthiness, and hatred of wickedness. Of liberality it is to be ready to
give money toward praiseworthy things, and lavish in spending on what is
needed, to be helpful in a difference, and not to take from where one ought
not. The liberal man is also clean in dress and in dwelling, a furnisher of
things superfluous and noble and that make for a pleasant way of life without
profit, and a keeper of animals that have something peculiar or wonderful.
Following liberality of character are pliancy, tractableness, humanity, and
being compassionate, fond of friends, fond of guests, and fond of the noble.
Of greatness of soul it is to bear well both good fortune and ill fortune,
honor and dishonor, and to admire neither luxury nor attendance nor power nor
victories in the games, but to have a certain depth and greatness of soul. The
great-souled man is neither one who sets much store by living nor one who is
fond of life. He is simple in character and noble, able to be wronged, and not
vengeful. Following greatness of soul are simplicity and truth.

Of folly it is to judge affairs badly, to deliberate badly, to keep company
badly, to use present goods badly, and to hold false opinions about the noble
and good things that belong to life. Attending on folly are inexperience,
ignorance, lack of control, clumsiness, and forgetfulness. Of irascibility
there are three kinds: quick temper, bitterness, sullenness. Of the irascible
man it is not to be able to bear even small slights or diminutions, to be
punitive and vengeful, and easily moved to anger even by a chance deed or
word. Following irascibility are a character easily provoked and changeable,
bitter speech, being pained at small things, and suffering these things
quickly and for a short time. Of cowardice it is to be easily moved by chance
fears, and most of all those about death and bodily maimings, and to suppose
it better to be saved anyhow than to die well. Following cowardice are
softness, unmanliness, lack of toil, and clinging to life. Under it there is
also a certain caution and an uncontentious character. Of intemperance it is
to choose the enjoyments of harmful and shameful pleasures, to suppose that
those who are in such pleasures are happiest, to be fond of laughter, fond of
jeering, and fond of wit, and to be unscrupulous in words and in deeds.
Following intemperance are disorder, shamelessness, unseemliness, luxury,
laziness, neglect, slightness, and slackness. Of incontinence it is to choose
the enjoyments of pleasures when reason forbids, and, while supposing it
better not to share in them, to share in them none the less, and to think one
ought to do both the noble and the advantageous, yet to stand off from them
because of the pleasures. Following incontinence are softness and regret and
most of the same things that follow intemperance.

Of injustice there are three kinds: impiety, greed, and insolence. Impiety is
offense concerning gods and daimons, or also concerning the departed, and
concerning parents and country; greed is in contracts, choosing the advantage
contrary to desert; insolence is that by which they procure pleasures for
themselves by bringing others into disgrace, whence Evenus says of it /

which, though it gains nothing, still does wrong.

/ Of injustice it is to transgress ancestral customs and the lawful usages, to
disobey the laws and the rulers, to lie, to swear falsely, to break agreements
and pledges. Following injustice are slander, boastfulness, pretended
humanity, malice, and knavery. Of illiberality there are three kinds: sordid
gain, stinginess, and niggardliness. Sordid gain is that by which they seek to
profit from every quarter, and set gain above shame; stinginess is that by
which they will not spend money on what is needed; niggardliness is
that by which they do spend, but little and badly, and are harmed the more by
not making the difference at the right time. Of illiberality it is to set most
store by money, and to count as no disgrace whatever makes for gain, a
hired and slavish and squalid life, alien to love of honor and to freedom.
Following illiberality are pettiness, sullenness and smallness of soul,
lowliness, lack of measure, ignobility, and hatred of men. Of smallness of
soul it is to be able to bear neither honor nor dishonor nor good fortune nor
ill fortune, but when honored to be puffed up, when having a little good
fortune to be lifted out of oneself, to be unable to bear even the least
dishonor, to judge a failure a great ruin and misfortune, and to wail at
everything and take it hard. Further, the small-souled man is such as to call
all slights insolence and dishonor, even those that come through ignorance or
forgetfulness. Following smallness of soul are pettiness, complaining,
despair, and lowliness.

In general, of virtue it is to make the disposition about the soul excellent,
using quiet and ordered motions, agreeing in all its parts; which is why an
excellent disposition of soul seems also to be a pattern of a good
constitution. Of virtue it is also to do good to those who deserve it, to love
the good, and to be neither punitive nor vengeful, but gracious, kindly, and
ready to pardon. Following virtue are goodness, fairness, good sense, and good
hope. Further also such things as being fond of one's house, fond of friends,
fond of companions, fond of guests, fond of mankind, and fond of the noble;
all of which belong to the things that are praised. Of vice the opposites.
