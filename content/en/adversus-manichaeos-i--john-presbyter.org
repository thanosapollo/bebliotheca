FIRST HOMILY OF JOHN OF THE GRAMMARIANS AGAINST THE MANICHAEANS

1. It would have been good, beloved, both to point out to you by discourse the
ascent into the heavens and to describe the way of life which Christ ordained
for his own
disciples, and thus to send you on, as the trainers do the good among the
athletes, to the wrestling-bouts of virtue. For with such teachings the Church
is accustomed to anoint | her own votaries. But since some, filled with the
madness of Manes, attempt to undermine and to shake the nurslings of the
Church, mixing tares with the wheat and sowing among the right doctrines of
the apostles mythologies full of laughter, for the sake of your security I
will take up, as in brief, the war against those men, and tearing out the
thorns by the roots I will show the Church's crop free of impiety.

2. Often indeed the demon who hates the good has burst in against the flock of
Christ, stirring up introducers of false doctrines against piety, such as
Simon was, and Marcion, and as many as of old have appeared imitators of the
wonder-tales and false opinion both of those men and of those after them. But
of those men the all-abominable doctrines have been extinguished, and the
nonsense of the myths, and whatever they uttered toward impiety with sharpened
and God-fighting tongues. But the lawless heresy of the Manichaeans, having
its beginnings from there, still vomits a bitter poison against the living
God, setting the vanity of things that have no existence against those that
subsist substantially; for it arrays matter against the true and only God, and
draws up darkness against the brightness of the light, and says that evil is
equally balanced with the good, evil which is known only in unholy deeds. Come
then, using the panoply of the Word of God, let us pull down the greatest
fortress of the godless doctrines among them, passing over the other idle talk
of their impiety and whatever they fashion for us in myth. For when their
tower has been overthrown, the discernment of the remaining doctrines, or
rather blasphemy, will be easy of access even for the very ignorant.

3. The inventors of all impiety say, then, that matter was without beginning,
living, and ungenerated. To whom one may reply thus in argument: Everything
that has subsistence substantially is either body or incorporeal, or has come
to be from both of these by union. And the intellectual and rational natures
are incorporeal, while bodies are those composed of elements; and from each of
these substances the race of men has its subsistence. The things
of colors are seen or
for besides these, such as bitterness and sweetness and whatever through
is known, belong to quantity or to relation, or are accomplished through
actions; they are by nature without hypostasis in themselves, and without the
substances that receive them they neither were nor will be. But the
substances, that is the underlying things, stand unchanged toward the
contrary; for the incorporeal substance will not become body, nor will the
body be incorporeal. The colors, however, and shapes in them, and whatever is
seen about the substance, both change and are transposed; for they are not by
nature self-subsistent.

4. What then of the things said do they claim matter to be? For if it is
incorporeal, how did it become body, the substances standing unchanged toward
the contrary, as has been said. For the changes about the qualities and
quantities and energies | are accustomed to take place; for one may see him
who is pale from fear becoming red, anger often pouring the blood about the
heart over the outer surface, and a change has taken place, through both
passion and energy, and the quality concerning the color. But if they say that
matter
became body through mixture with God and swallowing, as it is dear to them to
mythologize, the blasphemy will profit them nothing; for a short and true
argument will overturn the falsehood. For how did a coming-together of
opposites, as they themselves say, and of things unmixable, take place without
a craftsman who brings these things to composition? And how did the mixture of
incorporeals make a body? For as the running-together of many irrational
things would not make a rational thing, so neither would the conjunction of
God and of matter, both being incorporeal, produce the nature of a body.

5. But if, changing the argument, they shall say that matter is a body, even
so their clever engine against the Church will be overturned; for every body,
being composite, is alien to things ungenerated, being numbered with things
generated. For of every composition a creator is the cause, and thus the
matter without beginning that is championed among the mad will be shown not
only generated but also having a beginning, having received its being from
another. But if they should say that matter is incorporeal, yet a body in
potency, one must know that the "in potency" is found in the case of things
that admit of it, as water is hot in potency, the child a grammarian in
potency; for it is possible that the water be heated and that the child be a
grammarian. But for an incorporeal to become a body belongs to things
impossible. How then is one to suppose that the matter which according to them
is incorporeal is a body in potency?

6. And let these things be said in brief; for it is not possible to linger
over the demonstrations, another inquiry pressing. For they say that light and
darkness are both ungenerated and co-eternal, existing and living; for the
mystagogues of Manes, not having grasped either as it has by nature, have
fallen toward the pit of destruction, having supposed darkness to be some
power opposed to God. For they say: If there is light, its enemy would be
darkness, not having come to be from it; for none of the contraries has its
constitution from the contrary; darkness therefore will be eternal, there
being no creator. For God, being light and good, is not creator of the
darkness. These things are the offspring of some demon; with these Satan nets
the simpler and trips them in their reasonings, whenever, despising the
teaching of Christ, they heap up for themselves teachers from the crossroads,
itching in the ear, and turn the ear away from the truth, and are turned aside
to the myths, as the divine Apostle says. But you, being a soldier of the
court of Christ, ward off those who approach, striking them down with words.

7. For with one and only voice he will put the adversaries to flight, crying
out such things splendidly: Let someone demonstrate the existence of the
darkness itself, and then he will have a just occasion for the error. But they
would not be able to name a hypostasis of darkness, neither eternally before
the world, nor having later come to subsist. But if they should at all wish to
gaze toward the radiance of the truth, and not to converse as sons of
darkness, they will find that darkness is not | a thing subsisting in itself,
but "6a certain unilluminated air, or a place overshadowed from the
interception of a body"6, or simply to say "6a place deprived of light"6, as
also the great Basil said, who interpreted the creation of the universe with
accuracy according to the prophecy of Moses. Darkness, then, is to be defined
as the unlit state of the air by privation of light, not subsisting as a
substance; that therefore which does not subsist in itself, how do those of
the maddened heresy compel us to deify?

8. For that darkness does not even at all have a peculiar existence will be
shown still more clearly also through the things that now come to be in each
case; for three things must first run together for darkness, that is a shadow,
to supervene: the light, the body, the unlit place, so that thus from
interception, a solid body intervening, the place lying opposite the light may
be overshadowed in the absence of the solar ray. How then have they supposed
that the darkness which follows upon the things created, I mean upon body and
place and light, has precedence over the created things, no argument conceding
that that which comes to be exists before that which makes it? But if, toward
the overturning of the truth, they shall contend that the light also subsists
in the absence of the darkness, as through the equality in each they yield
place to one another and change stations, the argument will refute these men,
making the demonstrations through the things that come to be in each case. For
neither does the light give place to the darkness, as one may see from the
light that comes to be through fire; for by the presence of this the darkness
gives place, even if it should be exceedingly unlit and gloomy; but when light
is present, no sneaking-in will come about for the darkness. But if this too
should be granted, and as they themselves say the argument is true, that light
and darkness yield place to one another, not on this account will the darkness
be ungenerated. For if the sensible light is generated, the opposed darkness
also will be generated; for contrariety is found in generated things only, not
in the ungenerated; and light and darkness are contrary.

9. But if they say they are not inquiring about the darkness that comes to be
in each case, but about that from the beginning and constituted concerning
the light, concerning which it has been written: "And darkness was upon the
abyss," which one would not say had come to be with a body opposing the
radiance from the light (for the light had not yet been created), we say to
them, passing over the things said by the blessed Basil to well-disposed
hearers; for being
contentious they are hostile to our teachers; thus then one must meet them:
Air alone among bodies, being according to nature and not thickened from some
vapor, does not possess a color of its own, which is the proper sensible of
sight; for as taste distinguishes the differences of bitter and sweet and
astringent juices, and smell apprehends odors, and hearing noises and sounds,
and touch every resistance, smooth and rough, so also sight is distinctively
discriminative of colors. Since therefore the air is colorless, as I said,
reasonably it is also invisible by nature, | not only existing without light
(for this you will find in the case of the other bodies that are visible by
nature; for without light none of these either is to be seen), but even when
light comes upon the air itself it is not seen, being colorless, nor does it
have the light mixed through the whole of its own parts, God also having done
this wisely, so that, no color naturally inherent in the air becoming a
hindrance, it may send our sights timelessly toward the things seen through
the mixture of the light through the whole toward it.

10. The naturally colorless of the air, then, and not yet illuminated, Moses
named darkness. But I do not say this, that he named the air not yet
illuminated and colorless darkness; for if we speak thus someone will suppose
that the air has been named darkness and will think that the darkness has
subsistence, since the air subsists, which we have conceded to be darkness. I
do not say this, then, but that the unilluminated and colorless of the air,
which things are non-beings, said according to privation, these he called
darkness. But if the colorless and not-illuminated is privation and happens to
be non-beings, it is clear that the darkness itself also is without hypostasis
in itself. For that which is known through things without hypostasis and not
in itself will much more itself be without hypostasis. We do not, however, say
that that darkness, the one upon the abyss, is by absence of light; for that
which comes to be in the absence of something shows that this first was, then
has departed, and that what has come to be has come to be by the absence of
this. But the light was not yet; for it had not come to be. How then shall I
say the darkness is by absence of the light that is not yet? Rather then, as I
said, the not-yet-illuminated of the air is to be named darkness.

11. But if someone should also search out the nature of light by logical
methods, the darkness will not be contrary to the light, but rather the
darkness is privation. But the light will be shown to be a bodily and
subsisting substance through the reflection that comes to be from it; for
reflections belong to bodies only. For that which is bent makes an angle, and
an angle is magnitude, and magnitude is shown in line and in surface and in
body. But the light is not a line, nor yet a surface; for each of these is
unable to pass through the whole of the solid body. For there is one dimension
in a line, describing length without breadth. And the surface has the two
dimensions contemplated in length and breadth. How then would these pass
through a solid body, the light always doing this and instantaneously, without
temporal extension, being mixed through the whole by a certain sharp impulse
with the fine and rarest nature of the air? It remains, then, that the light
is extended in three dimensions, which is proper to bodies. Moses testifies
that the light is a body. For he enumerated it in the creation of the bodies,
recording as the first voice of God after the constitution of heaven and
earth, "Let there be light."

12. But if Scripture is accustomed to call God light, do not on this account
imagine that the light is incorporeal, since the divine is incorporeal. For
God is named from the brighter and fairest things among us, not being these, |
but having surpassed their substance by much and without measure, as great as
is the difference of the maker toward that which comes to be. According to a
most dim example, then, the divine Scripture is accustomed to name God light.
For since God, being cause of all knowledge, gives himself to the intellectual
substances toward comprehension, both supplying the thinking and being known
to them, as far as is possible, through his own grace and supply, so also the
sensible light, coming upon the eyes as cause of seeing, first itself falls
under the sight; from this the God-inspired teaching named God light in an
example, sketching for those bound in the thickness of the body, through
sensible examples, that which is above sense and thought.

13. And concerning darkness and light let this much be said, lest perhaps, the
discourse going on to infinity, the inquiry of the things next in order escape
us. For they say that the good and the evil exist ungeneratedly, contrary to
one another and in no way able to come together; to whom one may say: You err,
not knowing how to distinguish the nature of things; for the contrariety of
the qualities is known in the generated, not in the ungenerated. For in the
generated, as having received generation from things that are not, one may see
a difference of generation; for the generation of the things that have come to
be is not one, whence reasonably contrariety will be found in the differences,
naturally about the mixture and surface of the body, but by free choice about
the energies of the soul, which we are accustomed to name virtue and vice. But
of ungeneratedness what difference would one ever find, so as to give
contrariety also to the ungenerated? If therefore matter is ungenerated
according to you, who dare to say all things, and God also is ungenerated,
both happen to be good; for I would not go mad according to you so as ever to
predicate contrariety of the ungenerated.

14. And that I may also refute you from the nonsense confessed by you, how is
the worse, which you say is matter, subordinated to the better, who is God
even according to you, if they are equally ungenerated and originating? And
how would matter be evil by nature, if it admits of being ordered by the good?
For if, desiring, it is ordered by God, there is in it some desire of the
good, and that which desires the good would not be unmixedly evil. But if, God
giving it the good things, it is by nature even so to receive the better
things, how is that which is by nature to receive the good things evil by
nature? For you say that matter desires to snatch away something of the good,
but that God destroys it. But if this should be granted, how is it not rather
good-formed for us, as desiring the good? And he who destroys it and does not
order it, how would he not be destructive of the things that are, and not
good? And the contrary of the things dogmatized among us will be found.

15. Further, if the good and the evil are opposed substances, and life, like
some form, makes the underlying | substances a form, they will share with one
another according to life and being, and will not be contrary through all
things, as you say, being deceived; for no opposition is opposed from things
contrary in entireties, in that there are also some things common to the
contraries, as the white, being contrary to the black, has the contrariety
according to the differences alone, the colors of the quality being common to
them. And none of such things has the contrariety about them as prior, but it
is by nature contemplated later and after the common things in the things; and
in the common things, inasmuch as they happen to be common, there is not the
contrariety. And there must be a common color and quality, in order that thus
the white and the black may be. Likewise also after the qualitative, the
disposition, virtue and vice.

16. If then there are two ungenerated principles and these contrary, either
they have the contrariety from entireties, or there are some things common to
them. But nothing admits contrariety from entireties; they therefore also have
common things, the being and the being substances and whatever else is found
common about them. And what makes them contrary is that the one is good, the
other abominable, and the one light, the other darkness. And their being
substances is conceived before their being good or evil. Contrariety therefore
is not according to the common, which is a principle, but according to the
peculiarity. And they are posited to be ungenerated; and nothing is before an
ungenerated; but before the evil and the good there is that very thing which I
said is common to them, the ungenerated; they are not therefore ungenerated
principles, but one ungenerated principle, the very being ungenerated. But if
they should say that the ungenerated is not common to them, we say, then if it
belongs to the one, it does not belong to the remaining. There will then be
ungenerated either the good or the evil; both are impossible, both the good
and the evil.

17. These are the doctrines of Manes; with these as unbreakable he has been
armed against the truth; thus his all-abominable doctrines have been
overturned by the truth. For the disorder of matter toward itself, and
whatever he mythologized about the sweats of matter, saying that from there
the rains burst down upon those on earth, and whatever he further reviled
in these, I think it is not even good to say. Flee then, beloved, those who
have received the putrefaction of Manes, who often, hunting the appearance of
being continent by paleness of body, deceive the simpler by the figure and the
look and the name of Christ, and hiding the wolf within under a sheep's fleece
they leap in and attempt to tear apart the flock of Christ. Flee then, and do
not say farewell to such men: "Evil communications corrupt good manners." But
if anyone among you, formerly defiled with these doctrines, has now been
illumined by the light of the truth, let him keep the doctrines of the
prophets and apostles. For one is the aim of these, spoken from the Holy
Spirit; let him proclaim one creator of the universe, who has made all things
from things that are not; because to him is the glory unto the ages of the
ages. Amen.
