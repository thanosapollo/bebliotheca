ON ECCLESIASTES
Epitome of the exegetical extracts of Procopius, Christian
sophist, from the voice of Gregory of Nyssa and Dionysius of Alexandria,
Origen, Evagrius, Didymus, Nilus [and Olympiodorus]. ηψπο.τ Hypothesis of the
same. ηψπο.1 The proverbial thoughts having trained the mind, the ascent now
takes place to the higher contemplation, still needing greater labors.
Nevertheless according to our power we must give place to the inquiry
concerning these things, obeying the Saviour who said: search the scriptures.
For he himself will supply us the power for this also. Now in all the other
scriptures, both histories and prophecies, the aim looks also toward certain
other things not altogether useful to the church; but the teaching of this
book looks toward the ecclesiastical way of life alone, setting forth the
things by which one may accomplish the life in virtue, lifting the mind above
sense and above the things that seem splendid according to sense, casting in
the desire for the things unattainable by sense. Therefore by distinction the
name Ecclesiastes belongs to the book. And perhaps the word of the inscription
also leads us up to the true Ecclesiastes, who gathers together from all error
the things scattered, the true king, the son of God, to whom Nathanael says:
you are the son of God, you are the king of Israel. If then these words belong
to the king of Israel, and he himself is also son of God, then he himself also
is named Ecclesiastes, and we learn that the force of the present words is
referred to him. 1.1 Words of the Ecclesiastes, son of David, king of Israel
in Jerusalem. {Didymus.} He makes the hearer attentive. For the words are not
of a private man or of anyone who happens along. For when teaching ethical
things he calls them his own proverbs, but when handing down physical things
he no longer says of Solomon but of the Ecclesiastes. For it is not as Solomon
simply and a man, but as wise and a minister of God that he puts these things
forward. At any rate it is his to hear saying: my words have been spoken by
God, just as of apostles and prophets. But also he whom God sent speaks the
words of God. And he speaks as king not perceptibly but intelligibly of
Israel, of the mind that sees through the words spoken by God. And he reigns
not in a bare place, but in Jerusalem, the vision of peace. {Nilus.} Solomon
called himself Ecclesiastes, as assembling all men and binding them into
concord, and handing down the physical contemplation in common. {Dionysius.}
So also Matthew names the Lord son of David. 1.2 Vanity of vanities, said the
Ecclesiastes, all things vanity. {Didymus.} Very good; but as toward God all
things are vanity, and the things seen are vanity of vanities. And David too:
but all things together are vanity, he says, every man living. And if the
living man is vanity, the one not living is vanity of vanities. But also
toward those who have come to understand the church and who marvel at the
contemplation of the things that have come to be, the word says: do not think
that this is the last end, that which is stored up for us in promise. For all
these things are vain before the knowledge of God; for after the knowledge of
the Trinity the words are vain to the age and the world, so that we wander in
vain about this life, spending the time superfluously. For when the end
arrives, none of the things seen stands by us, and we shall groan as having
toiled in vain. Let the church then open her ear; for to her the Ecclesiastes
speaks the words, as many as we lead astray and are led astray about life,
which is vanity. 1.3 What surplus is there for man in


all his toil, in which he toils under the sun? {Dionysius.} For who, having
grown rich from the working of earthly things, being two cubits high became
three cubits? Or being blind recovered sight? Therefore the labors must be
sent above the sun; for there the toils of the virtues pass over. 1.4 A
generation goes and a generation comes, but the earth stands unto the age.
{scholia auctoris nomine carent} But not unto the ages. 1.5 The sun rises and
draws toward its place. {Of Nyssa.} He shows that this great element runs
through everywhere and goes according to its own authority. For even if it
also changed its course, still it draws toward its place. And he says this
wishing to reproach the human race, how it transgressed the law of God, which
it received from the beginning. Therefore also a generation goes and a
generation comes, and like leaves one grows and another falls and withers. And
we would not have suffered this, if we had kept the law of God. 1.4bis But the
earth stands unto the age. {Of Nyssa.} You then, holding to virtue, imitate
the earth standing unto the age, yourself having a short time of the contest.
1.8 All the words are laborious; a man will not be able to speak. {scholia
auctoris nomine carent} The word therefore strikes also those who listen
carelessly. And the eye will not be filled with seeing, and the ear will not
be filled from hearing. {Nilus.} For as the taking in of colors and shapes
does not cramp the power of sight, nor do sounds of voices and words crowd the
hearing; for to both senses the insatiable is naturally present; so also
vanity, once it has overcome the due measure of need as an unattainable bound
of creation, has fanned out the eagerness of desire. =1.11= What is that which
has come to be? The same as that which will come to be; and what is that which
has been made? The same as that which will be made; and there is nothing fresh
under the sun. Who will speak and say: see, this is new; it has already been
in the ages that have come to be before us. There is no memory for the first
things, and indeed for those who will come to be last there will be no memory
of them with those who will come to be at the last. {Of Nyssa.} As if we, from
what he said, were objecting that, if all things are vanity, nothing has come
to be; for the vain is altogether non-existent. What then is that which will
come to be, which remains in being? Meeting this he says: if you understand
that which will come to be, you learn that it has already come to be. For
consider what you will be when purified and having recovered the divine
characters, and you will know that which came to be according to the image of
God and likeness. Therefore the present things have been named vanity, because
that is not in the present things. Then having spoken of soul and body he
added, saying, that [but] the body has been made by the hands of God, which
the resurrection will show at the fitting times. Accordingly then he added:
there is nothing fresh under the sun, so that someone pointing to this should
say: this is new and truly has subsistence. Then he contends further saying:
it has already been in the ages before us. And if forgetfulness of the things
that have come to be has prevailed, it is no wonder; for the things now
existing will also be seized by forgetfulness. For this is what "there is no
memory for the first things" and what follows means; for of the evils that
came upon men after the first well-being, the things that come again at the
last will wipe out the memory. =1.1213= I the Ecclesiastes became king, king
of Israel in Jerusalem. And I gave my heart to seek out and to survey in
wisdom concerning all the things that have come to be under the sun; because
God has given an evil distraction to the sons of men, to be distracted in it.
{Didymus.} For I was not satisfied with human dignities, nor did I incline
toward slackness and luxury, but I was surveying not in such wisdom as the
Greeks have, but in the divine wisdom. And what was I surveying? Concerning
all the things that have come to be under heaven; and what did I find when I
had surveyed? That an evil distraction, and what follows. {Of Nyssa.} A
distraction which he has set after the disobedience, saying: in the sweat of
your face you shall eat your bread. For this is toilsome. And where there is
distraction, there also is a choice of an evil spirit, since of distractions


they are worthy of evil, those who have withdrawn themselves from the
knowledge of God. =1.14= I saw all the works that have been done under the
sun, and behold all things vanity and choice of spirit. {Of Nyssa.} For there
was none that understood or that sought out God, since all turned aside
together they were made useless. Therefore he says: and all things vanity. But
God is not the cause of this, but human choice, which he named spirit, not
being such from the beginning, but from distortion fitted away from the
orderly. =1.1618= I spoke in my heart saying: behold I have been magnified and
have added wisdom above all who were before me in Jerusalem, and my heart saw
much wisdom and knowledge. I knew parables and science, that this too is
choice of spirit; because in the multitude of wisdom is multitude of
knowledge, and he who adds knowledge adds pain. {Dionysius.} I was puffed up
in vain and added wisdom, not that which God gave, but that concerning which
Paul says: the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For Solomon had
been trained in this also beyond the prudence [beyond] of all the ancients. He
shows then the vanity of this, as what follows also makes clear: and my heart
saw many things, wisdom and knowledge, I knew parables and science; but wisdom
and knowledge not the true, but that which according to Paul puffs up. And he
spoke, as it is written, also three thousand parables, but not those in the
Spirit, but such as fit the common way of life of men, for example concerning
animals or drugs. Therefore also mocking he added: I knew that this too is
choice of spirit. And a multitude of knowledge, not of the Holy Spirit, but
what the ruler of this world works, and sends to trip up the souls, to busy
themselves with the measures of heaven, the position of earth, the bounds of
the sea. But he who adds knowledge of these things adds pain. For they search
out the deeper things of these: what is the need of fire going up, and water
down? And having learned that the one as light, the other as heavy, they add
pain: and why not the reverse? 2.1 I said in my heart: come, try as in mirth,
and see in good. And this too is vanity. {Dionysius.} For the sake of trial
and by accident he came from the more solemn and austere life into pleasure.
And he calls mirth what men name so, and in good the things they call goods,
which are not sufficient to give life to the one who has acquired them, which
make vain the one who practices them. 2.2 To laughter I said a going-about,
and to mirth: what does this do? {Dionysius.} Laughter has a double
going-about: both that a going-about begets laughters and does not allow one
to mourn for sins, and that this is carried about, changing times and places
and persons; for it flees those who mourn. And to mirth: what do you do? Why
do you go to those for whom it is not lawful to make merry, to the drunkards
and the greedy and the plunderers? And why as wine? Because wine makes the
heart glad; for it makes men gentle-tempered. And the flesh also makes it glad
when moving in good order and moderately. 2.3 And my heart guided in wisdom
and to lay hold upon mirth, until I know what is the good for the sons of men,
which they shall do under the sun the number of the days of their life.
{Dionysius.} Having been guided, he says, through wisdom I mastered the
pleasures unto mirth; and the aim of my knowledge is to occupy life with
nothing vain, but to find the good, which one who obtains does not miss the
judgment of what is profitable, which is lasting and not temporary, and
extends through the whole life. =2.48= I magnified my work, I built me houses,
I planted me vineyards, I made me gardens and parks, and I planted in them
every fruit-bearing tree; I made me pools of waters to make from them a grove
shooting up trees; I got me men-servants and maid-servants, and home-born
servants were mine, and also possession of cattle and flock became mine much
above all who were before me in Jerusalem; I gathered me also silver and gold,
and the substance of kings and of the countries; I made me male singers and
female singers, and the delights


of the son of man, cupbearers and a cupbearer. {Dionysius.} You see how,
having numbered a multitude of houses and fields and the rest of what he says,
then finding nothing profitable in these things; for neither did the soul
become better from them, nor on this account did he acquire intimacy with God;
of necessity he transfers the word to the true wealth and the firm existence.
Wishing then to show which of the possessions stands for the one who has
acquired it, and remains for him continually and is saved, he added: and also
wisdom stood for me. =2.911= And I was magnified and added beyond all who were
before me in Jerusalem; and also wisdom stood for me. And all that my eyes
asked I did not take away from them, I did not withhold my heart from all
mirth, because my heart was glad in all my toil, and this became my portion
from all my toil. And I looked upon all my works which my hands had done, and
on the toil in which I toiled to do, and behold all things vanity and choice
of spirit, and there is no surplus under the sun. {scholia auctoris nomine
carent} And also wisdom stood for me. For this alone remains, but the other
things flee and stand off, as many as he numbered before. Wisdom then stood
for me, and I through it stood; for those things both fall and make those who
run after them fall. But wishing to set a comparison of wisdom and of the
things supposed by men to be goods, he added these: and all that my eyes asked
I did not take away from them, and what follows, not only blaming these toils
which those who live in luxury in mirth suffer while toiling, but also those
which men endure toiling toward necessity and force, for the sake of daily
food, working the crafts in the sweat of the face. For the toil is much, he
says, but the delight from the toil is temporary, adding nothing useful to the
one delighted. Therefore the gain is nothing; for where there is no surplus,
there is no gain either. Reasonably then the things pursued are vanity and
choice of spirit. And he names the soul spirit; for choice is a certain
movement of mind. And David: into your hands I commit my spirit. And truly
wisdom stood for me, because it has made me know and understand to say that
there is no surplus under the sun. If then we desire the gain of a good, if we
want surplus, if we seek to be incorruptible, let us toil toils that run above
the sun. For in these there is no vanity nor choice of the random spirit
distracted here and there in vain. =2.12= And I looked to see wisdom and
going-about and folly; because who is the man who will come after the counsel
in all the things that it has done? {Gregory.} I marvel at whoever, being
carried irrationally, then taking hold of himself, has turned back to what is
due after the counsel. {Another of Dionysius.} He calls wisdom that from God,
which also stood for him, and going-about and folly the human toils and the
random and mindless delight upon them. Having discerned these and what is the
interval between them and true wisdom, blessing he added: because who is the
man who will come after the counsel? For this counsel teaches us the wisdom
that truly is, and grants release from going-about and folly. =2.13= And I saw
that there is surplus to wisdom over folly, as surplus of light over darkness.
{scholia auctoris nomine carent} He does not say this by comparison; for
opposites are incomparable and destroy one another; but that he knew the one
as to be chosen, the other as to be fled. Such also is "men loved the darkness
rather than the light." For the "rather" belongs to the choice of the one who
loved, not to the comparison of the things. {Another of Nilus.} Having groped
many things, I have scarcely been able to find, having lifted the eye of my
understanding toward the counsel, which Christ brought us from the heavens. As
there is a mean of light and of darkness, this of wisdom, that from God and
concerning God which is pure and peaceable, between also the folly that turns
about the affairs of life,


has been established. For in the light we see all things clearly; and in the
same way, those who have occupied themselves about the folly of life can see
nothing of the divine things nor look through toward the intelligibles, and
what they see they see not rightly but even much overcome; for they think
things not good to be good, and they think those poor according to virtue to
be rich, they consider well-seeming those who are full of all unseemliness
from their deeds. But those who obey the wisdom of God know what is truly good
and what is evil, and which of the things that are are goods; therefore they
also order their own life toward these. =2.14= The eyes of the wise man are in
his head, and the fool goes in darkness [always bending down and having the
ruling part darkened]. {Dionysius.} And yet all we men have the eyes in the
head, according to the position of the body; but he speaks of the eyes of the
understanding. For as the eyes of the pig do not look up to heaven, because
they have been formed by nature to bend toward the belly, so the mind of one
once sweetened by the pleasures is hard to pull away from there because of not
looking upon the commandments of the Lord in all things. And again the head of
the church is Christ. And wise are those who walk in his way, because he said:
I am the way. The wise man must therefore always stretch the eyes of the
understanding toward Christ himself, that he may be lifted up at nothing,
neither at prosperity of an affair, nor grow faint at misfortune, because his
judgments are abysses, which you will know more exactly from what follows. And
I knew that I too, that one event will meet them all. And I said in my heart:
as the event of the fool will meet me, and why have I been wise? And I spoke
superfluously in my heart, because a fool speaks from abundance, and this too
is vanity. Because there is no memory of the wise with the fool unto the age,
inasmuch as already the coming days have forgotten all things; and how will
the wise man die with the fool? {Dionysius and Nilus.} The sequence of the
word through what follows heals those who are small-souled about this life, to
whom something of death is thought hard, and the bodily irregularities, and on
this account they set no gain of the life according to virtue, because there
is no difference in these accidents upon wise man and fool. He speaks then
words of the going-about that has inclined toward folly, whence he also adds:
because a fool speaks from abundance, having called himself a fool, or also
everyone who reasons thus. Condemning then this absurd thought; therefore he
also said it shrinking in the heart, altogether fearing the just condemnation
from those who were going to hear; he solves the objection of the difficulty
upon a reasoning. For "why have I been wise?" was of one doubting and
wavering, whether the expenditure upon wisdom was well or in vain, if indeed
he differs in no advantages from the fool, being subject similarly to him to
the same sufferings in the present age. And on this account, he says, I spoke
superfluously in my heart, having thought there was no mean between wise and
fool, since there is no remembrance of the wise with the fool unto the age.
For the accidents of life are passing, whether they have been painful or not,
concerning which he says: inasmuch as already all things have been forgotten,
since when a little time has run by the things that happen in life are
quenched for men by forgetfulness. And they themselves, to whom these things
have happened, are not remembered similarly, even if they met with life's
accidents in like manner. For they are not remembered from these, but
according as they had wisdom or folly, virtue or vice. For the remembrances of
these for men, because of the requitals upon them, are not quenched. Therefore
he added accordingly: and how will the wise man die with the fool? For the
death of sinners is evil, but the memory of the righteous is with praises, and
the name of the ungodly is quenched. =2.2223= Because it happens to the man in
all his toil and choice of his heart, as he himself toils under the sun;
because all his days are a distraction of pains and wrath


is, and also in the night his heart does not sleep. And this too is vanity.
{scholia auctoris nomine carent} For in truth for those who occupy the soul
upon the distraction of life the life is painful, whipping the heart as with
certain stings by the desires of more things, and the zeal about greed is
grievous, not so much gladdened by what it has as pained by what is lacking,
and the day being spent in toils, and the night sending sleep away from the
eyes by the cares of gain. Vain then is the zeal of the one who looks toward
these things. =2.2425= And there is no good for a man, except what he shall
eat and drink and show to his soul good in his toil. And this too I saw, that
it is from the hand of God; because who shall eat and drink apart from him?
{Dionysius.} That the word is not now about perceptible foods, he himself will
add: it is good to go to the house of mourning rather than to the house of
drinking, and what he added to these now: and he shall show to his soul good
in his toil. And yet perceptible food or drink is not good for the soul; for
the flesh turning toward it wars against the soul and riots against the
spirit. And how is also prodigality of foods and drunkenness not apart from
God? Therefore he speaks of mystical things; for no one will partake of the
spiritual table, unless called by him and having heard wisdom: come and eat.
=2.26= What is for the good man? In his face he has given knowledge and wisdom
and science and mirth, and to the one who sins he has given distraction to add
and to gather, to give to the good before the face of God. Because this too is
vanity and choice of spirit. {Evagrius.} Note that the base man does not
receive wisdom; to which you will join the things in Isaiah toward those wise
in conceit: do not say: yes, I know them. And this is the difference of wisdom
and knowledge: that to have the true things left behind is proper to
knowledge, but also to have them constructed is of wisdom. And we are further
taught that one must first set the habits right. 3.2 A time to bear and a time
to die. {Of Nyssa.} Well in the first places he arranged this necessary pair
in the word, joining death to birth; for death follows of necessity upon
childbirth, in order that, by the things being shown conjoined as by a certain
sting, by the memory of death he may awaken those who go deep into the life
according to the flesh and who love the present course, and may raise them
toward the care of the things to come. For the time came and he was born, the
time will come, in an hour which he does not know, and he will die. And Moses
the great yoked the book of Exodus to that of Genesis. A time to plant and a
time to pluck up what has been planted. {Of the same.} For one must pluck up
the tares of the after-sower and plant the most genuine seeds of the word, cut
out vice and plant virtue. 3.3 A time to kill and a time to heal. {Dionysius.}
To kill the one who sins unforgivably, to heal the one who has a wound that
can wait to receive a drug. A time to pull down and a time to build. {Origen.}
To pull down the wretched building, and to build up also the better. 3.4 A
time to weep and a time to laugh. {Dionysius.} A time to weep, when the time
of the passion, the Lord saying: amen I say to you that you will weep and
lament. And to laugh concerning the resurrection; for your sorrow, he says,
will become joy. A time to mourn and a time to dance. {Dionysius.} When one
considers the death which the transgression of Adam brought on, to mourn; but
to keep festival, when we take into mind the resurrection from the dead which
we expect through the new Adam. 3.5 A time to throw stones and a time to
gather stones. {Of Nilus.} Perhaps by a riddle he names stones the words
thrown unseasonably, whether dogmatic into unworthy hearings, or also various
ones that will be painful to those who hear. If then at some time someone
throws such words readily as stones, either rubbing the fool through untimely
praises into conceit, or


grieving a friend by a seeming jest, or scandalizing one unworthy of the
mystical words, let him at once gather these to himself earnestly, calling
back the words that were thrown off without consideration. 3.6 A time to keep
and a time to cast out. {Dionysius.} A time to keep the scripture in the case
of the unworthy, and to cast it out to the worthy. Or also there was a time
for the legal letter to be kept before the advent, and to be cast out when the
truth blossomed. 3.7 A time to tear and a time to sew. {Origen.} To make peace
between those who fight, which is to sew; and to tear, when we take out the
evil one. And each of the evangelists and apostles, having torn from a legal
or prophetic voice, sewed upon an apostolic and evangelical voice, not sewing
a new thing onto an old; for all things are spiritual. And being split off
from heresy, we are sewn onto the church. A time to be silent and a time to
speak. {Dionysius.} A time to speak, when there are hearers who receive the
word; and to be silent, when those who hear distort the word, as Paul says: a
heretical man after a first and second admonition reject. 3.8 A time to hate
and a time to love. {Of Nyssa.} The good is properly beloved; for you shall
love the Lord your God from your whole heart and strength and power. And one
thing in truth is hateful, the inventor of vice. =3.10=-11 I saw then the
distraction which God has given to the sons of men to be distracted in it. All
the things which he made are good in his time, and also he has given the age
together in their heart, so that man may not find the work which God has done
from beginning even to end. {Dionysius.} And this is true. For no one can
grasp the works of God wholly. And the work of God is the world. Of this no
one can find that from beginning even to end, that is the time ordered for it
and the appointed term upon it. Since God has given ignorance, the whole age,
in our hearts, as someone says: tell me the fewness of my days. Thus toward
our profit the end of this age, that is of the present life, has been unknown.
=3.12=-13 I knew that there is no good in them, except to make merry and to do
good in his life. And also every man who shall eat and drink and see good in
all his toil, this is a gift of God. {scholia auctoris nomine carent} He did
not call good mirth the eating alone and the drunken revel, but that with
doing good in his life. For then truly the enjoyment becomes mirth and a gift
of God, when the soul, delighting in the comforts of the poor, celebrates the
mirth from the requital. =3.14= I knew that all things whatever God has made,
they themselves will be unto the age; upon them there is not to add and from
them there is not to take away, and God has made them that they may fear from
his face. {Didymus.} The word is about the visible creatures. For some are
without succession, and others remain by succession until the end, needing
neither addition nor a taking away of anything as superfluous, even according
to thought. And those who find the word itself as far as possible through
contemplation are struck with the wisdom of the craftsman. For God has cast
into us an innate fear through the good-fitting of these things, as we reckon
the maker. And see that he did not say: that they may fear "God", but: "his
face". For the face of the Lord is upon those who do evil, and the face of God
is primarily the Son, according to "make your face to shine and we shall be
saved." =3.15= That which has come to be already is, and as many things as are
to come to be have already come to be, and God will seek the one who is
pursued. {Didymus.} If anything has come to be, he says, it now is, and as
many things as are going to come to be have already come to be with God. For
the architect of this great fair city has nothing incomplete; for even if he
makes each day, still there is nothing new or fresh under the sun. And God
avenges the one pursued, whether by men or by sin and the demons who work it.
=3.1617= And still I saw under the sun the place of


judgment, there the ungodly, and the place of the righteous, there the godly.
I said in my heart: God will judge the righteous together and the ungodly
together; because there is a time for every matter and upon every work.
{Origen.} He says then that the ungodly is in a place of judgment, but no
longer also the righteous. And it would follow to set against the place of the
righteous the place of the unjust, if not that of the judgment; and the
"together" taken in a place shows that we are not judged one by one, but all
under one time. =3.18= There I said in my heart concerning the speech of the
sons of man, that God will distinguish them, and to show that they themselves
are cattle. {Didymus.} That is: if God is going to judge the sons of men
concerning speech, requiring an account of every idle word, how will not the
godly, having been judged worthy of the sight of the one who judges, stand
with boldness, and the ungodly, having been distinguished and separated from
them, be reckoned cattle, having imitated their irrational course, and not
even coming into judgment, according to David? =3.2021= All things go to one
place; all things have come to be from the dust, and all things return to the
dust; and who has seen the spirit of the sons of man whether it goes up above,
and the spirit of the cattle whether it goes down below into the earth?
{Didymus.} If the wise man says this from his own person, he would be saying
thus: who knows if the cattle-like will be raised, as the nations and the
robber, and men will be humbled, as Israel and the Jews? And he does not say
these things doubting, but blessing the one who knows this, living worthily.
For even if our flesh returns similarly to the earth, nevertheless the spirit
of the cattle is dissolved with the flesh, but that of men goes toward the one
who has given it. He then who knows that he himself breathed it, in every way
lives worthily. =3.22= And I saw that there is no good except what a man shall
be glad in his work, because this is his portion; because who will bring
him to see in what may come to be after him? {Nilus.} A good then, he says, is
not a collection of wealth nor strength of body nor season of beauty, but what
a man is glad in his work, providing him the eternal mirth. And if no one
will bring us to behold the things that come to be after us, how can we manage
our toil concerning the things commanded? For we do not even know what the
coming day will bring forth. 4.1 And I turned and I saw the calumnies that
have come to be under the sun; and behold a tear of the calumniated, and there
is not for them one who comforts, and from the hand of those who calumniate
them strength, and there is not for them one who comforts. {scholia auctoris
nomine carent} I turned, he says, from where, from the mist of life many and
various calumnies come to be; and some through envy, some through greed, some
through love of first place are accustomed to calumniate. And behold, he says,
a tear of the calumniated, and there is not one who comforts them. And yet
there are many who comfort with the word, but with the deeds no one. For who
without shrinking has convicted the calumniator? For we do not share the
contest toward the one calumniated, we do not share his bonds or prison, nor
even ransom him with our money. And from the hand of those who calumniate,
strength, from dignities and birth and wealth, and because there is not the
one who helps the tear abounds for the weak. =4.23= And I praised the dead who
have already died above the living, as many as they themselves live until now;
and better above these two is he who has not yet come to be, who has not seen
the evil work that has been done under the sun. {scholia auctoris nomine
carent} He does not mean this common death, but another, which those who die
are in much praise, as having found the death that renews unto life. He does
not then say "I blessed", but "I praised"; and praise is properly upon the
living. And whom did I praise? Those who have died to sin, those crucified to
the world, those who set the human life as nothing, since they neither
calumniate nor are calumniated. For to the one being judged about a tunic they
let go


also the cloak, they despise glory, their course is for poverty. These are
praiseworthy above those who live to sin. And better, he says, above the two
is whoever has not yet come to be, not meaning the one not yet born; for how
is he good who has not come to be? But since he praised the one who mixed
himself with the affairs of life and turned among those calumniated or those
who calumniated, then holding off from these and having died to the worldly
way of life, above those living in this, by a certain superiority of life he
calls him good and praises and marvels even above that one at the one who has
not come to be at all in the tumults of life. 4.4 And I saw: I, all the toil
and all the manliness of the work, that this is zeal for a man from his other;
and this too is vanity and choice of spirit. {Evagrius.} Vain together with
those who envy are those envied for power or money or false reputation. 4.5 A
fool folded his hands, and ate his flesh. {scholia auctoris nomine carent} And
being filled while living with the evils that grow from his own flesh,
according to the mind of the flesh, and being fed by the fleshly works. 4.6
Better a fullness of a handful of rest than a fullness of two handfuls of toil
and choice of spirit. {Nilus.} Better a little to the righteous than much
wealth of sinners. Or is it not better to rest in few things and not to be
distracted in many and to undergo dangers of maltreatment of spirit? For
nothing so maltreats the spirit also as the cares of wealth, as the apostle
says.
