* Description of Saint Sophia

Paul the Silentiary, Ekphrasis of the Temple of Saint Sophia Iambics to the
emperor Justinian Is it possible to find a greater day than the present one,
in which both God and emperor are honored? It is not possible to say so. We
know Christ as Lord, we know it wholly; for this, mightiest one, you make
known even to barbarians by your words; whence you have him present as
fellow-worker in your deeds, legislating, founding cities, raising temples,
moving arms if need be, and arranging treaties and putting down battles,
whence victory is inborn in your labors as a distinguishing mark; (is not the
ocean a boundary for us toward the west of your power, for those who have run
through the earth? and toward the east have you not already turned some by
battles, and taken others to yourself before they came to this point? and do
you not long since hold every Libyan as a slave?) whence you easily escape
diseases, when no one hoped it; whence fittingly, mightiest one, having always
learned the dangers in the unseen you passed them by, guarded not by spears,
not by shields, but by the very hand of God. I admire you, all-mightiest, for
your courage, I admire you for your judgment and your faith. The ambush was
assembled, and the sword was prepared, and the appointed hour was at hand, and
the conspirators had already passed within the palace, and they were laying
hold of the inner gate, after which they were about to assault your throne.
But knowing these things and having learned them long since you endured and
trusted only in him who shields you, I mean God, by whom you conquer all
things; and you did not miss the mark. For what followed upon these things?
The leader of the ambush fell by his own hand; for Justice did not wish to
save him. And he knew openly from the tyrants who had suffered many times
that, if you should get him in your power while he still lived, you turn at
once to pity, to mercy wholly, conquering even here every nature of man. For
sympathizing with the sins of life you have often groaned over our failings,
best of men; and often with tears you wet your gentle eye in kingly fashion,
grieving over us; and looking especially at incontinence, the housemate of
life, you loose all from the debts of evils, as the divine does, and you run
toward pardon. And you ask yourself, when the greatness of the petitions does
not allow others to begin the petitions; for you never permit your utter mercy
to become another's. And from the unholy daring that we do you have occasions
of frankness above. Does not he who does not wish this emperor to rule arm
himself against God himself, the mild, the sweet, who in measure benefits both
friends and not friends? These things save you; these things make the soul of
the empress, mightiest one, the blessed, the all-best, the fair and all-wise,
to have frankness toward God on your behalf, whom you had living as a pious
fellow-worker, and when she passed away she furnished to the subjects an oath
as helper, an oath most unbreakable, which you did not pass by nor would you
willingly overstep. And these things are these; but to those wishing already
to go toward the temple grant courage. And let this too be among your wonders,
that words appear daring to speak a thing that conquers all wonders by excess.
And a proof of your extreme wonders is this so great love which the whole city
nurses for you, mightiest emperor, and for your temple. For when you were
keeping the feast as was due, the whole people at once, the senate, those who
pursue the middle safe life, asked to extend the days of the feast; you
granted it; they ran out; they asked again; again you granted it. And doing
this many times you extended the feast richly. These things having been spoken
in the palace the hearing was divided, and the rest was spoken in the bishop's
house, before Eutychius the most holy patriarch, the iambics set below having
been spoken first.


We have come to you, men, from the hearth of the emperor to the hearth of the
emperor the all-greatest, the maker of all things, through whom victory is
inborn in the lord. There the protector of the honors, letting himself down
into a theatre of words, granted his mind kindly; but here it is possible to
see the protector of the sacred things; and let him too be kindly. And let no
one hearing these things abuse the words; for someone might reasonably say "my
good man, how very much you talk superfluously. You ask this one to become
kindly to words, the house of good things, him who is clothed in all kindness?
You have suffered just what one would reasonably suffer, if someone should
beg, pressing insistently, that the sun rise in the day, that the light shine,
and that words be words." But I would not declare that this is the manner of
these things; but looking toward the aim, which is all-greatest and has no
excess, I fear the contest. Yet I begin again to take courage from the things
I feared before. For if there were some hope of being equalled by a strong
word to the all-beautiful temple, it was dangerous to strip for a wrestling in
which victory was a matter of risk. But since we all know that a word would
never appear running together with the deeds of the emperor, and of the deeds
the all-greatest is the founding of this temple, we must not remain
daring-less in fear over things already known beforehand, but move all
eagerness, as each has strength. The frankness of a man has something good.
For if the lord had not, boldly and thinking great things, created the temple,
having surpassed every hope of spectacle, the city would not have come forth
into so great good-cheer and blessed luxury. What then is unreasonable, that a
fitting frankness appear now also, and a speaking without penalty? For
whatever they may pass by, having grown weak, it is possible to take these
things in addition with the eyes. And it is not a bean-eating Attic who judges
these, but men both pious and forbearing, in whom both the divine and the
emperor take pleasure, those who array the cities, those who have the reins of
all things both of words and of deeds. Must one then go toward the verses
themselves, having been led out into frankness? One must do this, I myself
will answer back, having appeared as some echo of my own words. Therefore I
return to the great emperor. Description by Paul the Silentiary, son of Cyrus,
of the Great Church. Today the clash of shields does not carry me, nor do I
hasten upon a western or a Libyan victory, nor do I strike a ringing-footed
rhythm about tyrant-slaying trophies; let the unshouted glories of
Mede-slaying works remain today. Peace much-blessed, nurse of cities, whom the
lord embraced more than Victory of the good helmet, come; having boasted in
city-holding contests we will sing with holy hymns a house higher than every
all-glorious contest, under which alone all the glory of a high-roofed
wondrous work has collapsed. But you, bountiful Rome, for me crown the
life-bearing emperor, clothed abundantly in undefiled hymns, not because he
fitted your yoke-bond upon the nations of the earth, not because he stretched
the overweening measures of your thrones to the farthest bounds along the
ocean shores, but because having raised an infinite temple about your arm he
made you more shining than the Tiber that bore you. Yield to me, Capitoline
rumours of Rome, yield; so much has my emperor overleapt that wonder, as much
as the great God is better than an idol. Hence I wish, gold-robed Anthousa, to
sing your sceptre-holder with honey-voiced dances. For the lord not only,
arming his hand in weapons, enslaved the infinite barbarian with hide-piercing
spear, that he might bow an untamed neck to your straps and crouch to the yoke
of your law; but even black Envy himself, shrieking overweening things,
collapsed to the bow of the city-holding emperor, and with successive
missiles, tattered, he crashed, and falling he deepened the dust. And you too,
elder-born Latin Rome, come, singing a fellow-voiced song to newly blooming
Rome; come exulting, because you behold your child rising above the mother,
since this is the grace of parents. Men to whom it is a care to honor
god-revering ordinances, come to me, having cast off misty grief, and
rejoicing clothe yourselves


in snow-white robes at the joints. And having wiped the five-year tear from
the eyes we will sing with well-omened lips swift-footed hymns. The Ausonian
sceptre-holder opened the bolts of the heavenly gates upon the earth, and he
spread wide gladness over all the feast, and he blunted all cares. For since
the mighty work of the lords crashed, there was unceasing mourning through the
city. Be gracious to the tale, be gracious to the daring one, mighty stay of
the earth, be gracious to the words, even if I stir your ear a little only;
for the grace of great works coming on covered the downcast stings with
Lethean streams. And the light of the sun is more shining to the traveller
after a wintry night, and lovely calm warms sea-wandering men more after the
waves. Be gracious to the daring one, mighty one, be gracious to the tale.
Already the wondrous rim of the half-sphere, having stepped upon strong
foundations, had fallen; and it shook all the seats of the mystic hall, and
all the bases of the foundations leapt up in the city, and the earth groaned
for a time, and misty dust mixed with aerial clouds covered the midday gleam
of the heavenly brightness. Blessed Christ, you stretching your hand over your
haunts did not let the evil-working Telchines defile your land with
man-slaying bloods; for you did not endure with the all-seeing cast of your
undefiled eye to look upon blood poured in the precincts of a bloodless
sacrifice. Nor yet did the broad-breasted temple collapse as far as the
foundations, bound with the knots of best-travailing art; but the eastern horn
of one arch slipped away, and a portion of the sphere was mixed with dust. And
the one part was on the floors, and the other still (a wonder to behold) as if
unsupported consorted hanging from the airs. And every man, struck with
dejection, groaned. Let no one strike my siren with a jealous tale as she
walks a path of unremembered grief; sweeter is laughter after a tear, health
after disease. Not so did flame poured from the aether grieve men, when it
burned the pale back of the earth, when countless streams of withering
torrents hissed, nor when the blazing heaven over the fruit-bearing land,
gaping wide, opened the gates of a destructive rain and joined the dry ground
with the surges of the sea. But my sceptre-holder, hearing the abhorrent pain,
did not for long cover the brightness of his mind, nor dejected did he endure
to rest in the knots of idle hesitation, but he shook off the sting of brief
grief, and he rushed toward the labors that rebuild the house. And Rome
standing beside him, shield-brandishing, said: "All-powerful, blessed-fated
seat of Justice, stay of cities, envy has seized me, and there is some grace
of Megaera, that while you live she has attacked the beauty of Rome. A wound
burst all around rises on our breasts; but, blessed one, (for you are able to
strew medicines upon a wound) stretch forth your hand, nurse of flowing
wealth. Directing all things with your steersman's bit I have subjected them
to your trophy-bearing triumphs. The Median lord is still, and the Celtic
war-cry, and the Indian man has granted a friendly sword to your thrones,
bringing elephants and pearls of the sea; Carthage has bowed a slave's knee to
my trophies. Toward me every merchant-ship stretches life-bearing hope,
looking upon the circling course of the twin Bear, that I may offer
overflowing wealth to my children; and the winds blowing upon them bring up
commerce. These things follow upon your toils; but never, sceptre-holder,
allow the wondrous work to fall under the streams of Lethe upon the heap of
all splendour. For, lord, even if the nations of the infinite earth crouch to
you, having been bent to Ausonian ordinances, even if you have built me every
city, you will never find another brighter token of your throne." So speaking
she longed to fix her graceful lips to the imperial feet. And he stretched a
gracious right hand to familiar Rome, raising her as she crouched. And smiling
gently, that he might drive away countless pain, he uttered words full of
carefree gladness: "Cast off grief, queen city, do not stir your spirit; as a
missile did not conquer your shield nor did any other barbarian spear shake
your unmoved mind, do not crouch under heavy-enduring cares. Endure, all-queen
city, do not tear your spirit; for by my toils I will make you more
song-worthy, having raised again the well-rimmed peak of the temple." So


speaking he hastened to the precincts, and soon the work was swifter than the
neighboring word; for rising as is lawful he did not wait for the
fellow-travelling shield-bearer who takes the golden chain at the unbending
neck, nor some golden staff always going before the lords, nor an army
furnished with the manliness of swift-footed youth, fellow-runner of the
well-armed black-booted road. And suddenly from either side they streamed
together from other places, others, the emperor having gone before; and the
ox-hides of men being crowded beat, and a mixed echo rang. But when he had
entered the temple and knew the unshaken base of the house, he turned his
whole purpose to the measured peak, and he praised the art and the mind of
Anthemius furnished with prudent counsel. That man first fixed the foundations
of the temple, serving the counsels of well-toiling emperors, a terrible man
both to take the compass and to mark a figure, who put into the walls so much
strength as to rival the heavy-enduring onsets of a hostile daemon. For when
the high-legged head had been cut off it did not collapse, but it supported an
unshaken foot on a well-founded foundation; and upon the former walls the
director of the great throne among the Ausonians built again the grace of a
blameless head. But who would sing how he revived the temple with high-necked
adornment? Who is sufficient to mark the best-travailing cunning of the
many-sceptred emperor? Those things, O sceptre-holder, that are a care to the
carpenter's art we will leave, and I will come to the ends of your
well-toiling labors, having seen the newly finished reverence, upon which a
divine love of every eye has fluttered its rays. Every mortal having stretched
his eye to the bright-formed heaven has not long endured with unbent neck to
see the circling meadow of the star-robed dance, but has led his eye away also
to a greening hill, and has longed to see the flow of a flowery torrent and
the youthful corn and the shelter of a well-treed wood and skipping flocks and
the twisting olive, the vine leaning upon thriving branches and the grey-eyed
calm over the flashing sea, carded by the sea-wet oars of the sailor. But if
someone plants his foot in god-revering precincts, he does not wish to lead
his foot backward, but with charmed eyes to shake a much-turning neck here and
there; all satiety is driven outside the well-helmeted house. Such an
unblamable temple the ever-watched lord of the immortal God raises by helping
counsel. For by your toils, lord, you draw Christ all-glorious to be gracious
through and through. For you did not wish to set high-legged Ossa upon the
heads of Olympus, nor to drag Pelion over the neck of Ossa, making the aether
climbable under human footsteps; but having accomplished by holy labors a work
beyond hope you have no need of a stepping of mountains, that you might leap
up to heaven, but with the straight-going wings of piety you drive toward the
divine aether. But why do I delay to hymn the day of the care-forgetting
feast? And why do we wind the tale outside the temple? Let us go in the
precincts, and you initiates hymn God, calling him as a suppliant helper of my
tales. Just now the sickle, having blunted its jaw after the clusters, was
awaiting the summer-born work of the coming sheaf, and the sun shaking the
reins beside the wing of the south was driving the unwarming portions toward
the fishy goat, having just left the downcast sender of missiles. And reverend
Dawn came, and the immortal gate of the newly built temple, being opened,
bellowed, calling the people within and the stay. When black night dwindles
and daylight grows for all, so truly, the temple of the great one having
appeared, the night of griefs dwindles and the gleam of joy has run over all.
It was fitting for you, mighty sceptre-holder, it was fitting for Rome, it was
fitting to spread the door of the temple as forerunner of the feast of the
immortal God for your peoples; it was fitting that after that wondrous day in
succession the birthday of life-giving Christ should come. And indeed night
was completed, forerunner of swift-footed dawn, calling gladness, and the
immortal herald of God, unsleeping Plato, received the sound of the dance in
his own divine precincts, where with mystic voice men gladly shouted all-night
hymns of life-supporting Christ with unsilenced songs. But when rose-armed
gleam, having drawn up the shadowy veil, ran under the heavenly rims, then
indeed all the people


was gathered, and every chief of thrones, serving the commands of the strong
emperor and bringing thanksgiving gifts to Christ the King, cried god-revering
hymns with suppliant mouths, lighting silvery wax under well-toiling hands.
And the priest followed, and he led the sacred dance, the much-hymned priest,
whom the Ausonian sceptre-holder found worthy of the temple; and the
broad-streeted path of all Rome was crowded. And having come to the oracular
courts the whole people shouted a thanksgiving, and thought they set their
footsteps upon the undefiled heavenly rims. Open the key for me, god-revering
ones, open, initiates, and open the wondrous palace to our tales, and bring a
prayer to the words; for it is necessary that those touching the starting-line
stretch the eye toward you. Threefold the haunts of eastern half-circles are
spread open; and on high after the upright neck of the walls the fourth
portion of the four-cut sphere creeps up, as over a three-crested head, high
upon the back, a peacock raises a figure with many-eyed wings. These heads
wise men have called conchs by a craftsman's tale; but the exact thing,
whether the skilled call them from a sea-shell or from the art, they
themselves surely know. And the middle one has girt mystic thrones and
circling bases. And the one of them, gathering under the last edge, draws them
together nearer about the centre upon the earth; and as many as rise from on
high widen the interval as far as the silver thrones little by little, and
with increasing circles they wind a horn always stepping upon it. And after it
there succeeds, fitted to strong foundations, a straight-lined one toward the
base and above a circular rim, not like in figures to a sphere, but to a
cylinder cut in two. And it puts forth two other well-columned conchs on both
sides to creep toward the west, as if stretching out bent arms, that it may
embrace the much-hymned people in its own houses. And spotted columns shining
with purple down, with golden heads, lighten them, standing around the
half-cut rim of the circle, lifting an overweening burden, which the
well-legged cliffs of Nilotic Thebes once brought forth. With twin columns
here and there the seats of both arches are raised; and threefold smaller
half-complete arches men skilled have wound under the footsteps of the conch,
under whose edge columns have set heads bound with bronze, carved,
gold-wrought, leading cares astray. And there are other columns upon purple
columns, bright flowers of Thessalian green-eyed stone; there [you] will
perceive the fair upper rooms of women. They bear the whole sch[eme which] it

shine. And it is possible to wonder at the mind of the man who once boldly

stone supports, where women having leaned have rested their work-toiling
