SEVENTEEN CHAPTERS OF JOHN THE GRAMMARIAN AND PRESBYTER AGAINST THE
ACEPHALI

1. If the one who says that Christ is God only Manichaeizes, and the
one who says that Christ is man only Paulianizes, and the one who says
that Christ is perfect God but not perfect man Apollinarizes, and the
one who says perfect God and perfect man, the man being contemplated as
having his own subsistence and considered separately, and as being
actuated by the Word, Nestorianizes, while the one who says that the
same Christ is perfect God and man, inasmuch as God the Word united to
himself our lump, that is, the humanity that is ours, thinks
orthodoxly: those who have invented for us the saying "God-man" perhaps
claim that Christ is something else besides these, just as one would
say that the goat-stag is neither a goat nor a stag, but something else
in kind. If you confess one incarnate nature of the Word, you
Apollinarize by not saying that the human nature was assumed and saved
as perfect; but if you add flesh ensouled with an intellectual soul,
you are orthodox, if indeed you keep the sense of the confession. Say
then: you confess one nature of the Word incarnate in flesh ensouled
with a rational soul; do you confess one nature only in Christ, or two?
But if you confess one nature in Christ, you either Manichaeize or
Paulianize. If you say two perfect natures, yet divided and separated
part from part, you Nestorianize. If you confess two perfect natures
preserved in one characterizing hypostasis, you are orthodox. But if
you confess neither one only, nor two divided, nor yet united
inhypostatically, say how many natures you affirm in Christ, while
confessing one incarnate nature of God the Word in the assumption of
flesh that has an intellectual soul.

2. If according to you there is one nature of God the Word and of the
flesh, but not one nature of the Father and of the flesh, how is the
nature of God the Word and of the Father one and the same? And how will
there be one nature of uncreated Godhead and created flesh, or of the
timeless Godhead and of the Word's flesh that is under time, unless the
Godhead has been changed into the nature of flesh, or the flesh altered
into the nature of Godhead, rather than each nature being preserved
unchanged and undivided in Emmanuel?

3. Saying that Christ is from two natures, yet anathematizing those who
say that the two natures exist in Christ, united indivisibly in one
hypostasis, how will you understand "from two natures"? For you would
say either two before the union, or in the union. But if you say before
the union, you grant that each pre-existed before the union, which is
impious. But if you say the two natures in the union, and the union is
preserved unto the ages, then the two natures also are preserved unto
the ages in Emmanuel.

4. If God the Word, remaining what he was, became what he was not, and
he was not what he became, yet remained what he was, how did he not
remain as not being what he became? And if these things are true, the
evangelist, by saying "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us,"
teaches us the indivisible and unconfused union of the natures in
Christ.

5. If the Word, having become incarnate, remained what he was, and he
was without flesh, how will he not be without flesh according to the
nature of the Godhead, even though he became incarnate, having taken to
himself the lump of our nature? And if this is true, how should I not
say that the one Christ, that is, God the Word incarnate, has the two
natures, that is, essences: I mean that of the Father and the human
essence taken from the Theotokos Mary?

6. If the Father is impassible God and immortal, but the Son incarnate,
that is Christ, is impassible and passible, immortal and mortal, having
the impassible and the immortal according to the nature of the
Godhead, and having the passion and the death according to the nature
of the humanity, being one and the same, how should I not say the two
natures in him, the one receptive of passion and death, the other
incapable of receiving these? And if you too say that he received death
and the passions in the flesh, you speak rightly; but I ask whether the
flesh is the nature of his Godhead or other according to essence; and
if it is the same as the Godhead, you teach us a suffering of God; but
if it is other in nature, yet united inhypostatically to God the Word,
why do you not confess the two natures in the one Christ?

7. Every hypostasis is contemplated in a commonality, that is, in an
essence; and if the hypostasis should be simple, the commonality also
is simple; but if the hypostasis should be composite, the commonality
will also be composite, or the composite hypostasis will be in
different commonalities. For example, the hypostasis of the Word is
simple, and the commonality which he and the Father and the Spirit have
is simple; the hypostasis of any given man is composite, and the
commonality contemplated commonly in all men is composite. For every
man is a rational mortal animal. But if we should not wish to
understand the composite commonality, the given man will certainly be
found in two commonalities: the soul in the commonality with the other
souls, and the body in the commonality with the other bodies. But in
the case of Christ the hypostasis, being composite, is not contemplated
in a composite commonality. For no other Christ who is God and man is
in truth composite, except the one born of the Virgin Theotokos. If
then he has no composite commonality, it remains that he is
contemplated in different commonalities: in the commonality of the
Father and the Holy Spirit, according to which he is God, and in the
commonality with all men, to whom he is like according to being man.
And by commonality in all these we mean the essence, that is, nature;
for these exist commonly in all who are contemplated under the same
species, being present not more in one and less in another.

8. It belongs to the supreme union to have identity and unity and
otherness, as in the union according to hypostasis; for this, as in the
case of soul and body, has otherness of essence and identity of
hypostasis. For the essence of the soul is other and the essence of the
flesh is other, even if man is completed as one living being. And no
one would say that by nature the soul is mortal and the flesh
intelligible; and when the flesh is weak we say that the soul is in
health. But the man composed of both is one, and in the one each
likewise both is and is understood.

9. If essence is that which exists equally in all the particulars
ranked under the same species, and apart from the particulars happens
to be non-existent, do those who say "from two essences" mean that each
exists peculiarly in some of the particulars? Was the coming-together
from non-existents? But if peculiarly in some of the particulars, did
the two natures, contemplated in two persons, come together, which is
the teaching of Nestorius and of those who say that Christ was a mere
man before the union? But we do not say that Christ is in two natures
in this way, but that it is possible for two and more natures to be
contemplated in one and the same person, when their union is
inhypostatic. For if they were divided, each would certainly be
understood in a person having its own hypostasis.

10. The "one" is said not only of simple hypostases, that is, persons,
but also of composite ones. What then will be the difference between
simple and composite, unless we recognize the one in one essence and
the other in two essences? We therefore neither divide the natures from
one another after the union, for we proclaim one incarnate hypostasis;
nor do we confuse them with one another into one essentiality; for we
do not abolish the difference of the natures because of the union, but
we both recognize the composition in the whole and contemplate the
whole in the composition, proclaiming Christ wholly God together with
the body, but not according to the body, and confessing the same wholly
man also together with the Godhead, but not according to the Godhead.

11. As many natures as are divided inhypostatically are contemplated in
an equal number of persons. As many natures as are united
inhypostatically are contemplated in one person.

12. The flesh is flesh and not Godhead, and the Godhead is Godhead and
not flesh. If then these two are the same, how do they differ from one
another? According to nature, or according to hypostasis, or only
according to a mere name and not according to a difference of natures?
Of which of the natures in Christ is the Father God, and of which of
them is he Father? Is he God of the whole one incarnate nature of the
Word? Then the Arians speak well in calling the Father God of the Word.
But if he is not God of the Word, how does he say: "My Father and your
Father, and my God and your God"?

13. Did the things unlike one another before the union, that is, the
body and God the Word, remain unlike after the union, or did they
become like one another according to nature? If you say that to speak
of two natures is an addition of a nature in the Holy Trinity, then he
who speaks of two births of Christ also speaks of an addition of a
birth in the Holy Trinity.

14. When Christ was taken up, what of ours did he bring up to the
Father? A nature or a hypostasis? A body like the Word, or unlike? And
if like the Word, then either the Word is circumscribed or the body is
uncircumscribed. But if it is not uncircumscribed, where was the
uncircumscribed going up, so that the holy disciples were seeing a
phantasm and not the truth?

15. Is this one incarnate nature of God the Word, after the ineffable
taking-up, like the Father in every way and consubstantial with him,
or does Christ have that which is consubstantial with his own Father
and that which is of another essence? And if the whole Christ is
consubstantial with the Father, it is clear that the Father is created;
for the flesh is a creature. But if the body is of another essence
besides God the Word, how are there not two natures?

16. If Christ had said, "Destroy me, and in three days I will raise
myself," we should have said that the whole one incarnate nature of God
the Word was destroyed and died. But since he said, "Destroy this
temple, and in three days I will raise it," let them tell us which
nature was destroyed, and which raised the one that was destroyed. And
if they do not want this, let them tell us: did the whole one incarnate
nature of God the Word die, or part and part, or half and half, or a
third and two thirds?

17. If the one who says that the Godhead suffered Manichaeizes, and the
one who says that a man suffered and was crucified Nestorianizes, then
surely the one who says that the inseparable body of God suffered
thinks rightly.
