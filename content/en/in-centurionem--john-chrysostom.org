On the Centurion.
=61.769=
A certain centurion's slave, being ill, was about to die; but he did not die
because of the Master. He was about to die, for he was a man; but he did not
die because of the one who interceded, who was a lover of God. Nature demands
its own debt; but the Master of nature yielded the possession to the creature
through faith. Death and the necessity of nature were forcing him toward the
end, but faith pulled the other way, warring against death and paralyzing the
laws of death. And do not disbelieve if faith has such power as even to burst
the bonds of death, and to snatch souls up from the very pits of the
underworld.
Faith both opens the gates of Hades, and sets bolts upon the heavens, and
changes the bounds of nature, and puts the power of fire to sleep and shows
flame to be dew, and blunts the sting of death. I do not speak without
witnesses; I will confirm it for you from the holy Scriptures. I said that
faith opens the gates of Hades, and sets bolts upon the heavens. Therefore
receive the proofs as clear: see for me the blessed Peter spreading open the
gates of Hades by a word, and bringing Tabitha up from below. For he only
said, "Tabitha, arise," and at once the gates of Hades are opened, the
Tartarean bars fall, and she who had died obeys more swiftly than the living.
Again Elijah the prophet, trusting in this faith, said: "There shall be no
rain except by my mouth"; and the heaven is forged as bronze, and the rain is
held back for three years and six months, and the earth, as if iron by nature,
refuses the accustomed fruit, that you may know what faith can do. A man on
earth pronounces, and God in heaven confirms the sentence; a mortal mouth
utters, and the will of God votes with it, ratifying the words by deed.
Elijah, it says, shut the heaven for three years and six months. The bolt of
heaven was the mouth of a mortal man, adorned with pious faith. The same
Elijah again dared fiery chariots, and as a winged thing flew to the ethereal
dwellings, and indeed until today remains without tasting death, not by the
law of nature, but by the grace of the Only-Begotten, and by the power of
faith kept unswerving. And why must one say many things? Enoch was carried
from life to life with the body, overcoming death and old age and nature
through faith.
There are many other things one could say about faith, but I draw the
discourse together, that I may not produce satiety. I know that, even so, the
divine oracles know how to produce not satiety but longing in your
Christ-loving ears; yet all the same the word will return to the matter at
hand. A certain centurion's slave, it says, being ill, was about to die, who
was precious to him: a household servant precious to his master. For even if
the greed of life invented the slave, yet the master's goodwill did not deny
the kinship of nature. He did not spit him away as a household servant, but
cherished him as of the same race, lightening the heavy yoke of slavery by
love of mankind. Hear and learn, you who are masters of slaves, not to despise
your household servants as alien to nature, nor to be unsparing of those of
the same kind as if they were enemies, being persuaded by the blessed Paul who
says: "Masters, grant equality to your slaves, forbearing the threat, knowing
that their Lord and yours is in the heavens." God allowed there to be a slave,
that you might learn to be a slave to God. Do you demand goodwill from the
household servant? Be well-disposed yourself also toward the Master. Do you
punish the one who sins? Then do not sin, that you may not be punished by God.
If you do not grant pardon to one of the same race, do not ask for pardon
yourself.
For the Gospel teaches you henceforth to pray thus: "Forgive us our debts, as
we also have forgiven our debtors." As therefore seeking love of mankind from
God, so you also bestow love of mankind on those of the same race. And having
heard concerning Jesus, he sent to him elders of the Jews. What did he hear?
That by a word he heals, by a word he gives life to the dead, by a word he
drives out demons, by a word he accomplishes all things. For the word of God
is living and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword. He does not apply
herbs, nor drugs, but a word is sent from him, and the work is completed. See
how great is the difference between the centurion and the Jews. The one,
having heard, from mere hearing =61.770= believed, and having believed
approached him as God; but they, seeing the wonders before their eyes, denied
the wonder-worker; although sight is more effective than hearing. Sight is
more effective than hearing, but they had wickedness as teacher of the
disease. Therefore, blind in judgment, they set the Judge at naught. He sent,
it says, elders of the Jews, asking him that he might go and save his slave.
He sent elders to him, not declining to go himself through arrogance, but
hesitating to intercede himself for himself because of the greatness of the
dignity. He knew himself to be a man from a perishable man, but Jesus to be
God from God, Only-Begotten Son from the unbegotten Father. Therefore, moved
by reverence, he sends the elders of the Church, fulfilling the apostolic
things before the apostles: "Is anyone fainthearted? Let him call for the
elders of the Church, and let them pray over him, and if he has committed
sins, they shall be forgiven him." He therefore sent the elders to him, asking
him that he might come and save his slave. And you also marvel at the man's
compassion; for when his household servant was ailing he did not despise him,
but set such zeal as one would show suffering for himself. And these things
belong to a man looking toward virtue, to make such zeal, and to send an
embassy for the healing of a household servant.
He knew the equal honor of nature; and he did not despise him as a bondman,
but suffered with him as a fellow-slave, knowing that God is common to all.
But such was the centurion. Many of the rich, however, demand services from
those who are healthy without pardon, and when they are sick take no care at
all; but when disease has fallen, and the maidservant is thrown on the ground,
the master does not attend, the mistress does not visit; and if ever the
mistress must come near, she does not visit as one of the same race, but
attends with a grim look, converses with a harsh word, denying the kinship of
nature through the swelling of pride. And read the Scriptures. Do you not know
what the holy David says? "Behold, as the eyes of slaves toward the hands of
their masters, as the eyes of a maidservant toward the hands of her mistress,
so our eyes toward the Lord our God." Revere the image, stand in awe of what
is written, come down as helper of the one who is weak. The Savior, the
Only-Begotten Son of God, was well pleased to become man for men; you, being a
man, refuse the one of equal honor as not of equal honor, refuse compassion
toward kindred nature? And when an irrational animal is lame, you take care
because of the use; but when the rational one, that is, your household
servant, is weak, you cast him off and do not provide. Do you overlook that
what serves is rational? For this we are jealous. Man is a great good before
God; but a merciful man is precious. But we do not attend to what is written,
but we sometimes feed those outside, hunting the glory from men, while we let
those within be destroyed by hunger and freeze with the cold, not considering
the recompenses from God. But such was not the centurion, the one sober
everywhere, the one who truly possessed, keeping both the possession and
himself as God's possession, full of prudence and illumined by divine
temperance.
Therefore he also first sends the elders to Jesus. They then intercede with
much eagerness, they bring forward sufficient pleas, and they say: "He is
worthy to whom you would give the grace, the receiver of the gift is
well-disposed, he is worthy of your presence. For he loves our nation, and he
himself built the synagogue for us." Truly sufficient marks of piety,
sufficient proofs of a good conscience. What are these? Love toward God, and
genuine disposition toward man. "For he loves our nation": clearly the law.
For love is the fullness of the law. And he himself built the synagogue,
showing goodwill toward God. For constructing a house for God, he as it were
raises this temple as a conspicuous pillar of his love toward him. See how
good deeds, being remembered in the time of need, furnish much boldness of
access to those for whom embassy is made; for everywhere good deeds have
received a sufficient plea.
They then were interceding thus; but Jesus received the =61.771= exhortation
of the elders, not out of respect for them, but having accepted the faith of
the centurion. Rising therefore he was going with them. Perhaps he even walked
more slowly, and made his step quiet, not delaying toward doing good, but
giving time, that he might make public the centurion's hidden inner
disposition, that he might parade the unswerving faith for the refutation of
those who do not believe, that he might display as on a stage the unmixed and
unadulterated mind. For already, when he was not far from the house, the
centurion sent to him his slaves, saying, "Lord, do not trouble yourself; for
I am not sufficient that you should enter under my roof." According to the
first impulse of his soul he sends the elders; but even having done this, and
remaining by himself, he does not rest. For in the meanwhile, having
considered how great is the difference of dignity, he sends slaves, entreating
him and saying, "Do not trouble yourself; and I am not sufficient that you
should enter under my roof." O the understanding and sobriety of the
centurion! God hidden in a man did not escape him; the Only-Begotten Word
covered by the wrapping of the body did not escape him. He sees the temple,
and understands the inhabitant. The Master in a slave's rank did not escape
him, the king in a captive's appearance, the small one in unbounded greatness.
Immediately then Jesus marvels, and bears witness to him of his utmost faith,
of the noble impulse of his soul, according to which he said to the Savior: "I
am not sufficient that you should enter under my roof." Such also was Peter,
struck at the catch of the fish, and taking thought of the greatness of the
power, then entreating him and saying: "Go out from me, for I am a sinful man,
Lord." But perhaps he did not know the things concerning Peter, yet he had
come to know the things in Leviticus. Leprosy, it says, lying upon a man makes
unclean the one who has fallen under the disease. And to leprosy are akin
white patches and white spots and lichens. Such affections are hard to discern
for the many, and especially at the beginning of the disease; whence the law
wishes the priest to be appointed as knower of such things. The law therefore
leads the priest into the house of the one being judged; and there, having
judged it as leprosy, he declares all things in the house polluted. Whence the
many rearrange beforehand, and then call the priest. Taking the thought from
this, the blessed centurion declines the coming of the Savior.
"I am not," he says, "sufficient that you should enter under my roof." Many
are under that roof; I know my own sins; I therefore decline the presence as
of a judge, before I am cleansed. I know you as leader of all the priests, and
high priest of the Father; "I am not sufficient that you should enter under my
roof." Heaven is a throne for you, and the earth a footstool; what house will
contain the uncontained? I do not have a heavenly roof, that you should enter
under my roof; I do not have royal courts, that I may receive the king of the
heavens. How shall I receive under a roof him who roofs his upper chambers in
waters? how shall my poor lodging receive as tent-mate him who measured the
heaven with a span? what roof will contain him who contains the whole heaven
and the earth together? "I am not sufficient that you should enter under my
roof." As of a benefactor I seize the entrance, as of a judge I stand in awe
of the =61.772= boldness of access. You are overseer of souls, examiner of the
inward parts, searcher of the heart and judge of sins. "I am not sufficient
that you should enter under my roof." I fear myself; "Say therefore a word,
and my servant will be healed." Does it not seem to you that the one who says,
"Say by a word only," clearly knows that "By the Word of the Lord the heavens
were established, and all their host," and that all things coming to be by the
Word both are held together and are governed? For having been illumined from a
divine thought, the man all at once saw Jesus in the body, recognizing this
one as Son of God. "Say," he says, "by a word only." I know what the wise
Solomon proclaimed concerning you: "Neither herb nor poultice healed your
people, but your word, O Lord, that heals all things." "Say by a word only." I
know whose Word you are named, and how great the power you possess, and that
by a word you accomplished making all things. The Father did not travail in
begetting you; you do not travail in making, you do not travail in creating.
"Say by a word only." For your word is most effective toward all things, not
striking the air, but making your will known.
"Say therefore by a word only; give the signal for the full assurance of those
who hear." See understanding: he asks that the coming be postponed, yet he
does not circumscribe the knowledge; for he bears witness to the knowledge in
him, but declines the personal coming along the road. And this so wise thought
is succeeded by another still greater. For thus the man advances from power to
power, and a wealth of noteworthy thoughts springs in him, growing young in
the increases of contemplations. "Say by a word only, and my servant will be
healed. For I too am a man set under authority, having soldiers under myself;
and I say to this one, Go, and he goes; and to another, Come, and he comes;
and to the slave, Do this, and he does it." I know, he says, that being girded
with the authority of all things, you sojourned among us as one of all. You
came to us as we are, having the authority of all things, as the holy David
says: "By your ordinance the day remains, for all things are your slaves." You
sleep, and the sea rises; you rise, and the waves grow calm. You call the dead
and the dead man obeys more swiftly than the living. "Say by a word, and my
servant will be healed. For I too am a man set under authority." Here he makes
clear the self-governing impulse of the soul, preventing transgression against
what is better. "I have," he says, "soldiers under myself." He calls soldiers
the various and differing reasonings that lurk in the man. For the blessed
Paul knows to name these soldiers: "I delight," he says, "in the law according
to the inner man; but I see another law warring against the law of my mind,
and taking me captive to the law of sin."
Reasonably then he was saying, "I have soldiers under myself; I do not have
them against myself; and I say to this one, Go, and he goes; and to another,
Come, and he comes; I am not ruled, but I rule the reasonings." Perhaps the
centurion is called centurion also for this, as ruler and autocrat of the
passions, showing us such endurance in the irrational passions; of which may
it be granted to us all to attain, by the grace and love of mankind of our
Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might, now and always, and
unto the ages of ages. Amen.
