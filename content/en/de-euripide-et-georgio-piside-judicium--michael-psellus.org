* A Judgment on Euripides and George of Pisidia
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The same man, to one who asked, "Who versified better, Euripides or
Pisides?"
Both are serviceable, at least for meter and for poetry: both the
Phliasian Euripides the tragedian, and George of the Pisidians, who
perfected the iambic meter beyond the many who [followed] him. But
since he made the same discourse, so to speak, most nearly like, the
distinction is hard: how the two differ from one another, and which of
the two is fairer than the other. Yet concerning both arts, if someone
possesses the science of meter and a noteworthy knowledge of rhythms,
and is lofty-minded and theoretical about meters and feet, it is not
very hard to find some difference in both and to give one the prize of
victory over the other. And let this stand at the head of the whole
discourse: that meters and rhythms have admitted countless variations
across the ages. For the heroic meter no longer preserves its ancient
magnificence, nor does it stand among the most exact meters, but it
has been mixed with the dactylic and the spondaic, and with ithyphallic
meters and pairings of iambs; nor does the iambic meter appear
possessing the same pauses and the same rhythms and the same
composition as those of former times, but it is now more theatrical
and, as if upon the dancing-floor, has grown shameless, and it
overleaps every base and flies over every rhythm, and now the only
thing sought after is the darting of the meter and its iambic beat.
Tragic poetry, however, adorned with diverse rhythms and taking varied
meters, does not [throughout] dance its subjects out of measure, nor
overleap them, but there are places where it also seeks well-struck
sounds and prefers the eurhythmy of the feet and welcomes the metrical
pairings of the speech; yet wherever it seems good to the poet, it
changes its tone and alters the rhythm, and sometimes the rhythm is
snatched away, and sometimes it walks upon a trisyllabic or
tetrasyllabic metrical base. And rhetorical speech, in beauty (I mean
in the idea), somehow rhythms the [...] more musical rhythms, but in
the remaining ideas it especially roughens, I mean it sets the meters
as rough, and roughens the hearing.
Euripides, then, having perfected poetry as no one else has, unless
someone should prefer Sophocles to him, is marvelous for his variety
and able to imitate all things. And sometimes you will find the poet
going dithyrambic, seeking after new things and preferring them, and
sometimes leading the other graces and dignities, adorned with
choriambs, and becoming manifold in his poetry; most ethical where
character must be made solemn, and again most pathetic where the
sufferings of those who have suffered surge beneath, since the most
perfect head of tragic poetry is pathos. And of the two forms, speaking
tragically and speaking comically, this is the difference: that there
pathos is the underlying matter, but here for the most part laughter
and graces. And since the parts of tragedy are also more numerous, not
only as regards the stage, but also as regards the whole treatment and
composition, the part upon the stage is one thing, [the] choral
[another], the part from another scene is yet another, and the
episodes are one thing, the pre-episodes another. Some things
messengers utter, others Phrygians, a barbarian company, or captive
women, or another chorus; and not for all are [the same] meters
appropriate to all, but the poet must also call up a womanly song and
imitate a barbarian character, and perfect the Greek tongue, and
otherwise play what is fitting to the persons at hand. For these
reasons the verses too, in these cases, are different at each point,
and the meters are of another kind, and the rhythms are not suited to
one another. For Sophocles, then, and indeed for Aeschylus, the
thoughts are deeper and the construction of the speech more solemn,
and there are not graces everywhere, nor well-struck rhythms, but most
things are more solemn and, so to speak, most seemly.
Aeschylus, at least, in the Prometheus hung aloft, departs a little
from his own character, and, delighting greatly in pure iambs and in
certain little words that fawn upon the ear, took hold of the subject
more smoothly than it [warrants]. But in the rest of his dramatic
subjects, especially where he imitates the Dareian characters, he is
for the most part formidable and hard to utter, and no one would
understand him who had not been, so to speak, initiated in the
theophanies. But Euripides, having composed eighty dramas or even more,
is everywhere statuesque and graceful, not only in the graces of
speech, but even in the sufferings themselves. And often, having
dramatized the Athenians at the fitting time, he brought them down to
a great many tears; for they thought they saw what was spoken as if it
were coming to pass. And where he brings in Orestes raving, having
first, as was fitting, prefaced as much as [concerns] Tantalus, then
coming down to the sister Electra and the women gathering from the
neighborhood, so as to see Orestes and visit him, he does not even
utter "with quiet foot" (Or. 136), but that man stood where he was
standing, and they, answering Electra as she said "Be silent, make no
noise, let there be no din" and the rest, wound a kind of chorus
toward one another, and uttered the meters to the song, and it was no
care to them if they had to give the word an acute accent: they sang
otherwise. Thus everywhere his concern is for rhythmic phrasing and
the eloquence of diction and the tunefulness of rhythm. Quite simply,
then, he introduces the whole of music and the rhythmic movements
themselves into his own poems, and his speeches are neither without
intervals nor without a change of rare words. And the man has taken
thought especially in other things: for the wise man transposes the
meters and the diction and varies the phrasing according to his power.
The man neither overlooks the plots nor the characters of the persons,
although in these things the tragedies of Sophocles, more than the
others, were worked out and labored over. But Euripides cared less for
these, and worked more than the other at song-making, I mean that in
words, and the use of it, and these three of the fairest sciences,
music and rhythmics and metrics, gathering them like pipes and
kitharas and lyres into his own subjects. And when the tongue needed
to speak barbarously, he has imitated it so that the same man seems
both to hellenize to the utmost and to solecize exactly; for what is
beside the Attic tongue in barbarian words has the look of solecism.
And there are places where he misses what is fitting, and becomes more
a thing of rhetorical power than of the exactness of poetry. For
instance, having brought Hecuba on against Odysseus, a noble and
rhetorical man, he raises her against him and grants her the first
honors; and he does exercise that man too not without charm, but he
makes him lesser than the captive woman. And for the man the fault is
for others a success. But the exactness of speech and the power of the
science demand the best from all things.
Such, then, is Euripides. But the sage from Pisidia, I think from
lesser Antioch, is not a poet of hexameters, but having pursued one
kind of meter, the iambic, I mean the ancient and of a single form,
and having worked at this for the most part, he varies in no way the
[...] persons belonging to this, nor a dramatic construction nor a
varied [...]. But he composes the iambs very rhythmically and
eloquently, not finely chopping the iamb into many parts, but in some
places content with three only. And in all things his phrasing is
unforced, as if he were not inventing either the thoughts or the
words, but reading the things set before him. All his words are Greek,
and, together with magnificence, well-sounding. And if he should take
hold of a trope, whether that in play and in graces or that in
solemnity, he so advances what he has found in the discourse and so
fittingly sets it that he does not seem to spoil at all the other
subject from which he started toward his own. If, then, he has named a
disease in the discourse, he at once wheels in the whole of medicine,
sparing neither the causes nor the things by which diseases are
treated. And if he should hang a chain from heaven and fasten some
powers to it, at once there are the bonds and the hangings and all
that belongs to the height and all that belongs to the last link of
that slack chain. And if he should gather the Hours and urge them on
together as in a chorus, ah, the meeting of hands and the circle and
the winding and the circuit and the epode and the strophe and the
antistrophe and the motion. And if he should also choose to
theatricalize the discourse and call the composition of the Hours a
four-horse team, how much he utters about charioteers and the finest
charioteering, how much about yokes and wheels and axle-boxes, and
what the hubs are like for him, and the felloes of the wheels, and the
horse-trappings and snortings. And his discourse descends even to the
last things of the craft, and concerning things about which not even
those who work at the craft themselves would speak. And if he should
also set phalanxes upon one another, you would find there the whole
cavalry and infantry array: the spearmen, the javelin-throwers, the
archers, the enomotarch, the half-file-leader, the file-closer, the
file-mates, the rear-rankers. And his verses leap as if from a sling,
together with the feet, and with the meter they bring the thought to a
swift close. If, then, you should compare the Pisidian meters and the
rhythms with tragic poetry, I mean that of Euripides, inferior [...]
of that man [...] diction, and looking upon blood from the earth, and
[...] the first honors against the poet. For I do not know if anyone
knows how to write iambs more finely than this man, just as [I do not
know it] of writing hexameters either, except that he composed the
heroic verses few and easily numbered.
