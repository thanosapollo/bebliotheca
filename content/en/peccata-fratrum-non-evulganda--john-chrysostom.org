* That the Sins of the Brethren Ought Not to Be Published

On not publishing the sins of the brethren, nor cursing the enemies.

=51.353=

αʹ. I count you blessed for the zeal, beloved, with which you run together
into the paternal house. For from this zeal I have confidence also concerning
our health according to the soul; for the teaching of the Church is a wondrous
infirmary: an infirmary, not of =51.354= bodies, but of souls. For it is
spiritual, and it does not correct wounds of flesh, but sins of the mind; and
of these sins and wounds the medicine is the word. This medicine is composed
not from herbs that are upon the earth, but from the =51.355= words that are
from heaven; this not the hands of physicians, but the tongues of prophets
prepared. For this reason it is lasting, and is neither dimmed by multitude of
years, nor convicted by the power of diseases. For the medicines of physicians
have both these defects: for while they are fresh they display their own
strength, but when much time has passed, like bodies that have grown old, they
become weaker; and often even the difficulty of ailments has convicted them;
for they are human. But the divine medicine is not such, but when much time
has elapsed, it has all its proper strength. At any rate from the time Moses
came to be (for from him is the beginning of the Scriptures), it has healed so
many men, and has not cast away its own power; nor has any disease ever
prevailed over it. This medicine it is not possible to receive by laying down
silver, but he who has displayed a genuine choice and disposition has gone
away having it all. For this reason both rich and poor alike enjoy this
healing. For where there is necessity to lay down money, the well-provided
shares in the benefit; but the poor often goes away deprived of the gain, his
income not sufficing him for the preparation of the medicine. But here, since
it is not silver that must be laid down, but faith and choice that must be
displayed, he who has laid these down with eagerness, this one especially
reaps the benefit; since these also are the wage of the healing. And the rich
and the poor share the benefit alike; or rather they do not share the benefit
alike, but often the poor goes away having enjoyed more. Why ever? Because the
rich, preoccupied with many cares, having the arrogance and the swelling of
prosperity, living with neglect and sloth, does not receive the medicine of
the hearing of the Scriptures with much exactness, nor with much zeal; but the
poor, delivered from luxury and gluttony and sloth, spending all his time in
the labor of his hands and in just toils, and gathering from this much
philosophy in his soul, becomes more attentive and more vigorous, and has
attended to what is said with more exactness; whence, having also laid down a
greater wage, he goes away having reaped a greater benefit.

βʹ. I have not said these things simply accusing those who are rich, nor
simply praising the poor; for neither is wealth an evil, but the using wealth
badly; nor is poverty a good, but the using poverty well. That rich man in the
case of Lazarus was punished, not because he was rich, but because he was
cruel and inhuman. That poor man in the bosom of Abraham was praised, not
because he was poor, but because he bore poverty with thanksgiving. For of
things (but attend with exactness to this word; for it will be able to put
into you a sufficient philosophy


and to cast out every corrupted reasoning, and to make you have a right
judgment concerning the things that are), of things, then, some are good by
nature, and some the contrary; and some are neither good nor evil, but occupy
the middle rank. Piety is good by nature, impiety is evil; virtue is good,
wickedness is evil; but wealth and poverty in themselves are neither this
=51.356= nor that; but according to the choice of those who use them they
become either this or that. For if you use wealth toward loving-kindness, the
thing has become for you a ground of good; but if toward robberies and greed
and insolence, you have turned its use to the contrary, yet wealth is not the
cause, but he who has used wealth toward insolence. So also it is possible to
speak concerning poverty: for if you bear it nobly, giving thanks to the
Master, the thing has become for you an occasion and ground of crowns; but if
you blaspheme the Maker because of this, and accuse his providence, you have
again used the thing for evil. But just as there wealth is not the cause of
greed, but he who has used wealth badly; so also here we shall not blame
poverty for the blasphemy, but him who was not willing to bear the thing
temperately. For everywhere both the praise and the blame belong to our mind
and our choice. Wealth is good, but not simply, but that with which there is
no sin; and again, poverty is wicked, but not simply, but in the mouth of an
impious man, since he is displeased, since he blasphemes, since he is
indignant, since he accuses the Maker.

γʹ. Let us not then accuse wealth, nor simply abuse poverty, but those who are
not willing to use these things well; for the things themselves lie in the
middle. But as I was saying (for it is good to return to the former subject),
that both rich and poor enjoy the medicines here with the same freedom and
confidence; and often the poor with greater zeal. For this is not the only
excellence of the medicines, that they heal souls, that they are not destroyed
by length of time, that they are not defeated by disease, that the benefit is
set forth freely, that the healing is equally for rich and poor; but they have
also something else not less than these goods. What then is this? Those who
come into this infirmary we do not make public. For those who go off to the
infirmaries outside have many who look upon the wounds; and unless the
physician first uncovers the sore, he does not apply the medicine; but here it
is not so, but seeing countless who are sick, we make their healing secretly.
For we do not bring those who have sinned into the midst, and thus publish
their sins; but having set forth the teaching common to all, we leave it to
the conscience of the hearers, so that each may draw to himself from what is
said the medicine suitable to his own wound. For the word of teaching goes
forth from the tongue of the speaker, having accusation of vice, praise of
virtue, blame of wantonness, encomium of temperance, censure of arrogance,
praise of gentleness, like a varied and manifold medicine composed from all
kinds; but to take what is fitting and useful for himself belongs to each of
the hearers. The word then goes forth openly, but seating itself in the
conscience of each, it both supplies its own healing secretly, and often has
brought health before the disease has been made public.

δʹ. You heard, at any rate, yesterday how I praised the power of prayer, how I
abused those who with sloth pray =51.357= ing, having published none of them.
Those then who were conscious of zeal in themselves received the encomium of
prayer, and became more zealous by the praises; but those who were conscious
of sloth in themselves received the rebuke in turn, and laid aside their
neglect. But we know neither these nor those; and this ignorance is useful to
both. How, I will say. He who heard the encomiums of prayer, and was conscious
of zeal in himself, if he had many witnesses of the encomiums, would have
slipped into arrogance; but now


having received the praise secretly, he has been delivered from all
boastfulness. Again he who was conscious of sloth in himself, having heard the
accusation, became better from the accusation, having no known men of the
rebuke; and this benefited him not a little. For because of being fluttered
toward the opinion of the many, as long as we think we are hidden being evil,
we are eager to become better; but when we have become manifest to all, and
have lost the consolation from being hidden, we become more shameless and more
slothful. And just as sores, being stripped and frequently associating with
cold air, become more grievous; so also the soul that has sinned, if it is
convicted among many for the things in which it has transgressed, becomes more
shameless. In order then that this may not happen, the word has healed you
secretly. And that you may learn that this hidden healing has much gain, hear
what Christ says: If your brother sin against you, rebuke him; and he did not
say, between you and the city, nor between you and the people, but, Between
you and him alone. Let the accusation be without witnesses, he says, that the
change toward correction may become easy. A great good, then, is it to make
the exhortation not published. The conscience is enough, that incorruptible
judge is enough. You do not rebuke the one who has sinned as his own
conscience does (that accuser is more bitter), nor knowing more exactly the
things that have been transgressed. Do not then add wound to wounds,
publishing the one who has sinned, but make the exhortation without witnesses.
This then we also do now, which Paul also did, constructing without witnesses
the accusation of the one who had sinned among the Corinthians. And hear how:
For this reason, he says, brethren, I have transferred these things to myself
and Apollos. And yet it was not he, nor Apollos, who split the people and cut
the Church asunder; but nevertheless he shaded the accusation, and as with
certain masks, having hidden the faces of those responsible under his own and
Apollos' names, he was giving them authority to change from that wickedness.
And again, Lest somehow God humble me when I come, and I mourn many of those
who have sinned before and have not repented of the uncleanness and wantonness
which they practiced. See how here too he speaks of those who have sinned
indefinitely, that he may not, having made the accusation manifest, make the
soul of those who have sinned more shameless. As then we make the rebukes with
so much sparing, so, I exhort, do you also receive the correction with all
zeal, and attend with exactness to what is said.

εʹ. We discoursed to you yesterday concerning the power according to prayer. I
showed how the devil then am =51.358= bushes, being a worker of evil. For
since he sees the greatest gain coming to us from prayer, then especially he
attacks; that he may knock us away from our defense, that he may send us home
with empty hands. And just as in the case of rulers, whenever those of the
order and those about the ruler himself are hostile toward those who approach,
they drive them away from afar through the rods, preventing them from coming
near and lamenting and obtaining loving-kindness; so also the devil, whenever
he sees men approaching the Judge, drives them away from afar, not through a
rod, but through sloth. For he knows, he knows clearly, that if they approach
sober, and speak the things they have sinned, and lament with a boiling soul,
they go away having received much pardon; for God is loving toward man; and
for this reason he anticipates, and leads them away from the petition, that
they may obtain none of the things they need. But the soldiers of the rulers
scare away those who approach with force; but he not with necessity, but
deceiving us and casting us into sloth. For this reason we are not even worthy
of pardon, willingly depriving ourselves of the goods. Prayer with zeal is a
light of mind and soul, a light unquenchable and continual. For this reason he
casts countless rabble of thoughts into our minds, and what we have never
thought, these


gathering at the time of prayer he pours upon our souls. And just as winds
often falling from the opposite side, having fanned a lamp-fire that is being
kindled, have quenched it; so also the devil, whenever he sees the flame of
prayer kindled in us, fanning with countless cares from this side and that,
does not withdraw until he has quenched the light. But what those who kindle
those lamps do, this let us also do. And what do they do? Whenever they see a
violent wind approaching, putting the finger upon the hole of the lamp they
wall off the entrance against the wind. For as long as it strikes from
without, we shall be able to resist; but when we open to him the doors of the
mind, and receive the enemy within, we are no longer able to resist even a
little; but having quenched our memory from every side, as a smoking lamp, he
lets the mouth bring forth empty words. But just as they put the finger upon
the hole of the lamp, so let us put the reasoning upon our mind; let us block
the entrance of the wicked spirit, that he may not quench our light of prayer.
Do you remember both these examples, both that of the soldiers, and of the
ruler, and that concerning the lamp? For this reason we say these examples to
you, among which we turn, among which we are, that even having withdrawn from
here and having come to be at home, we may take from the things at hand a
reminder of what has been said. Prayer is a great weapon, and a great
security.

ϛʹ. You heard yesterday how the three children, being bound, dissolved the
power of the fire, how they trampled the flame, how they prevailed over the
furnace, and mastered the working of the element. Hear today again how the
noble and great Isaac prevailed over the very nature of bodies through prayer.
Those dissolved the power of the fire; this one =51.359= today loosed the
bonds of nature that had been maimed. And learn how he did this. Isaac, it
says, entreated concerning his wife, because she was barren. These things were
read to you today; yesterday the discourse was concerning prayer, and today
again a demonstration of the power of prayer. Do you see how the grace of the
Spirit managed that the things read today should be in harmony with what was
said yesterday? Isaac, it says, entreated concerning Rebecca his wife, because
she was barren. This first is worth inquiring, for what reason she was barren.
She too, and the husband, were of a wondrous life and one full of much
temperance. We cannot take hold of the life of the righteous and say that the
barrenness was a work of sins. And not she only was barren, but also his
mother Sarah, who bore him; and not only his mother was barren, nor the wife,
but also the daughter-in-law, the wife of Jacob, Rachel. What does this chorus
of barren women mean? All righteous, all living in virtue, all witnessed to by
God. For concerning them he was saying: I am the God of Abraham, and the God
of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Concerning the same Paul also speaks somehow
thus: For which cause God is not ashamed to be called their God. Many are
their encomiums in the New, many their praises in the Old. From every side
they are splendid and well-reputed, and all had barren wives, and continued in
childlessness for a long time. Whenever then you see a man and a woman living
together in virtue, whenever you see them beloved of God, attending to piety,
and sick with childlessness, do not think that the childlessness is altogether
a recompense of sins. For many are the reasons of God's economy and
unspeakable to us, and we must give thanks for all things, and count wretched
only those who live in vice, not those who have not acquired children. Often
God does what is expedient; but we do not know the cause of the things that
come to be. For this reason everywhere we must marvel at his wisdom, and
glorify his unspeakable loving-kindness.

ζʹ. But this word can educate us unto character; yet we must also tell the
cause for which those women were barren. What then is the cause? That,
whenever you see the Virgin bearing our common Master, you may not disbelieve.
Therefore


exercise your mind in the womb of the barren women; that, whenever you see the
womb that has been maimed and bound toward childbearing being opened from the
grace of God, you may not marvel hearing that a virgin has borne. Or rather,
both marvel, and be struck, but do not disbelieve the marvel. Whenever the Jew
says to you, How did the virgin bear? say to him, How did the barren and aged
woman bear? Two hindrances were there then, both the untimeliness of age, and
the uselessness of nature; but in the case of the Virgin there was one
hindrance only, the not having partaken of marriage. The barren woman then
makes a way beforehand for the virgin. And that you may learn that for this
reason the barren women went before, that the birth of the Virgin might be
believed, hear the words =51.360= of Gabriel toward her. For when he came and
said to her, You shall conceive in the womb, and shall bear a son, and shall
call his name Jesus, the Virgin was struck and marveled, and said: How shall
this be to me, since I know not a man? What then did the angel? The Holy
Spirit shall come upon you. Do not seek the sequence of nature, he says, when
what is coming to be is above nature; do not look around for marriage and
birth-pangs, when the manner of the generation is greater than marriage. And
how shall this be, she says, since I know not a man? And yet for this reason
this shall be, since you know not a man. For if you knew a man, you would not
have been counted worthy to serve this ministry. So that through that for
which you disbelieve, through this believe. And you would not have been
counted worthy to serve such a ministry, not because marriage is an evil, but
because virginity is better; and the entrance of the Master needed to be more
august than ours; for it was royal; and the king enters through a more august
way. It was necessary both that that birth should share, and that it should be
different from ours. Therefore both these things are managed. For to come to
be from a womb is common toward us; but to come to be without marriages is
greater than according to us. And to be carried in the womb and conceived is
of human nature; but for the conception to come to be without intercourse is
more august than human nature. For this reason both these things have come to
be, that you may learn both the superiority and the sharing of the one born
toward you.

ηʹ. And consider with me the wisdom of the things that come to be. Neither did
the superiority injure the likeness and kinship toward us, nor did the kinship
toward us dim the superiority, but each was shown through all the things; and
some things it had wholly ours, and some different toward us. But as I was
saying, that for this reason the barren women went before, that the birth of
the Virgin might be believed, that she herself might be led by the hand toward
the faith of that promise and pledge which she heard from the angel saying,
The Holy Spirit shall come upon you, and the power of the Most High shall
overshadow you; thus, he says, you have to bear. Do not look toward the earth;
the working comes from the heavens. What is coming to be is a grace of the
Spirit; do not seek from me nature and laws of marriages. But since those
words were greater than this, he wishes to furnish also another demonstration.
And you observe with me how the barren woman leads her toward the faith of
this. For since that demonstration was greater than the mind of the Virgin,
hear how he also brought the word down to the humbler things, leading her by
the hand through the sensible. For behold, he says, Elizabeth your kinswoman,
she also has conceived a son in her old age; and this is the sixth month for
her who is called barren. Do you see that the barren woman is because of the
Virgin? since, for what reason did he introduce to her the birth of the
kinswoman? for what reason did he say, In her old age? for what reason did he
add, Who is called barren? Through all these things clearly leading her to
believe the good tidings. For this reason he also spoke the age, and the
maiming of nature; for this reason =51.361= he also waited the time from the
conception; for he did not immediately from the beginnings evangelize her, but
waited for a six-month time to come to be for the barren woman; that the bulk
of the womb might thereafter guarantee the pregnancy, and become an undisputed
demonstration of the conception. And see with me again the understanding of
Gabriel. For he did not remind her of Sarah, nor of


Rebecca, nor of Rachel; and yet these too were barren, and these too had grown
old, and what was coming to be was a marvel; but the narratives were old. And
new and recent things, and those happening according to our own generation,
are accustomed to lead us more toward faith in marvels than the ancient. For
this reason, having left those, he put forward from Elizabeth the kinswoman
herself to consider what was upon her, so as from that one to lead her toward
her own birth, that most fearful and most august. For the birth of the barren
woman was in the middle of both ours and the Master's, less than that of the
Virgin, but greater than ours. For this reason, as through some bridge,
Elizabeth being in the middle, he leads the mind of the Virgin up from the
birth-pangs according to nature to those above nature.

θʹ. I wished to say more, and to teach you other reasons for which Rebecca and
Rachel were barren; but the time does not allow, pressing the discourse toward
the power of prayer. For this reason we have also stirred all these things,
that you may learn how the prayer of Isaac loosed the barrenness of the wife,
and a prayer of so much time. Isaac, it says, entreated concerning Rebecca his
wife, and God heard him. For do not think that he called upon God, and was
immediately heard; for he spent much time entreating God. And if you wish to
learn how much, I will tell you this also with exactness. He spent a number of
twenty years entreating God. From where is this clear? From the sequence
itself. For the Scripture, wishing to show the faith and the endurance and the
philosophy of the righteous man, did not even keep silent about the time, but
made even this clear to us, secretly indeed, so as to awaken our sloth, yet
nevertheless it did not allow it to be unseen. Hear at any rate how it made
the time clear to us secretly. And Isaac was forty years old, it says, when he
took Rebecca the daughter of Bethuel the Syrian. You have learned how many
years old he was when he brought the wife? He was forty years old, it says,
when he took Rebecca. But since we have learned how many years old he was when
he married the wife, let us learn also when he thereafter became a father, and
how many years old he was then, when he begot Jacob; and we shall be able to
see how much time the wife remained barren, and that throughout all this time
he was entreating God. How many years old then was he, when he begot Jacob?
Jacob came forth, it says, holding with his right hand the heel of his
brother; for this reason he called him Jacob, and that one Esau. And Isaac was
sixty years old, when he begot them. If then, when he brought Rebecca, he was
forty years, and when he begot the sons, sixty, it is quite clear that for
twenty years between the wife remained barren, =51.362= and throughout all
this time Isaac was entreating God.

ιʹ. Then are we not ashamed, nor do we hide our faces, seeing the righteous
man remaining twenty years and not withdrawing; but we often from a first or
second request giving up and being displeased? And yet he had much confidence
toward God, and nevertheless he was not displeased at the delay of the gift,
but remained enduring; but we, being full of countless sins, living with an
evil conscience, displaying no goodwill toward the Master, if we are not heard
before we even speak, we are distressed, we lose heart, we withdraw from the
request; for this reason we always retire with empty hands. Who has entreated
God twenty years for one thing, as this righteous man? or rather who even
twenty months only? Yesterday then I was saying that there are many who pray
with sloth, and yawning, and stretching, and turning about continually, and
using all neglect concerning the prayers; but today I have found also another
harm coming upon the prayers more destructive than that. For many, throwing
themselves prone, and striking the earth with the forehead, and pouring forth
hot tears, and groaning bitterly from below, and stretching out the hands, and
displaying much zeal, use this heat and this eagerness against their own
salvation. For they do not


entreat God for their own sins, nor ask pardon for the things they have
transgressed, but they move all this zeal against their enemies, doing the
same as if someone, having sharpened the sword, should use the weapon not
against the enemies, but should thrust it through his own neck. So also these
use the prayers not for the forgiveness of their own sins, but against the
punishment of the enemies; which is to thrust the sword against themselves.
And the wicked one has devised this also, that we may destroy ourselves from
every side, both through sloth, and through zeal. For some provoke God by
neglect concerning the prayers, displaying the contempt through the sloth; but
others, since they display zeal, display the zeal again against their own
salvation. So-and-so, he says, is slothful; it is enough for me that he obtain
nothing; this one is zealous and awakened; what then is to happen? I cannot
dissolve the zeal, nor cast him into neglect; I will hunt his destruction
otherwise. How? I will prepare him to use the zeal unto lawlessness; for to
pray against the enemies is lawless. He will then go away not only having
gained nothing from the zeal, but also having endured a greater harm than that
through sloth. Such are the devices of the devil; some he destroys through
sloth, and some through the zeal itself, whenever this does not come to be
according to the laws.

ιαʹ. But it is worth hearing also the very words of the prayer, and how the
words are of a childish mind, how of an infantile soul. I am ashamed then as I
am about to speak them, yet it is altogether necessary to speak and to imitate
that uneducated tongue. What then are =51.363= the words? Avenge me from my
enemies; show them that I too have a God. They do not then learn, O man, that
we have a God, when we are indignant, and angry, and displeased; but when we
are gentle, and meek, and tame, and practice all philosophy. Thus also God
said: Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works, and
glorify your Father who is in the heavens. Do you not consider that it is an
insolence toward God, to ask God against the enemies? And how is it an
insolence? he says. Because he himself said, Pray for your enemies, and
introduced this divine law. Whenever then you think it fit that the lawgiver
dissolve his own laws, and you exhort him to legislate against himself, and
you beseech him who has forbidden you to curse the enemies to hear you cursing
the enemies, you are not praying when you do this, nor exhorting, but you are
insulting the lawgiver, and you are behaving drunkenly toward the one who is
about to give you the goods from the prayer. And how is it possible to be
heard praying, tell me, when you provoke the one who is about to hear? For
doing these things you thrust your own salvation into a pit, and you are borne
down a precipice, striking the enemy before the eyes of the king. For even if
you do not do this with the hands, you strike him with the words; which you do
not even dare to do in the case of fellow-servants. Dare at any rate to do
this before a ruler; and even if you have succeeded in countless things, you
will at once altogether be led away to death. Then before a ruler you do not
dare to insult the one of equal honor, but doing this before God, tell me, do
you not shudder, nor fear, in a time of petition and prayer being so savage
and becoming a beast, and displaying a greater ingratitude than the one
demanding the hundred denarii? For that you insult more than that one, hear
the history itself. Someone owed ten thousand talents to the master; then not
having to pay, he asked him to be longsuffering, that, his wife and house and
children having been sold, he might dissolve the master's debt. But the
master, seeing him lamenting, pitied him and forgave the ten thousand talents.
That one going out and finding another servant owing him a hundred denarii,
throttling him demanded with much cruelty and inhumanity. The master hearing
these things threw him into the prison, and the debt of the ten thousand
talents which he had formerly forgiven, he laid upon him again, and


for the cruelty toward the fellow-servant that one paid the punishment.

ιβʹ. But you, see how much more ungrateful and =51.364= more insensible you
have become than even that one, cursing the enemies. That one was not asking
the master to demand, but himself was demanding the hundred denarii; but you
even exhort the Master toward this shameless and forbidden demanding. And that
one was not throttling the fellow-servant before the eyes of the lord, but
outside; but you in the very time of prayer, standing before the king, do
these things. And if that one, neither having exhorted the master toward the
demanding, and doing these things after going out, obtained no pardon, you,
both stirring the Master toward this forbidden payment, and doing these things
before his eyes, what punishment will you not give, tell me? But your mind is
inflamed at the memory of the enmity and swells, and the heart has risen, and
remembering the one who has grieved you you cannot settle the swelling of the
reasonings? But set against this inflammation the memory from your own sins,
the fear from the coming punishment. Remember how many things you are liable
for to the Master, and that you owe him penalties for all those, and this fear
will altogether overpower that anger, since this also is much more powerful
than that passion. Remember gehenna and punishment and vengeance at the time
of prayer, and you will not even be able to take the enemy into mind. Crush
the mind, humble the soul by the memory of the things you have transgressed,
and wrath will not even be able to trouble you. But this is the cause of all
the evils, that we examine the sins of others with much exactness, but send
our own by with much sloth. But we ought to do the contrary: to keep our own
evils unforgettable, and never to take thought of those of others. If we do
this, we shall both have God propitious, and we shall cease being immortally
angry at our neighbors, and we shall never have any enemy; and if we should
ever have one, we shall quickly also dissolve the hatred, and we shall receive
a quick forgiveness of our own sins. For just as he who remembers evil against
the neighbor does not allow the punishment upon his own sins to be dissolved,
so he who is pure of anger will quickly be pure also of sins. For if we who
are wicked and serving wrath, because of the command of God overlook all the
sins against us, much more will the One who is loving toward man and good and
pure of every passion overlook our transgressions, rendering to us in the
forgiveness of our own sins the recompense of the kindness toward the
neighbor; which may we all obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our
Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the power, unto the ages of the
ages. Amen.
