** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h1
:END:

You asked me, Lucilius, why it is that, if providence governs the world, many
evils befall good men. This would more conveniently be answered in the course
of the work as a whole, when we were proving that providence presides over the
universe and that God is concerned with us. But since it is your pleasure to
tear a particle from the whole and to settle one objection while the main
dispute remains intact, I will do a thing that is not hard: I will plead the
cause of the gods.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h2
:END:

It is superfluous for the present to show that so great a work does not stand
without some guardian, and that this gathering and racing of the stars is not
the work of a chance impulse. What chance sets in motion is often thrown into
disorder and quickly collides; this unhindered speed, carrying so great a mass
of things on land and sea, so many of the brightest lights shining from their
appointed places, proceeds under the command of eternal law. This order is not
of wandering matter, nor do things that came together at random hang with such
art that the heaviest weight of the earth sits unmoved and watches the flight
of the hurrying sky around it; that the seas poured into the valleys soften
the lands and feel no increase from the rivers; that from the smallest seeds
huge things are born.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h3
:END:

Not even those things that seem confused and uncertain (I mean rains and
clouds, the hurling of thunderbolts wrung from the sky, fires poured from the
burst peaks of mountains, the tremors of slipping ground, and the other things
that the tumultuous part of nature moves around the earth) happen without
reason, sudden though they are. They too have their own causes, no less than
those sights which, come upon in unexpected places, are a marvel: hot waters
in the midst of the waves, and new stretches of islands leaping up in the vast
sea.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h4
:END:

Indeed, if anyone has watched the shores laid bare as the sea draws back into
itself and covered again in a little while, he will think that by some blind
rolling the waves are now pulled together and driven inward, now burst out and
rush with a great course to reclaim their place. Meanwhile they grow by
measured portions and, at a set hour and day, come on larger or smaller,
according as the moon has drawn them, at whose bidding the ocean overflows.
Let those things be reserved for their own time, all the more because you do
not doubt about providence: you complain.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h5
:END:

I will restore you to good terms with the gods, who are best toward the best.
For the nature of things does not allow that what is good should ever harm
good men.
Between good men and the gods there is friendship, with virtue as the bond.
Friendship, do I say? Rather kinship and likeness, since the good man differs
from God only in time: he is his pupil and rival and true offspring, whom that
magnificent parent, no soft taskmaster of the virtues, educates more harshly,
as stern fathers do.

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1-h6
:END:

So when you see good men, accepted by the gods, toiling, sweating, climbing
the steep, while the bad run riot and flow with pleasures, think that we take
delight in the modesty of our sons and in the license of house-born
slave-children: the former are held by a sterner discipline, the latter's
boldness is fed. Let the same be clear to you about God. He does not keep the
good man among his pets. He tests him, hardens him, prepares him for himself.

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h1
:END:

"Why do many adversities befall good men?" Nothing evil can happen to a good
man; contraries do not mix. Just as so many rivers, so much rain thrown down
from above, such a force of medicinal springs do not change the taste of the
sea, do not even weaken it, so the onset of adverse things does not turn the
mind of a brave man. He remains in his station and turns whatever happens to
his own color, for he is stronger than all that is outside him.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h2
:END:

And I do not say this: that he does not feel those things, but that he
conquers them, and, otherwise quiet and calm, he rises against what rushes at
him. He counts all adversities as exercises. And who, if he is a man and
raised toward what is honorable, is not eager for just labor and ready for his
duties even when they bring danger? For whom, if he is industrious, is leisure
not a punishment?

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h3
:END:

We see athletes who care for their strength contend with the strongest of all,
and demand of those by whom they are prepared for the contest that they use
their whole force against them. They let themselves be beaten and mauled, and
if they do not find single opponents who are a match, they are thrown against
several at once. Virtue withers without an adversary.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h4
:END:

Then it appears how great she is and how much she can do, when endurance shows
what she is capable of. You may know that good men must do the same: they must
not shrink from hard and difficult things, nor complain of fate; they must
take whatever happens as good and turn it to good. What you bear does not
matter; how you bear it does.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h5
:END:

Do you not see how differently fathers and mothers indulge? Fathers order
their children to be roused to take up their studies early; they do not allow
them to be idle even on holidays, and they shake sweat and sometimes tears
from them. Mothers want to fondle them in the bosom, keep them in the shade,
never saddened, never weeping, never toiling.

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h6
:END:

God has a father's mind toward good men. He loves them stoutly, and He says:
"Let them be harried by toils, pains, losses, that they may gather true
strength." Things fattened in idleness grow slack; they fail not only from
labor but from movement and from their own weight. Unhurt prosperity does not
bear any blow. But the man whose quarrel with his own hardships has been
constant has grown a hide through injuries, and yields to no evil; even if he
has fallen, he fights from his knee.

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h7
:END:

Do you wonder if that god who most loves the good, who wants them to be as
good and as excellent as possible, assigns them a fortune with which they may
exercise? I for my part do not wonder if the gods sometimes take a desire to
watch great men wrestling with some calamity.

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h8
:END:

It is sometimes a pleasure to us if a young man of steady spirit has taken a
rushing beast on his hunting-spear, if he has borne the charge of a lion
without fear, and the spectacle is the more welcome the more honorable the man
who did it. Those are not things that can turn the faces of the gods upon
them. They are childish, the amusements of human lightness.

*** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h9
:END:

Behold a spectacle worthy for God, intent on His own work, to look back upon.
Behold a pair worthy of God: a brave man matched with bad fortune, especially
if he has even issued the challenge. I do not see, I say, what Jupiter has
more beautiful on earth, if He should wish to turn His mind there, than to
watch Cato, already with his party broken more than once, standing nonetheless
upright among the public ruins.

*** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h10
:END:

"Though," he says, "all things have passed into one man's power, though the
lands are guarded by legions, the seas by fleets, though Caesar's soldier
besieges the gates, Cato has a way out. With one hand he will make a broad
road for liberty. That sword, even in civil war clean and harmless, will at
last do good and noble work: it will give Cato the liberty it could not give
his country. Attack, my soul, the work long meditated; snatch yourself from
human affairs. Already Petreius and Juba have charged together and lie slain,
each by the other's hand. A brave and distinguished agreement of fate, but one
that does not become our greatness. It is as shameful for Cato to ask death of
anyone as life."

*** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h11
:END:

It is clear to me that the gods watched with great joy while that man, the
fiercest champion of his own cause, looks to another's safety and arranges the
flight of those who must leave; while even on his last night he pursues his
studies; while he drives the sword into his sacred breast; while he scatters
his vitals and with his hand draws out that most holy soul, unworthy to be
defiled by iron.

*** Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h12
:END:

From this I would believe the wound was not sure or effective enough. It was
not enough for the immortal gods to watch Cato once. Virtue was held back and
called again, that she might show herself in the harder part; for death is not
entered with so great a spirit as it is sought a second time. Why should they
not gladly watch their own foster-child departing by so famous and memorable
an end? Death consecrates those whose end even those who fear it praise.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h1
:END:

But now, as the speech proceeds, I will show how those things that seem evils
are not. For the present I say this: those things which you call harsh, which
you call adverse and abominable, are first for the good of those to whom they
happen, next for the good of the universe, of which the gods have a greater
care than of individuals; after this, that they happen to those who are
willing, and that they deserve the evil if they are unwilling. To these I will
add that they so happen by fate, and that they rightly come to good men by the
same law by which they are good. Then I will persuade you never to pity a good
man; for he can be called wretched, he cannot be so.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h2
:END:

The hardest of all I have proposed seems what I said first, that those things
at which we shudder and tremble are for the good of those to whom they come.
"For their good," you say, "to be thrown into exile, reduced to poverty, to
bury children and wife, to be disgraced, to be crippled?" If you wonder that
these things are for someone's good, you will wonder that some are cured by
iron and fire, and no less by hunger and thirst. But if you think with
yourself that, for the sake of a remedy, some have their bones scraped and
picked and their veins drawn out, and some limbs cut off which could not
remain without the ruin of the whole body, you will also allow this to be
proved to you: that some hardships are for the good of those to whom they
happen, as surely, by Hercules, as some things that are praised and sought are
against those they have delighted, most like indigestions and drunkenness and
the rest that kill through pleasure.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h3
:END:

Among the many magnificent sayings of our Demetrius there is also this, from
which I am still fresh; it still sounds and vibrates in my ears: "Nothing," he
says, "seems to me more unhappy than the man to whom nothing adverse has ever
happened." For he has not been allowed to test himself. Though everything has
flowed for him according to his prayer, even before the prayer, still the gods
have judged badly of him. He seemed unworthy that fortune should ever be
overcome by him. Fortune shuns the most cowardly, as if she should say: "Why
should I take that man as my adversary? He will drop his arms at once. There
is no need of my whole power against him; he will be driven off by a light
threat; he cannot bear my look. Let someone else be looked for with whom we
can come to grips. It is shameful to meet a man who is ready to be beaten."

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h4
:END:

A gladiator thinks it a disgrace to be matched with an inferior, and he knows
that the man who is beaten without danger is beaten without glory. Fortune
does the same: she seeks the bravest as her equals, and she passes some by in
disdain. She attacks the most stubborn and the most upright, against whom she
may strain her force. She tries fire in Mucius, poverty in Fabricius, exile in
Rutilius, tortures in Regulus, poison in Socrates, death in Cato. Fortune
finds no great example except when she is unkind.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h5
:END:

Is Mucius unhappy because he presses the enemy's fires with his right hand and
exacts from himself the penalty of his own error? Because, with a burned hand,
he puts to flight the king he could not with an armed one? What then? Would he
be happier if he were warming his hand in a mistress's bosom?

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h6
:END:

Is Fabricius unhappy because he digs his own farm, in what time he had free
from public business? Because he wages war as much with Pyrrhus as with
riches? Because he dines at his hearth on those very roots and herbs which the
old man, after his triumphs, pulled up while clearing his field? What then?
Would he be happier if he were heaping into his belly fish from a distant
shore and foreign game; if he were rousing the laziness of a nauseous stomach
with shellfish of the upper and lower sea; if he were ringing first-rate wild
beasts, taken with much slaughter of hunters, with a huge pile of fruit?

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h7
:END:

Is Rutilius unhappy because those who condemned him will plead their case
through all ages? Because he suffered himself to be torn from his country with
a more even mind than exile from himself? Because he alone refused something
to Sulla the dictator, and when recalled he not only did not come back, he
fled farther? "Let them see to it," he said, "those whom your prosperity has
caught at Rome. Let them see the lavish blood in the Forum, and above the
Servilian pool (for that is the stripping-room of Sulla's proscription) the
heads of senators, and the packs of assassins wandering everywhere through the
city, and many thousands of Roman citizens butchered in one place after a
pledge, no, through the pledge itself. Let those see these things who cannot
go into exile." What then?

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h8
:END:

Is Lucius Sulla happy because the sword makes a way for him as he goes down to
the Forum, because he allows the heads of consular men to be shown to him and
pays the price of slaughter through a quaestor and the public accounts? And he
does all this, he who carried the Cornelian law!

*** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h9
:END:

Let us come to Regulus: what harm did fortune do him, in that she made him a
proof of good faith, a proof of endurance? Nails pierce his skin, and wherever
he has leaned his wearied body, he lies upon a wound; his eyes are stretched
in endless waking. The more the torment, the more the glory. Do you want to
know how little he regrets having valued virtue at this price? Restore him and
send him into the Senate; he will give the same opinion.

*** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h10
:END:

Do you then think Maecenas happier, who, anxious with loves and weeping the
daily divorces of a peevish wife, seeks sleep through the song of concerts
sounding softly from far off? He may steep himself in wine and call himself
away with the crash of waters and cheat an anxious mind with a thousand
pleasures: he will be as wakeful on down as the other on the cross. But for
the one it is a comfort to bear hard things for what is honorable, and he
looks back from his endurance to the cause; the other, limp with pleasures and
laboring under too much prosperity, is more harassed by the cause of his
suffering than by what he suffers.

*** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h11
:END:

Vices have not come so far into possession of the human race that it should be
doubtful whether, if a choice of fate were given, more would wish to be born a
Regulus than a Maecenas. Or if there should be anyone who dares to say he
would have preferred to be born Maecenas rather than Regulus, that same man,
though he keep silent, preferred to be born Terentia!

*** Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h12
:END:

Do you judge Socrates badly treated because he drained that draught mixed by
the state just as if it were a medicine of immortality, and discussed death up
to death itself? Was he badly used because his blood was chilled and, as the
cold gradually came on, the vigor of his veins stood still?

*** Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h13
:END:

How much more is he to be envied than those who are served from a jeweled cup,
for whom a catamite, trained to endure all things, castrated or of doubtful
sex, dissolves snow hung in gold! These will vomit back whatever they have
drunk, gloomy and tasting their bile again; but he will drink the poison glad
and willing.

*** Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h14
:END:

As for Cato, enough has been said, and the consensus of men will confess that
the highest happiness fell to him, whom nature chose as the one with whom she
might contend in things to be feared. "The enmities of the powerful are heavy;
let him be set against Pompey, Caesar, and Crassus at once. It is heavy to be
passed in honor by worse men; let him be put after Vatinius. It is heavy to
take part in civil wars; let him serve throughout the whole world for a good
cause as unluckily as stubbornly. It is heavy to lay hands on oneself; let him
do it. What shall I gain by these things? That all may know that those things
are not evils by which I thought Cato worthy."

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h1
:END:

Prosperous things come down even to the crowd and to cheap natures; but to put
the calamities and terrors of mortals under the yoke is the proper work of a
great man. Always to be fortunate and to pass through life without a bite of
the mind is to be ignorant of the other half of the nature of things.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h2
:END:

You are a great man; but how do I know, if fortune does not give you the
chance of showing virtue? You have gone down to Olympia, but no one besides
you. You have a crown; you do not have a victory. I do not congratulate you as
a brave man, but as one who has obtained a consulship or a praetorship. You
have been raised in office.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h3
:END:

I can say the same even to a good man, if a harder chance has given him no
occasion in which he might show the force of his mind: "I judge you wretched
because you have never been wretched. You have passed through life without an
adversary. No one will know what you could have done, not even you yourself."
For knowledge of oneself needs a trial. What each man could do he has not
learned except by trying. Therefore some have of their own accord offered
themselves to evils that were hanging back, and have sought for virtue that
was about to go into obscurity an occasion by which she might shine.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h4
:END:

Great men, I say, sometimes rejoice in adverse things, just as brave soldiers
do in war. I heard Triumphus the heavily armed gladiator, in the time of
Tiberius Caesar, complaining of the rarity of shows: "How fair an age is going
to waste!" he said. Virtue is greedy of danger, and she thinks of where she is
heading, not of what she will suffer, since even what she will suffer is a
part of glory. Military men glory in wounds, and gladly display blood flowing
from a luckier wound. Though those who return from the line unhurt have done
the same, the man who comes back wounded is more looked at.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h5
:END:

God, I say, looks to the interest of those themselves whom He wishes to be as
honorable as possible, whenever He offers them material for doing something
with spirit and courage, for which some difficulty of things is needed. You
would know a steersman in a storm, a soldier in the line. How can I know how
much spirit you have against poverty, if you overflow with riches? How can I
know how much constancy you have against disgrace and infamy and popular
hatred, if you grow old among applause, if an unconquerable favor follows you,
a favor that leans your way by a certain bent of minds? How do I know how
evenly you would bear the loss of children, if you see all those you have
raised? I have heard you when you were consoling others. Then I would have
seen you, if you had consoled yourself, if you had forbidden yourself to
grieve.

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h6
:END:

Do not, I beg you, grow pale at those things which the immortal gods apply as
goads to minds. Calamity is the occasion of virtue. Those men one might justly
call wretched who grow numb with too much prosperity, whom, as on a sluggish
sea, an idle calm holds. Whatever happens to them will come as something new.

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h7
:END:

Cruel things press more on the untried; the yoke is heavy on a tender neck. At
the suspicion of a wound the recruit grows pale; the veteran boldly watches
his own blood, who knows that he has often conquered after blood. Those,
therefore, whom God tests, whom He loves, He hardens, inspects, exercises. But
those whom He seems to indulge, whom He seems to spare, He keeps soft for
coming evils. For you err if you judge anyone exempt. His portion will come
even to that man long fortunate. Whoever seems to have been let off has been
put off. Why does God afflict the best of all either with bad health or with
mourning or with other hardships?

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h8
:END:

Because in camp as well the dangerous things are ordered to the bravest. The
general sends the choicest to attack the enemy by night ambush, or to scout
the road, or to dislodge a garrison from a place. None of those who go out
says, "The commander has deserved ill of me," but "He has judged well."
Likewise let those say who are ordered to suffer things tearful to the timid
and the cowardly: "We have seemed worthy to God, in whom He might try how much
human nature could suffer."

*** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h9
:END:

Flee luxuries, flee the enervating prosperity in which minds grow soaked, and
unless something comes between that reminds them of the human lot, they remain
as if lulled in a perpetual drunkenness. The man whom window-panes have always
protected from a draft, whose feet have been kept warm among fomentations
often changed, whose dining-rooms a heat laid underneath and poured around the
walls has tempered: a light breeze will not graze him without danger.

*** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h10
:END:

Since all things that have exceeded measure do harm, the most dangerous is the
intemperance of prosperity. It stirs the brain, calls up empty images in
minds, pours much of a mist midway between false and true. Why should it not
be better to bear perpetual misfortune with virtue called to help, than to be
burst by infinite and immoderate goods? Death is milder from fasting; they
split from indigestion.

*** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h11
:END:

The gods therefore follow this plan with good men which teachers follow with
their pupils, who exact more labor from those in whom the hope is more sure.
Do you believe that the Spartans hate their own children, whose mettle they
test by applying the lash in public? The fathers themselves urge them to bear
the strokes of the whips bravely, and ask them, torn and half-dead, to persist
in offering wounds to wounds.

*** Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h12
:END:

What wonder if God tests generous spirits harshly? There is never a soft proof
of virtue. Fortune beats us and tears us; let us bear it. It is not cruelty,
it is a contest, and the more often we have entered it, the braver we shall
be. The solidest part of the body is that which frequent use has worked. We
must offer ourselves to fortune, that we may be hardened against her by her.
Little by little she will make us her equals. Constancy in being endangered
will give contempt of dangers.

*** Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h13
:END:

So sailors' bodies are hard for bearing the sea, farmers' hands are worn,
soldiers' arms are strong for hurling weapons, runners' limbs are agile. That
in each man is solidest which he has exercised. By endurance the mind comes to
scorn the suffering of evils. What it can effect in us you will know if you
look at how much labor supplies to naked nations, stronger through want.

*** Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h14
:END:

Consider all the peoples where the Roman peace ends. I mean the Germans and
whatever of wandering tribes meets us around the Danube. Perpetual winter, a
grim sky, presses them; a sterile soil supports them stingily. They keep off
the rain with straw or leaf, they leap over pools frozen hard with ice, they
hunt wild beasts for food.

*** Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h15
:END:

Do they seem wretched to you? Nothing is wretched that custom has brought over
into nature; for little by little those things become a pleasure which began
by necessity. They have no dwellings and no seats except those which weariness
has set for the day. Even this cheap food must be sought by the hand, a
dreadful unfairness of sky, bodies uncovered. This which seems to you a
calamity is the life of so many nations. Why do you wonder that good men are
shaken, that they may be strengthened?

*** Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h16
:END:

No tree is solid or strong except the one against which a frequent wind
rushes. By the very harassing it is tightened and sets its roots more surely.
Those that have grown in a sunny valley are fragile. It is therefore for the
good of good men themselves that they may be able to be unafraid, that they
spend much time among things to be feared and bear with an even mind those
things that are not evils except to the one who bears them badly.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h1
:END:

Add now that it is for the good of all that the best, so to speak, should
serve and do their work. This is the purpose of God, as of the wise man: to
show that these things which the crowd seeks, which it dreads, are neither
goods nor evils. But they will appear to be goods if He assigns them only to
good men, and to be evils if He inflicts them only on the bad.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h2
:END:

Blindness will be detestable if no one has lost his eyes except the man from
whom they ought to be torn out. Therefore let Appius and Metellus lack the
light. Riches are not a good; therefore let Elius the pander have them too,
that men may see money, when they have consecrated it in temples, also in a
brothel. In no way can God more expose the things that are coveted than if He
carries them to the most shameful and drives them from the best.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h3
:END:

"But it is unjust that a good man should be weakened or pierced or bound,
while the bad walk with sound bodies, free and luxurious." What then? Is it
not unjust that brave men should take up arms and pass the night in camp and
stand before the rampart with bound-up wounds, while in the city the castrated
and those who have professed unchastity are at ease? What then? Is it not
unjust that the noblest virgins should be roused at night to perform the
rites, while the polluted enjoy the deepest sleep?

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h4
:END:

Labor summons the best. The Senate is often consulted through the whole day,
while at that time the cheapest sort either amuse their leisure in the Field
of Mars, or hide in a cookshop, or waste time in some knot of talkers. The
same happens in this great commonwealth. Good men toil, they spend, they are
spent, and willingly too. They are not dragged by fortune; they follow her and
keep step. If they had known, they would have gone ahead.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h5
:END:

I remember that I have heard this spirited saying too of Demetrius, that
bravest of men: "This one thing," he said, "I can complain of you, immortal
gods, that you did not make your will known to me before; for I would have
come first to those things to which I now come when called. Do you want to
take my children? I have raised them for you. Do you want some part of my
body? Take it. I do not promise a great thing; I shall soon leave the whole.
Do you want my breath? Why not? I will make no delay, that you may recover
what you have given. You will take from a willing man whatever you ask."

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h6
:END:

"What then is it? I would have preferred to offer than to hand over. What need
was there to take away? You could have received. But not even now will you
take away, because nothing is snatched except from one who holds on." I am
compelled to nothing, I suffer nothing unwillingly, and I am not a slave to
God but I assent, all the more because I know that all things run by a fixed
law, spoken for eternity.

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h7
:END:

The fates lead us, and how much time remains for each the first hour of those
being born has arranged. Cause hangs from cause; a long order of things draws
private lives and public ones. Therefore everything must be borne bravely,
because not, as we think, do all things fall upon us, but they come. Long ago
it was settled what you should rejoice at, what you should weep at, and though
life seems to be distinguished by a great variety of individuals, the sum
comes to one. We who are to perish receive things that are to perish. Why then
are we indignant?

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h8
:END:

Why do we complain? We were born for this. Let nature use her bodies as she
will. Let us, glad and brave toward all things, think that nothing of ours is
perishing. What is it to be a good man? To offer oneself to fate. It is a
great comfort to be swept away with the universe. Whatever it is that has
ordered us so to live, so to die, binds the gods by the same necessity. An
irrevocable course carries human and divine alike. That very founder and ruler
of all things has indeed written the fates, but He follows them. He always
obeys; He commanded once.

*** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h9
:END:

"Why then was God so unfair in the distribution of fate as to assign to good
men poverty and wounds and bitter deaths?" The craftsman cannot change his
material; this is what the material allowed. Some things cannot be parted from
others; they cohere, they are indivisible. Slack natures, about to go into
sleep or into a waking most like sleep, are bound to sluggish elements. That a
man may be made who deserves the name in earnest, a stronger fate is needed.
His road will not be level. He must go up and down, be tossed, and steer his
ship in the rough. Against fortune he must hold his course. Many hard and
rough things will happen, but he himself will soften and level them.

*** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h10
:END:

Fire tests gold, misery tests brave men. See how high virtue must climb. You
will know that she must not go through safe places:

/
The first stretch is steep, and one which the horses, fresh at dawn,
scarcely struggle up. Mid-course it is highest in the sky,
from which to look on sea and lands is often a fear even to me,
and my breast trembles with timid dread.
The last stretch runs downhill and needs a sure hand on the reins;
then even she who receives me in the waters below,
Tethys, is used to fearing the depths, lest I be carried headlong.
/

*** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h11
:END:

When that high-born young man had heard this, "The way pleases me," he said.
"I climb. It is worth it to go through those things, even if I am to fall."
The Sun does not cease to terrify the keen spirit with fear:

/
And that you may hold the road and not be drawn into any wandering,
you will still go through the horns of the opposing bull,
and the Haemonian bow, and the mouth of the violent lion.
/

After this he says: "Yoke the chariot you have given. By these things with
which you think I am being frightened I am spurred. I want to stand there
where the Sun himself trembles." It belongs to a low and idle spirit to hunt
what is safe. Virtue goes through the heights.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h1
:END:

"Why then does God allow anything evil to happen to good men?" He does not
allow it. He has removed all evils from them: crimes and shameful acts and
wicked thoughts and greedy plans and blind lust and avarice that hangs over
another's goods. He watches over them and protects them. Does anyone demand
this too of God, that He should keep even the baggage of good men? They
themselves let God off this care: they despise externals.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h2
:END:

Democritus threw away his riches, thinking them a burden to a good mind. Why
then do you wonder if God allows that to happen to a good man which a good man
sometimes wishes to happen to himself? Good men lose sons; why not, when
sometimes they even kill them? They are sent into exile; why not, when
sometimes they themselves leave their country, never to return? They are
killed; why not, when sometimes they themselves lay hands on themselves? Why
do they suffer some hard things? That they may teach others to suffer.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h3
:END:

They were born for an example. Imagine therefore that God says: "What have you
that you can complain of me, you to whom what is right has been pleasing? To
others I have wrapped false goods, and I have mocked empty minds as with a
long and deceitful dream. I have adorned them with gold and silver and ivory;
within there is nothing good."

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h4
:END:

"Those whom you look on as fortunate, if you see them not as they meet you but
as they are hidden, are wretched, sordid, ugly, like their own walls,
cultivated on the outside. That is not a solid and genuine happiness. It is a
crust, and a thin one at that. Therefore while they are allowed to stand and
to be shown at their own will, they shine and deceive. When something happens
that disturbs and uncovers, then it appears how much of a deep and true
foulness an alien splendor had hidden."

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h5
:END:

"To you I have given goods that are sure, that will remain, the more anyone
turns them and looks at them from every side, the better and greater. I have
allowed you to despise things to be feared, to disdain desires. You do not
shine outwardly; your goods are turned inward. So the world has despised
externals, glad in the spectacle of itself. I have placed every good within.
Not to need prosperity is your prosperity."

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h6
:END:

"'But many sad, dreadful, hard-to-bear things happen.' Because I could not
withdraw you from those things, I have armed your minds against all things.
Bear them bravely. This is the point in which you go ahead of God. He is
outside the endurance of evils; you are above endurance. Despise poverty: no
one lives so poor as he was born. Despise pain: it will either cease or it
will release you. Despise death: it either ends you or transfers you. Despise
fortune: I have given her no weapon with which she might strike the mind."

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h7
:END:

"Above all I have taken care that nothing should hold you against your will.
The way out is open. If you do not want to fight, you may flee. Therefore of
all the things I wished to be necessary for you I have made nothing easier
than to die. I have placed the soul in a sloping place; it is drawn out. Only
attend, and you will see how short and how unimpeded a road leads to liberty.
I have not set you so long delays in going out as in coming in. Otherwise
fortune would have held a great kingdom over you, if a man died as slowly as
he is born."

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h8
:END:

"Let every time, every place teach you how easy it is to renounce nature and
to fling her own gift back at her. Among the very altars and the solemn rites
of those sacrificing, while life is being prayed for, learn death. The fat
bodies of bulls fall by a slight wound, and a blow of a human hand lays low
animals of great strength. The joining of the neck is broken by a thin iron,
and when that joint which joins head and neck has been cut, that great bulk
collapses."

*** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h9
:END:

"The breath does not lie hidden in the deep, nor must it in any case be dug
out with iron. The vitals need not be searched with a wound pressed deep:
death is close at hand. I have not appointed a fixed place for these blows;
wherever you wish, there is a way through. That very thing which is called
dying, by which the soul departs from the body, is too brief for so great a
speed to be felt. Whether a knot has crushed the throat, or water has shut off
the breath, or the hardness of the ground beneath has crushed those who have
fallen on their heads, or the draught of fire has cut the course of the
returning breath: whatever it is, it hurries. Do you even blush? What is done
so quickly, you fear for so long!"
