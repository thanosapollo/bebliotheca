in them, except to be glad, and to do good in his life." The wise man knows
the good that is by nature. And by nature the good is God alone, who makes all
things by goodness. He therefore who does good as far as is attainable, and
keeps the love toward God and toward the neighbor, this man is glad with the

good. "And indeed every man who shall eat and drink, and see good in all his
toil, this is a gift of God." The wise man knew what the truly good is; but
every man, that is, the many and common man, having slipped from the knowledge
of the truly good, defining the good in pleasure, takes eating and drinking to
be good. And that the supply of sensible things is given to men from God is
evident; not, however, that we should abuse the things supplied by God, but
that we may be sociable, and ready to share in measure, and partake with
thanksgiving of the things given by God. He has therefore woven together the
persons of the wise man, both of him who properly knows the good, and of him
who has the knowledge of the good in a wandering way. "I knew that all things
whatsoever God has made, they shall be unto the age; upon them there is no
adding, and from it there is no taking away, and God has made them, that they
may fear from his face." Of the things that are, some are visible, and some
invisible. And the word now is about the visible; and of these some are always
the same, as earth, and heaven, and sun, and moon; and some remain the same by
successions, as man, and plants, and the like. I knew then, he says, that
there is nothing to add to the ordering of God, which is perfectly and without
lack; nor yet to take away any of all things as superfluous. And those who
apply themselves to the contemplation of the things that have come to be, and
find the reason of each as far as possible, are held by divine fear, and come
to astonishment, marveling at the wisdom of the maker of generation. There is
therefore laid up by nature in the common notions a fear of God, or a law from
the things that have come to be


harmony, stirring the faithful up to the power of him who made them. And one
must know that the things seen happen to be symbols of certain intelligibles;
so that according to the reason for which they have come to be, they are
without lack. For whether something has come to be primarily by God, or
according to a second reason, there is no adding, and from them there is no
taking away. All things then are fair, all the works of God are perfect and
not superfluous. =93.520= "That which has come to be already is; and as many
things as are of coming to be have already come to be; and God will seek the
one who is pursued." If anything, he says, has come to be before, it now is;
man has come to be, man is; a plant has come to be, a plant is; but also the
things that are going to come to be have already come to be. Men are going to
come to be after us, already others have come to be before them. And the, God
will seek the one who is pursued, he will seek also him who is not working
harm, and will save him. "And still I saw under the sun a place of judgment,
there the ungodly; and a place of the just, there the pious. I said I in my
heart: God will judge with the just and with the ungodly, because there is a
time for every work." Leading us up from the sensible to the intelligible he
teaches that by foreknowledge and with a pure eye of mind he contemplated the
place of the judgment of the ungodly as being under the sun, that he might say
in Hades; but he says he has also contemplated the place of the just. And he
added upon this, under the sun. If this be understood simply, it is quite
clear that the just are going to be in paradise and in the heavens. But if
under the sun be taken in common, both of the just and of the ungodly, he
contemplated those who have now departed from life, not yet having received
the kingdom of the heavens. And it is fitting to seek where under the sun the
pious happen to be. It is quite clear that they are in paradise, according to
the Saviour who said to the robber: Today you shall be with me in paradise.
And one must know that the history teaches paradise to be upon earth. And some
have said that paradise also happens to be in Hades. Therefore he says, And
the rich man saw Lazarus, but he himself happening to be somewhere below,
contemplated him somewhere above with Abraham. However these things may stand,
we are taught both from the present saying and from all the divine Scripture
that the pious man is in well-being, and the unjust in the punishments that
correspond. And to others it has seemed that paradise is in heaven; but the
simple Ecclesiast will rather follow the history. Then also examining together
he adds, he says, that "I said by myself: Can it be that with the ungodly the
just also is condemned?" and this he said inquiringly, not declaratively. For
once he has taught that the place of the just is other, and that of the
ungodly other; then he reminds us again, leading us into fear, that God will
bring all into judgment, and will examine thoroughly all our affairs. For
there is a time, he says, for every affair, and upon every work. i.e., There
will be also the time of the universal judgment. And toward the sense you will
understand thus: I saw under the Sun of righteousness our Lord Jesus Christ,
that he comes to judge living and dead, the place of the ungodly, the standing
on the left; and the place of the just, that on the right; and I said I in my
heart, that God will bring all into judgment, assigning to each according to
desert, having appointed that time in which every affair =93.521= of ours and
work will be examined with exactness. "There I said I in my heart, concerning
the speech of the sons of man, that God will distinguish them." Like that in
the Gospel, that Every idle word which men shall speak, they shall give
account concerning it in the day of judgment. And understand speech for me as
also the very thought, since we are required to give account also of the
thoughts. "And to show that they themselves are cattle, and indeed encounters
of the sons of man, and an encounter of the beast; one encounter for them. As
the death of this, so the death of this. And one spirit in all; and man has
nothing more than the beast." For this reason, he says, I saw and was made
wise by God in the reasons of the judgment, that I may exhibit, that is, that
I may teach men, that they differ in nothing from cattle, according to the
reason, clearly, of the body and of the body-loving life. For we all breathe
the same


air as the cattle, we undergo the same death, and in this man has nothing more
than the beast. Therefore the beastlike and flesh-loving life is vanity. And
from these things the Ecclesiast teaches us to attend not to our flesh, but to
the rational essence, according to which we have communion with the angels;
for according to our sensible essence we are like the cattle; but according to
the invisible and rational, like the holy angels. "All things go to one place;
all things have come to be from the dust; and all things return to the dust.
And who knows the spirit of the sons of man, whether it goes up into the
things above, and the spirit of the beast, whether it goes down below into the
earth?" Manichees, saying transmigration of souls, say that the immortal soul
puts on all bodies, both of rational things, and of irrational, and of plants,
and of creeping things. But Christians, being trained from the divine
Scriptures, are taught to understand cattle as the more irrational, those
compared to the cattle that have no understanding, and men, by contrast with
the more beastlike, as the more rational. Who then knows if those who seem
among the many to be more rational, in the time of the judgment are counted
worthy of the kingdom of the heavens, and are not cast down into the things
below? Or also the reverse, if those who seem to be more irrational are not
taken up above according to the unattainable judgments of God? For that the
soul of man is immortal, and that of the irrational perishes together with the
body, is quite evident to everyone who even in a small way partakes of reason.
Otherwise: In many places the Ecclesiast putting on the person of the
flesh-lover, as from that person puts forward the words, saying: Who knows the
things that will be? Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die. And making
clear the things concerning the life here the Apostle =93.524= said: If in
this life only we have hoped in Christ, we are more pitiable than all men.
Everywhere therefore the Ecclesiast, teaching us this through riddles, sends
us on to another life. "And I saw that there is no good, except that a man
shall be glad in his works; because this is his portion; because who will
bring him to see in what may come to be after him?" If the word is from the
person of the flesh-lover, you will understand thus: I saw, says such a one,
that there is nothing good in life, except to be glad in one's own works and
wills; and this is the portion and good lot of man. Then the Ecclesiast
teaches from where such thoughts arise for the body-lovers; because, he says,
no one can show to these, whose mind has been blinded, the things that are
going to be and that come to be after these, that is, the eternal punishments.
But if the word is from the Wise man, you will contemplate more highly. There
is no good, he says, for a man, except the gladness that is going to succeed
him, from his good works here. For the action that gladdens him, that is, the
virtuous, this will be his portion. For it is not possible for him who does
not live according to virtue, after the life here, to return again and come to
repentance. Chapter 4. "And I turned I, and I saw the calumnies that come to
be under the sun; and behold a tear of those who are calumniated, and there is
not one who comforts them, and from the hand of those who calumniate them
there is strength, and there is not one who comforts them." I turned, he says,
to another examination, and contemplating men, I beheld the calumniators doing
injustice, and those who are calumniated weeping from inability, because no
one strengthens them, and by defending them makes comfort for them. And some
calumniate for money, and some for doctrines, as the heretics the orthodox.
And the first-evil demon also, and the apostate powers, always calumniate,
lying. "And I praised I those who have died, those already dead, who
themselves live until now. And better than these two is he who has not yet
come to be, who has not known [with] the evil work that has been done under
the sun." He blesses those who have died above those who live. Why? Because
the just have ceased from the labors; and the sinners have been cut off from
the impulse


toward sinning. For the common death is brought upon both to their benefit.
And toward the sense, Those who have died =93.525= to sin, and live in Christ,
are incomparably better than those living in sins. Then he adds: "And better
than these two is he who has not yet come to be," nor has he seen this
toilsome life, which indeed he also calls a work. From here some say that the
soul exists before the body. For how, he says, is it possible for him who does
not even exist to be called good? to whom we will say: If the soul existing
beforehand, because of sin in the bodiless way of life, was cast into the body
as into a prison, that it might be trained (for these things you say), how was
she blessed who was sinning and not being trained? And yet whom the Lord
loves, he trains, and he scourges every son whom he receives. And if to be
loved by God is this good, and to be trained is good, and the soul comes into
a body that it may be trained, then the life in the body is good above that of
the soul sinning in the pre-life, and not counted worthy of divine turning.
This word then has been overturned, nor did the soul exist before the body.
The good then above these two, whoever has not yet come to be, he says
concerning the man who has not even come into existence at all, that he may
say according to the word of the Saviour: It were well for him if he had not
come to be. And from here another question succeeds us, if not to come to be
is better than to come to be, then coming into existence is for evil for men;
then the one who fashioned man is cause of evils. How then did God see all
things whatsoever he made, and behold they were very good? To this then we
say, that the Ecclesiast having set two persons, of the one calumniating, and
of the one being calumniated, has not simply set the person of the one being
calumniated, but has set under him a tear, for the things in which he is
wronged. Therefore the person introduced is not of a philosopher, nor of a
true Christian, but of someone himself also world-loving. For the philosopher
being wronged does not weep, but the contrary, he rejoices and is pleased; as
also the Apostle wrote: And you received the plunder of your goods with joy.
The Ecclesiast then having set under two unphilosophical persons, one of the
one doing injustice, and the other of the one being wronged, and weeping from
too great distress and lack of training, added, that in comparison with these
two unphilosophical men, it is well not even to come into existence; and the
very experience of the affairs will bear witness to what has been said. For if
someone should ask the holy martyrs, or prophets, or apostles, or also all who
have lived in virtue: Tell us, holy men, a choice being laid before you of
coming to be by God, or not coming to be, what would you have chosen? do you
not think they would answer at once, that Indeed we have thanks to the God who
has made us by goodness, and anointed us for the contests of virtue, and woven
for us these blessed crowns? It has been shown then that the word of the
Ecclesiast is about the sinners. =93.528= "And I saw I all the toil, and all
the manliness of the work, that it is zeal for a man, this is vanity and
choice of spirit." Blameworthy manliness, to set oneself upon the vain things,
and to prevail in the aim. And the blameworthy zeal is that which has envy as
neighbor. I saw then, he says, the toil of men about vain works, and others
zealously imitating them, or envying. For example someone grows rich from
injustice, others envying this man, or also wishing to obtain the like,
imitate the same manner. And the things about these then are vanity, and
choice of spirit. "A fool folded his hands, and ate his own flesh." The hands
have been given to men for working the things that are due. He then who holds
his own hands, that is, the idle man, later at some time repenting or also
wasting away, is said to eat his own flesh. And toward the sense, He who does
not unfold the energies of the soul about the virtues eats his own flesh,
becoming for himself a procurer of punishment. Otherwise: To fold is said also
of loving and embracing. The fool then, loving his own accused deeds, eats his
own flesh, himself preparing for himself the punishment from the just


punishment. "A good fullness of a handful with rest, above a fullness of two
handfuls with toil and choice of spirit." "Better a little for the just above
much wealth of sinners." And toward the sense, a handful is the faith without
lack, that toward the Lord Jesus Christ. And two handfuls, both to serve God
and mammon, and to be reproached by the prophet saying, How long will you limp
upon both hams? The former then is unambiguously good. Otherwise: One handful
is the immaterial unity; two handfuls, the material things that are divided.
And the dyad is also unclean. For the unclean went into the ark by twos. It is
good then to pursue the unity, and to wear the life according to virtue with
rest, that is, without vainglory; rather than to be drawn about the dyad, that
is, the material things. Otherwise: Someone contemplated the man as a handful,
since God taking dust from the earth, that is, taking a handful, fashioned
man. And a handful is also the woman; for from the side of Adam God taking a
handful fashioned her. Good then, he says, is unmarried life above the two
handfuls, that is, pairing and marriage. For even if the second is not
blameworthy, yet the first is praiseworthy and without toil. =93.529= "And I
turned I, and I saw vanity under the sun." In a circle, he says, carrying
about the eye of the heart, I saw nothing else being practiced of the things
under the sun for the sake of which it is not vanity; and he means the evil
works. "There is one, and there is not a second, and indeed a son, and indeed
a brother, there is no end to all his toil; and indeed his eye is not filled
with wealth; and for whom do I toil, and deprive my soul of goodness? and
indeed this is vanity, and an evil distraction." Still, he says, looking round
at human things, I saw some not even having the heirs from the law, and being
insatiably disposed about wealth, having an empty and unfilled eye. Then
having contemplated this, he introduces the person of this insatiable one
coming into repentance, and saying, And for whom do I toil, and deprive my
soul of goodness? And he also attaches to this toil the vain. Otherwise:
Toward the sense, There is one. He says one the self-lover, who does not wish
to have any brother, or son. For to those who wish, and live according to God,
even if they do not happen to be kin according to flesh, yet all are brothers
and sons to them; according to the, Become to orphans as a father, and in
place of a husband to their mother. This one then, who wishing to have no one
either as brother or as son, through a wretched choice deprives his soul of
goodness, neither reaching after the coming goods, nor doing anything good in
life. The vain then is attached also to this one. "The two are good above the
one, who have a good wage in their toil." He praises the concord of the two,
that which comes to be for good; for this also happens to be with wage from
God. And the Saviour well sent the apostles agreeing two by two. But those who
agreed badly in the tower-building God divided. And toward the sense: Good is
the agreement of soul and body upon the fair; and it is also with wage from
God. And how are the two good above the one? If the body is temperate, but the
soul is vainglorious, the temperance of the body is vain. And conversely, If
the soul is humble-minded, but the body fornicates, the humility is vain. For
when one part sins, soul or body, necessarily the other also is included.
"Good then is the agreement of the two, unto the wage-bearing reason of
virtue, because if they fall, the one will raise his partner. And woe to the
one when he falls, and there is not a second to raise him." Those who agree
for good are a help to one another. For even if the one as a man overlooks
something of the things due, he will have the other setting it right. And the,
when they fall, =93.532= not both at the same time, but sometimes this one,
and sometimes that. "And indeed if the two sleep, they also have warmth, and
the one how shall he be warmed?" From what happens bodily he teaches something
spiritual. For those who sleep according to pairing more than the one who is
alone


are warmed; so also those who agree according to spirit; For where two or
three are gathered into my name, there I am in the midst of them, says the
Lord, granting clearly the warmth of the Holy Spirit. "And if the one be
overpowered, the two also will stand against him." Again from the things that
happen in the senses he teaches us a spiritual theorem. For just as if in a
marketplace or in a desert, two walking of one soul, someone should come upon
them, the two happen to be safer for beating off the one who has come upon
them; so you will understand also according to the contemplation. For having
taught the things about soul and body, since the Apostle mentions three
saying: May your spirit and the soul be kept entire; he sets out the things of
the theorem more completely, and says, that if the one be overpowered, that
is, the mind of the flesh, the soul joined to the spirit bridles the body, so
that it also obeys the spiritual motions of the soul. And one must know that
man is said to be composed of three, soul, and body, and spirit, the spirit
not being something other besides the soul according to essence; for there are
not two rational things in man; but

according to conception we say the spirit is other besides the soul, calling
spirit that which is more easily moved and higher than the soul. This, I
think, is also the mind, as we noted in Esdras. Or also thus, Spirit is the
spiritual gift that illuminates and makes bright our soul; which we received
from the divine inbreathing. And keeping the according-to-image, we have
entire also the spiritual gift; but sinning we are fined of the gift. This
then, I think, the Apostle prays, that we remain entire, as we came to be from
the beginning, having a pure body and soul, and preserving pure the spiritual
gift. Otherwise: The motions of the soul are divided into the rational, and
the spirited, and the desiring. If then these agree praiseworthily, and the
spirited is moved according to nature against passions and demons, and the
desire reaching after the better things. For which also Daniel was called a
man of desires. And the rational does each thing with the just, driving the
spirited and the desire with good art. But if it happen through the pleasing
of nature that the spirited or the desire be moved disorderly, the two
remaining pure and agreeing, the rational, for example, and the desiring
prevail over the spirited; or also =93.533= the reverse. Thus then the two
agreeing well enslave the one acting against them badly. "And the cord of
three strands will not quickly be broken." Again from the triple-plaited rope
he sends on spiritual theorems; and first he teaches love, binding us into
concord. Soul, and body, and spirit agreeing are not quickly broken by the
assault of the enemy. Otherwise: The rational, and spirited, and desiring,
being purified in unity, preserve the soul unharmed. Otherwise: Those who
believe in the consubstantial Trinity, Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit,
happen to be unconquerable to those who stand against them. "A poor and wise
child is good above an elder and foolish king, who did not know to take heed
still; because from the house of prisoners he will go out to be king, because
indeed in his kingdom he became poor." The child according to appearance is
not so honorable as the old man; nor yet the poor as the king. Comparing then
wisdom and folly, the incomparable of wisdom clearly, he says that it is well
to be both a child and poor, yet wise, rather than an old man and a king, but
a fool. For the fool did not recognize that wisdom is able, even being a
household servant, and a prisoner, to show a king. And the proof is from
Joseph, whom being both young, and having become bought with silver, and a
prisoner, the God-given wisdom that was with him showed as ruler of Egypt. And
this wisdom prepares the wise man when he is king to be moderate-minded, and
neither to be lifted up, nor to be puffed up from the dignity. For he who is
poor according to God, neither in palaces, nor in abundance of wealth, stands
away from being poor, being blessed and poor in spirit. Otherwise: Of the


elder and foolish people of the Jews, the younger people from the nations,
being wise, and delivered from the diabolic bonds, through the faith toward
Christ, has become a royal priesthood; poor in spirit being blessed in the
Gospels. And a type of such poor has become our Lord Jesus Christ; being the
wisdom of the Father, but young in the age according to flesh, and being king
according to the divine dignity, but becoming poor for us though being rich,
and going out from the bonds of death, having undone the prison of Hades, and
teaching us by the example to be moderate-minded. Through which he said: Learn
from me, that I am meek, and humble in heart. =93.536= "I saw all the living,
those walking under the sun, with the second young man, who shall arise in his
place; there is no end to all the people, to all who came to be before them.
And indeed the last shall not be glad in him; and indeed this is vanity, and
choice of spirit;" the whole saying looking toward allegory. For no one can
see all men with sensible eyes. And the interpretation of the saying being
carried this way and that (for some have taken the second young man as the
Lord Jesus Christ, and some as the Antichrist); we will therefore set beside
each interpretation. I saw, he says, all the living, those walking under the
sensible sun, that is, the sinners, with the second young man; and he means
the Antichrist, who shall arise in his place; he says, in place of our Lord
Jesus Christ. And there was no end of the multitude of the sinners, in place
of those who have come to be from the age. But also the last, that is, those
in the presence of the Antichrist, believing in him, shall not be glad in him,
nor will ungodliness save those who are

Otherwise: He says he knows those under the sun of righteousness, that is,
living a true life. And this is the second young man from heaven, who arose in
place of the first, the earthy from earth; and he means Adam. And they were
without end, he says, those journeying under this young man before themselves,
that is, forgetting the things behind, and stretching forward to the things

know, because of the things said before, that Adam also is called a young man,
still being in paradise, and working the virtues in their prime. And the Lord
Jesus also is called a young man because of the all-powerful of the divine
power; and the Antichrist also is called a young man because of the love of
novelty, and always being young in vice. "Guard your foot in whatever you go
into the house =93.537= of God; and near to hearing." Homonymously the members
of the outer man are said also of the soul, as eye, and hand, and foot, and
the rest. And it is not also the case that the things said of the soul are
said of the body; for wings are said of the soul, according to the, If you
sleep in the midst of the lots, wings of a dove silvered. Guard then the foot
of your soul in every affair, in whatever you go, so that you may arrive at
the house of God, that is, the heavenly Jerusalem. And he who guards the foot
of the soul is near to hearing, that is, to obeying the word, and setting
right the divine commandments. And if you take what has been said also of the
sensible foot, Guard, he says, the whole body; for from the part, from the
foot, he signified the whole, that it may not through the same things both to
virtue and to vice


minister. For example: Through the same feet do not walk both in church and in
worldly theatres. And upon the others likewise. For those going into the house
of God ought to be without share in the contrary things. "Above a gift of
fools is your sacrifice, because they are not knowing to do evil." Since there
are also fools often offering, as those who work almsgiving through vainglory;
and Cain having offered rightly, but not having divided rightly; let your
sacrifice, he says, come to be with judgment and with knowledge. For the fools
do not even know that they offer badly, because they do not have knowledge and
discernment. For he who works the fair with knowledge, with knowledge also
holds off from the contrary. And he who is ignorant of the first will be
ignorant of the rest also. The, Because they are not knowing to do evil, i.e.,
They are held by ignorance, and happen to be darkened in mind. And properly
here he called the of fools a gift, but upon the prudent he said a sacrifice.
Chapter 5. "Do not hasten upon your mouth, and let not your heart be quick to
bring out a word before the face of God, because God is in heaven above, and
you upon the earth below." He exhorts the rash tongue to be checked; then
since from a rash heart the tongue is ministered to unwise thoughts, blocking
the very fountain of untimely words, he adds, Let not your heart be quick to
bring out a word before the face of God; i.e., Because God oversees all
things, even the thoughts constituted in the soul, even if they are not
brought in through words; for God dwelling in the highest lookout of the
heavens surveys all things in the depth. Otherwise: Do not speak quickly about
theology, nor as it happens; for you cannot, being mortal and wearing flesh,
and lying below, grasp the essence of the invisible and superheavenly =93.540=
God. Upon these let your words be few. Therefore because of all the things
said before, let your words be not only in number, but also in exactness.
"Because a dream comes in a multitude of temptations, and a voice of a fool in
a multitude of words." If he says these things proverbially and absolutely,
you will understand thus: Just as, he says, those who by day undergo many
temptations, also
