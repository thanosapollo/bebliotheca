And in the same books Clement (the Hypotyposeis) has set down a tradition of
the elders from the beginning concerning the order of the Gospels, having this
manner. He said that of the Gospels those containing the genealogies had been
written first, but that the one according to Mark had had this economy. Peter
having preached the word publicly at Rome and having spoken forth the Gospel
by the Spirit, those present, being many, exhorted Mark, as one who had
followed him from of old and remembered the things said, to write up the
things spoken; and having done it, to give the Gospel to those who asked him
for it; which Peter, having learned of, neither hindered nor encouraged by way
of exhortation. John last, having perceived that the bodily things had been
made clear in the Gospels, being exhorted by those known to him, borne by the
Spirit, composed a spiritual Gospel. So much Clement.

9 Euseb. H. E. II 15, 1f. When then the divine word had thus sojourned among
them, the power of Simon was extinguished and was immediately destroyed
together with the man. But so great a light of piety shone upon the minds of
the hearers of Peter, that they were not content to be satisfied with a single
hearing, nor with the unwritten teaching of the divine preaching, but with all
kinds of entreaties they begged Mark, whose Gospel is current, being a
follower of Peter, that he would leave behind also through writing a memoir of
the teaching handed down to them by word, and they did not let the man go
until they had accomplished it, and thus they became the cause of the writing
of the so-called Gospel according to Mark. And they say that the apostle,
having learned what had been done, the Spirit having revealed it to him, was
pleased at the eagerness of the men and ratified the writing for reading in
the churches. Clement in the sixth of the Hypotyposeis has set down the
history, and the bishop of Hierapolis named Papias also bears witness with
him. And that Peter mentions Mark in the former epistle; which they say he
also composed at Rome itself. And that he himself indicates this, having
called the city more tropically Babylon, through these words: "She who is in
Babylon, chosen together, greets you, and Mark my son."

12 Vatic. graec. 354 † Gospel codex S, in von Soden 89 after Mercati, Studi e
Testi 12 1904 p. 3. Of Clement from the 6th of the Hypotyposeis: And he healed
the leper and said: "Show yourself to the priests for a testimony" on account
of such a tradition. The priests had a custom of healing lepers by the power
of God on appointed days. This leper then, not having been able to heal him
for a long time, they said: "No one will heal this man except the Christ
alone, if he comes." The leper then having begged much, the Savior having
compassion, having healed him, for this reason said: "Go and show yourself to
the priests for a testimony, that, if this man has been healed, of whom you
said no one but the Christ alone will heal him, the Christ has come, and
believe in him."

13 Euseb. H. E. II 1, 4f. And the same (Clement) in the seventh of the same

14 Euseb. H. E. II 9, 2f. And concerning this James (namely of the son of

also confessed to be a Christian. "They were then both led away together," he
says, "and on the way he asked to be forgiven by James; and he, having
considered a little, said peace to you and kissed him. And thus both were
beheaded together."

21 Oecumenius II p. 263 misprinted at 249 A on II Tim. 2, 2. "Through many

22 Euseb. H. E. VI 14, 2-4. And the epistle to the Hebrews he says is Paul's
(Clement in the Hypotyposeis), but that it was written to Hebrews in the
Hebrew tongue. And that Luke, having translated it ambitiously, published it
to the Greeks. Whence the same complexion is found according to the
interpretation of this epistle and of the Acts; and that "Paul an apostle" was
not prefixed, reasonably; "For writing to Hebrews," he says, "who had taken a
prejudice against him and suspected him, very wisely he did not put them off
at the beginning, having set the name." Then going on he adds: "But already as
the blessed elder said, since the Lord, being apostle of the Almighty, was
sent to Hebrews, through modesty Paul, as having been sent to the nations,
does not inscribe himself apostle of Hebrews both on account of honor toward
the Lord and on account of writing to the Hebrews also from abundance, being a
herald and apostle of the nations."

23 Photius Bibl. Cod. 109. The Hypotyposeis then treat of certain sayings of
both the old and new scripture, of which he also makes as it were an
explanation and interpretation in summary. And in some of them he seems to
speak rightly, but in some he is altogether carried into impious and mythical
words. For he holds matter without time and ideas as introduced from certain
sayings, and he brings the Son down to a creature. And further he marvels
transmigrations and many worlds before Adam. And from Adam he declares Eve,
not as the ecclesiastical word wills, but shamefully and godlessly. And he
dreams that the angels mixed with women and begot children from them, and that
the Word was not made flesh, but seemed. And he is convicted of telling
marvels of two words of the Father, of which the lesser appeared to men, or
rather not even that; for he says: "The Son too is called word, homonymously
with the paternal word, but this is not the one who became flesh. Nor indeed
the paternal word, but a certain power of God, as an efflux of his word having
become mind, has gone through the hearts of men." And all these things he
tries to construct from certain sayings of scripture. And he talks nonsense
and blasphemes countless other things, whether he himself, or some other
having played his person.

28 Chron. Pasch. p. 7 A-C ed. Paris. † I p. 14f ed. Bonn. But also Clement,
who became a most holy priest of the church of the Alexandrians, a most
ancient man and not far from the apostolic times, in the word On the Passover
teaches similar things, writing thus: In the years then that had passed the
Lord, celebrating, ate the thing sacrificed by the Jews as Passover. But when
he preached himself being the Passover, the lamb of God, as a sheep led to
slaughter, he immediately taught the disciples the mystery of the type on the
13th, on which they also ask him: "Where do you wish that we prepare for you
to eat the Passover?" On this day then both the sanctification of the
unleavened and the preparation of the feast took place. Whence John on this
day reasonably records that the disciples, as already being prepared, had
their feet washed by the Lord. And our Savior suffered on the following day,
himself being the Passover, sacrificed by the Jews. And after other things:
Accordingly then on the 14th, when he also suffered, in the morning the high
priests and the scribes, having brought him to Pilate, did not enter into the
praetorium, lest they be defiled, but might eat the Passover unhindered in the
evening. With this accuracy of the days all the scriptures also agree and the
Gospels are in accord. And the resurrection also bears additional witness; on
the third day then he rose, which was the first of the weeks of the harvest,
in which also the sheaf was legislated for the priest to offer.

39 N. le Nourry loc. cit. Col. 1336 from Paris. 854 f. 125v. Of the most holy

word On Providence. What is God? God is, as the Lord also says, spirit. And

is that which is not filled out by a body, or of which the being is not

44 Scorial. Y III 19 from the year 1360 f. 246v-248r †S after Barnard loc.
cit. pp. 47ff. Precepts of Clement. Practice quiet in words, and quiet in
works, and likewise in tongue and in gait; and flee rash vehemence; for thus
the mind will remain firm, and not becoming turbulent by the vehemence will it
be weak and short about prudence and seeing darkly; nor will it be overcome by
gluttony, nor by boiling anger, nor by the other passions, lying ready as
plunder for them. For the mind must prevail over the passions sitting high
upon a quiet throne looking toward God. Be in no way full of irascibility
about angers, nor sluggish in words, nor filled with hesitation in gaits, so
that a good rhythm may adorn the quiet for you and the form may appear
something godlike and holy. And guard also the symbols of pride, a high-necked
form and a raised head and a delicate and lofty step of feet. Let your words
toward those you meet be gentle and greetings sweet, and shame toward women
and the gaze turned to the earth. And speak all things considerately, and give
to the voice what is useful, measuring the utterance to the need of the
hearers, until it is both audible and neither escaping the hearing of those
present by smallness nor exceeding with a greater cry. And guard that you
never speak anything which you have not considered beforehand and thought out;
nor readily and in the midst of the words of the other interject your own; for
one must hear and converse in turn, dividing by time speech and silence; and
learn gladly, and teach without envy, nor ever hide wisdom from the others by
envy, nor stand away from learning through shame. Yield to elders equal to
fathers; honor servants of God; begin wisdom and virtue. Nor be contentious
toward friends, nor a mocker against them and a jester; and strongly refuse
falsehood and deceit and insolence; and with good speech bear also the proud
and insolent as a meek and great-souled man. And let all things lie for you
toward God both works and words, and refer all your own to Christ, and often
turn the soul upon God, and rest the thought upon the power of Christ as in
some harbor, resting in the divine light of the Savior from all speech and
action. And by day often share your own prudence with men, but with God for
the most part in night likewise and in day; for let not sleep prevail over you
much of the prayers and hymns toward God; for the long sleep is a match for
death. Always become a partaker of Christ who shines the divine ray from
heaven; for let Christ be for you continuous and unceasing gladness. Nor loose
the tension of the soul in feasting and relaxation of drinks, but consider
sufficient for the body what is needed. And do not hurry to foods before the
time of supper also is present; and let bread be your supper, and let herbs of
earth be added and the seasonal things from trees; and go to the food steadily
and not showing rabid gluttony; nor be a flesh-eater nor a lover of wine,
except when some disease should lead a healing upon this. But instead of the
pleasures in these choose the gladnesses in divine words and hymns supplied to
you by the wisdom from God, and let a heavenly care always lead you up to
heaven; and let go the many cares about the body having taken courage in the
hopes toward God, that he will provide you the necessary things as sufficient
food both for life and a covering of the body and defenses against winter
cold. For of your king indeed is all the earth and as many things as grow from
it; and as his own members he exceedingly cares for the bodies of his own
servants as his sacred things and temples. For this reason then fear neither
excessive diseases nor the approach of old age expected in time; for disease
also will cease, when with whole-souled purpose we do his commandments.
Knowing these things prepare the soul strong also toward diseases, take
courage as some man best in the stadia to undergo the toils with unturned
power. Nor press the soul very much by grief, whether an impending disease
weighs or some other hard thing befalls, but nobly oppose the thought to the
toils, offering thanks to God even in the midst of the toilsome affairs as
thinking things wiser than men and which it is not possible nor easy for men
to find. And pity those who are suffering, and ask the help from God upon men;
for he will nod the grace to the friend who asks, and he will provide aid to
those who are suffering, wishing to make his own power known to men, so that
having come to knowledge they may go up upon God and enjoy the eternal
blessedness, whenever the son of God arrives restoring good things to his own.

48 Maximus Confessor De variis diff. locis Dionysii et Gregorii ed. Oehler
Halle 1857 p. 60f † PG 91, 1085). And these words which I said the Areopagite
holy Dionysius teaches us to be called predeterminations and divine wills by
scripture. And likewise also those about Pantaenus who became teacher of the
great Clement the Stromateus say that divine wills are called so as dear to
scripture. Whence having been asked by certain men proud of the outside
education, how Christians hold that God knows the things that are, those
having supposed that he knows the intelligibles intellectually and the
sensibles sensorily, they answered: neither sensorily the sensibles nor
intellectually the intelligibles; for it is not possible that he who is above
the things that are should grasp the things that are according to the things
that are, but we say that he knows the things that are as his own wills,
having added also the reasonableness of the word. For if he has made all
things by will (and no word will contradict), and it is both pious and just
always to say that God knows his own will, and each of the things that have
come to be he has made willing, therefore God knows the things that are as his
own wills, since he has also made the things that are willing.

sends crooked ways."

68 Sacra Par. 317 Holl from CR in both after Nr. 202 † Paed. II 60, 5. Lemma
in C from the same, in R immediately attached. Christian piety is a certain
science. In which manner indeed each science has its own words, according to
which the profession according to them comes about for the one taking them up;
as rhetoric from the rhetorical words, and medicine from the medical, so also
the Christian science comes about from the words according to it. For the
Christian words also are its own. As then it is not possible that one taking
up the rhetorical words become a physician or the medical a rhetor, so neither
a Christian from other words than from the Christian. Wherefore I say that
those wishing to be Christians must take up only the words of Christ and not
seek through others that this at least come about for them.
