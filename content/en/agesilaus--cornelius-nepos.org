* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Agesilaus the Lacedaemonian has been praised both by the rest of the
writers and especially by Xenophon the Socratic: for he used him most
familiarly. He first had a contention about the kingship with
Leotychides, his brother's son. For it was a custom among the
Lacedaemonians handed down from the ancestors, that they should always
have two kings, more in name than in command, from the two families of
Procles and Eurysthenes, who were the first kings at Sparta from the
progeny of Hercules. It was not permitted that from one of these a man
be made into the place of the other family: thus each kept its own
order. First account was had of who was eldest among the children of him
who had died reigning; but if he had not left male offspring, then he
was chosen who was nearest in kinship. King Agis had died, the brother
of Agesilaus: he had left a son, Leotychides. Whom that man had not
acknowledged born, the same when dying he had said was his. He contended
about the honor of the kingship with Agesilaus, his paternal uncle, and
did not obtain that which he sought. For with Lysander supporting, a
man, as we showed above, factious and powerful in those times, Agesilaus
was preferred.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
This man, as soon as he obtained the command, persuaded the
Lacedaemonians that they send him with an army into Asia and make war on
the king, teaching that it was better to fight in Asia than in Europe.
For a report had gone out that Artaxerxes was preparing fleets and
infantry armies, which he would send into Greece. Power having been
given, he used such speed that he arrived in Asia with his forces before
the royal satraps knew that he had set out. Whereby it came about that
he came upon all unprepared and unaware. When Tissaphernes, who then had
the highest command among the royal prefects, learned this, he sought a
truce from the Laconian, pretending that he was giving effort that it
might be agreed between the Lacedaemonians and the king, but in truth
for gathering forces, and he obtained a three-month one. Moreover each
swore that he would keep the truce without fraud. In which compact
Agesilaus remained with the highest faith; against that Tissaphernes
prepared nothing other than war. Although the Laconian perceived this,
yet he kept the oath and said that he gained much in this, that
Tissaphernes by his own perjury both alienated men from his affairs and
made the gods angry at himself, but that he, religion having been
preserved, strengthened the army, when it observed that the divine power
was acting with him, and made men more friendly to himself, because they
were accustomed to favor those whom they saw keeping faith.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
After the day of the truce passed, the barbarian, not doubting that,
because his own dwellings were most numerous in Caria and that region at
those times was thought by far the richest, the enemies would make their
attack especially there, had drawn all his forces there. But Agesilaus
turned himself into Phrygia and had laid it waste before Tissaphernes
moved himself anywhere. The soldiers having been enriched with great
booty, he led the army back to Ephesus to winter and there, workshops of
arms having been established, he prepared war with great industry. And
that they might be armed more eagerly and adorned more distinguishedly,
he proposed prizes, with which those would be gifted whose industry in
that matter had been outstanding. He did the same in the kinds of
exercises, so that he would affect with great gifts those who had
excelled the rest. By these things therefore he brought it about that he
had both a most well-equipped and a most well-trained army. When it
seemed time to this man to draw the forces out of winter quarters, he
saw that if he had openly announced where he was about to march, the
enemies would not believe it and would occupy other regions with
garrisons and would not doubt that he would do other than he had
announced. Therefore when he had said that he would go to Sardis,
Tissaphernes thought that same Caria must be defended. In which when his
opinion had deceived him and he had seen himself beaten in counsel, he
set out too late as a guard to his own. For when he had come there,
already Agesilaus, many places having been stormed, had obtained great
booty. But the Laconian, when he saw that the enemies excelled in
cavalry, never gave his men opportunity in the plain and joined battle
in those places in which the infantry forces were stronger. Therefore,
as often as he engaged, he routed far greater forces of the adversaries
and so conducted himself in Asia that by the opinion of all he was held
the victor.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
This man, when already he was considering in his mind to set out against
the Persians and to attack the king himself, a messenger came to him
from home by sending of the ephors, that the Athenians and Boeotians had
declared war on the Lacedaemonians: wherefore he should not hesitate to
come. In this his piety is to be admired no less than his military
virtue: who, when he was in command of a victorious army and had the
greatest confidence of obtaining the kingdom of the Persians, with such
modesty was obedient to the commands of the absent magistrates, as if he
were a private man in the assembly at Sparta. Would that our commanders
had been willing to follow his example! But let us return there.
Agesilaus preferred a good reputation to a most opulent kingdom and
thought it far more glorious if he had obeyed the institutions of his
fatherland than if he had conquered Asia in war. With this mind
therefore he threw his forces across the Hellespont and used such speed
that, which journey Xerxes had finished in a turning year, this man
crossed in thirty days. When already he was not so far distant from the
Peloponnese, the Athenians and Boeotians and the rest of their allies
tried to stand against him at Coronea: all of whom he conquered in a
heavy battle. Of this victory perhaps the greatest praise was that, when
most from the flight had thrown themselves into the temple of Minerva
and it was asked of him what he wished to be done with them, although he
had received several wounds in that battle and seemed angry at all who
had borne arms against him, yet he preferred religion to anger and
forbade them to be violated. Nor indeed did he do this only in Greece,
that he held the temples of the gods sacred, but even among the
barbarians with the highest religion he preserved all images and altars.
Therefore he used to declare that he wondered that those who had harmed
the suppliants of the gods were not held in the number of the
sacrilegious, or that those who diminished religion were not afflicted
with heavier penalties than those who spoiled shrines.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
After this battle the whole war was brought together around Corinth and
therefore it was called Corinthian. Here when in one fight ten thousand
of the enemies had fallen with Agesilaus as leader and by that deed the
resources of the adversaries seemed weakened, he was so far from
insolence of glory that he pitied the fortune of Greece, because so many
conquered by him had fallen by the fault of the adversaries: for with
that multitude, if there had been a sound mind, the Persians could have
been made to pay the penalty to Greece. The same man, when he had driven
the adversaries within the walls and many were urging that he storm
Corinth, denied that that suited his virtue: for he said he was that man
who would compel those erring to return to duty, not who would storm the
most noble cities of Greece. 'For if,' he said, 'we shall have wished to
extinguish those who have stood with us against the barbarians, we
ourselves shall have stormed ourselves while they are at rest. By which
deed without trouble, when they shall have wished, they will crush us.'

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
Meanwhile that calamity happened to the Lacedaemonians at Leuctra. That
he might not set out there, when he was being driven by most to go out,
as if he divined about the outcome, he excused his old age. The same
man, when Epaminondas was storming Sparta and the town was without
walls, showed himself such a commander that at that time it appeared to
all that, unless he had been, Sparta would not have been. In which
crisis indeed the swiftness of his counsel was salvation to all. For
when certain young men, terrified by the arrival of the enemies, wished
to desert to the Thebans and had seized a high place outside the city,
Agesilaus, who saw that it would be most ruinous if it were noticed that
anyone was trying to desert to the enemies, came there with his own men
and, as if they had done it with a good mind, praised their plan,
because they had occupied that place: that he too had noticed that this
ought to be done. Thus he recovered the young men by simulated praise
and, companions from his own having been added, left the place safe. For
they, the number of those who were without share in the plan having been
added, did not dare to move themselves and all the more willingly,
because they thought that what they had planned was hidden.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
Without doubt after the Leuctric battle the Lacedaemonians never
recovered themselves nor regained their former command, while in the
meantime Agesilaus never ceased to help his fatherland by whatever
things he could. For when the Lacedaemonians especially lacked money, he
was a protection to all who had defected from the king: by whom, gifted
with great money, he relieved his fatherland. And in this that was
especially admirable, when the greatest gifts were being conferred on
him by kings and dynasts and cities, that he never brought anything home
to his own house, changed nothing of the living, nothing of the clothing
of the Laconians. He was content with the same house which Eurysthenes,
progenitor of his ancestors, had used: which he who had entered could
see no sign of lust, none of luxury, against that very many of endurance
and abstinence. For it was so appointed that in no thing it differed
from that of any poor man and private person.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
And this man so great, as he had had nature a favorer in bestowing the
virtues of the mind, so he got her as a malefactress in forming the
body: for he was of low stature and of a small body and lame in one
foot. Which thing also brought some deformity, and those unknown, when
they looked upon his face, despised him; but those who had known his
virtues could not admire him enough. Which happened to him when at
eighty years he had been sent as aid to Tachos into Egypt and had lain
down on the shore with his men without any covering and had a bed such
that the earth was covered with straw and nothing more than a hide had
been thrown over it, in the same humble and worn clothing as all his
companions, so that their array not only indicated no king among them,
but offered the suspicion that they were not the most fortunate men.
When the report of his arrival had been carried to the royal people,
quickly gifts of every kind were brought there. To these asking for
Agesilaus it was scarcely made credible that he was one of those who
were then reclining. When they had given what they had brought with the
words of the king, he accepted nothing besides veal and kinds of food of
that sort which the present time desired: ointments, crowns, and the
second table he distributed to the slaves, the rest he ordered to be
brought back. By which deed the barbarians despised him even more,
because they thought that he, by ignorance of good things, had taken
especially the cheap ones.

This man, when he was returning from Egypt, gifted by king Nectanabis
with two hundred and twenty talents, which he should give as a gift to
his people, and had come into the harbor which is called Menelaus, lying
between Cyrene and Egypt, having fallen into a disease he died. There
his friends, that they might more easily carry him to Sparta, because
they did not have honey, poured wax around him and so brought him home.
