Since by some fortune I was left behind the Olympic festival, being unable in
body to share with you in what took place, and having come to the very last of
danger from the illness that then befell me, and having obtained as saviors
Zeus and Poseidon, the brother of Zeus, most sure, and having taken as it were
a harbor of this present health and safety, and of my stay with you, and
moreover since some report of this kind had gone abroad about it, that certain
persons longed for my presence, and the most evident of these, not so much for
my sake, as I judge, nor for my speeches, but showing the gallantry and the
love of honor in these things and the honor and goodwill toward their elders
in the case of as many as their life is with them, by whom they remember that
they themselves were often delighted before and made gladder in the common
festivals of the Hellenes, I knew that I must not delay for these reasons to
come to you, but at the same time I must pay a double repayment to two
brothers, the former and

the elder and in a way the accustomed one, and the one that is now about to
begin. For it would not perhaps be pious for me, mindful everywhere of the
divine, and having nearly the greatest part of my occupation in speeches about
these things, to seem to have seen the collection from the speeches go to this
god alone of the gods, and that there is no speech named after me either from
the god or from this very place in which we now are, but that so great a thing
was neglected; concerning which it would perhaps be well for me first to
propitiate the god and make him gracious, having pardon for the former things,
if anything has slipped past me in these matters, and to have these present
things received graciously and with propitious purpose, and to give the other
goods and to say what one ought. And even if it happen otherwise, to look to
my power, and not to the greatness of the speech, which is unpayable, and this
being literally as if one wished to count the sands of the sea. Nevertheless,
since this god is wont to make even the greatest of seas and impassable
passages passable, and often even from fair winds when he happens to be
gracious, let the speech about him now be launched for us too; and let it be
launched from the god himself, as one might say, in the manner of a stern
sail. Such then, as many as the first who philosophized about this god, in
that they thought fit to refer to the nature of the whole, [said]: everything
liquid in the universe, either being the greatest part of the whole or the
whole itself; and I mean the liquid substance so called, as also the speech of
the poets bears witness, saying this to be the genesis of the gods, as it is
somewhere said /Oceanus the genesis of the gods,/ in another name, that of
Oceanus, and the nourishment and life of all things, as many as are divine and
of the lower portion; and therefore this too is the greatest oath of the gods,
in another name again the water of Styx; and as gods the first men held
springs and rivers, greatest and first,

and indeed still streams; and that all things from here have a share of
generation and go away again thither according to their destruction, and that
this is the curse of Homer invoked upon water, that all come to water, as also
the verse has it /but may you all become water and earth,/ and as many other
such things said and devised by men and wise poets; let these be supposed
complete for us, or let it have been said to this extent at least for the
present. But the things that are common to all and known and in the eyes of
all, of these it might perhaps befit to make mention now too. And these are
not by themselves, but these also are spoken with the poets as witnesses.

For it is agreed that Cronus begat three sons from Rhea their mother, Poseidon
and Zeus and the third whom we hear of after these. And indeed the lot of the
father was divided among these, apportioning all this with the most just law.
Zeus got the heaven and the aether and the things in them, and has his origin
from there; and Poseidon the sea, and the third brother the place under the
earth. Let us then behold among ourselves which of them made his own portion
more humane and more common, whether he who shared none of it with anyone, or
he who shares indeed, but not before it departs, or he who receiving all
living and providing for himself to live together and be with and have
fellowship in the same city, and working out the most beautiful of portions
the sea, and all the good things from the sea invented, both from it and
through it, and having given himself to the whole race of humankind. And
indeed, what did a man differ from the trees growing on the earth of
themselves, as long as he was on the earth only? What was beautiful to him,
before this god opened the sea, and then also spread it out? Or what to see,
what to hear? For he did not even know the earth itself in which he was,
except what was under his feet, and not how the

limits of it, or the other continent, or that opposite. But those men of old
lived in the manner of beasts, in caves and dens and trees, the ones called
blessed. What was more wretched than these, knowing nothing and seeing nothing
and having nothing, except as much as each day on equal terms with the
irrational animals? But when this god bade them resort to him with confidence
and spread the sea, and showed in what way, and took away the fear from the
soul, which as toward a dreadful and terrible beast they had, expecting always
to be snatched away by the sea, both they themselves and seeing the whole land
together with the winds, then it is not easy even to tell in speech the
multitude of the good things coming to the land from the sea. And to bring
both these into one, the greatest work of Poseidon, and that friendship and
intercourse come to be between them. Before this not even this was, but the
land and the sea were hostile to one another. But now they were made to dwell
together by this god, and he made cities for men there, which we now call
islands, so as not to long for the land in the sea, nor anything else than to
be on land. And he granted all living things and beasts of every kind. Where
also plants, some for use for man, and some also for delight and pleasure. And
indeed he provided a table far more abundant than the earth's, and found
countless respites, and made man, so to speak, a winged creature, only not as
with certain wings making use of the sea, but ordered to be carried with the
winds, and did not even grudge this, as not any other, just as his eldest
brother is said to have admitted one, Perseus, into his own kingdom, and that
one being little short of both land and sea, and Bellerophon afterwards from
here, not on a good occasion. But this one, every one who wishes, both man and
woman and even the children; and indeed there are

whom he even raised up from the sea, as Apollo and Artemis. And for these Zeus
would justly owe him gratitude, because he neither looked on Leto driven all
that time for the things she chanced upon, nor his own offspring all but
destroyed. And this one also brought up from himself the beauty of Aphrodite,
as it were from some head, this one too from the sea, in imitation of Zeus,
whether indeed this one was earlier, not only the human things but also the
divine, as the story about Trojan Ida has it. But since we made mention of
Leto, there came into my mind how many other loves he shared with his brother;
for both Io of Inachus and Europa the fair, and perhaps one and another, of
which there are still even now names among men. And the beautiful child he
made with the fair mother both a child for himself and made him and the mother
partners of his kingdom.

But this speech awaits us for when it comes due; now we go through the works
of Poseidon among one another, such as they are and how great, and how worthy
his kingdom. Next perhaps would be also the honors among men, what sort and
how worthy. For all the most marked and most famous parts of the earth are
sacred to Poseidon. At the entrances of the Euxine sea, where the narrowest of
the sea unfolds from itself the great sea, there both a blue rock has been
named and gates of the sea formerly thought to be closed, and his sanctuaries
and precincts and altars, and other things, and the sea that comes down toward
us from there, and Leucas so named after the Leucas there at Actium, not being
a rock like this one, but much land advancing into the sea, pushing it forward
far in a rounded shape from half its length, literally in the manner of a
sickle, so that this is the name of the advance, and it is said that on it
there is also a story and a myth, that there was accomplished the deed about
Cronus by his sons, the one about the castration. And having crossed the
Propontis and the

Hellespont, and having crossed the islands called Cyclades: on the side toward
the Aegean sea both Euboea and its capes, the harbor at Geraestus, and a
sanctuary of the god. Of this Homer too makes mention in the return of the
Achaeans and the voyage from Ilium. And at the entrances into the Ionian sea
the cape at Taenarum of the god, and the launching from there. Homer also
makes him travel to Aegae and Helice to a feast, and from above running upon
the waves both he and his horses, not touching the sea; in which way he also
furnished for man a double carriage, a chariot on land, a ship on sea. Where
are his sanctuaries and precincts and altar smoking, as he himself said; in
which way also Zeus his brother among the milk feeders. For once, if we must
not make the speech petty: all capes, and all harbors, and all parts of land
and sea are sanctuaries of Poseidon and offerings and statues and precincts
and temples. Yet nothing is so dear or beloved to him or honored as this
Isthmus and this place. And this I call also the citadel of Poseidon and the
kingdom and the court, as Homer named the court of Zeus, and the starting
place. I infer from the other things and from this: that he turned all the sea
from every direction hither, setting gates on both sides and unfolding to it
this so-called isthmus, toward east and toward west alike, and closing it
together at the same time, so that they did not mingle, with not much measure
of land, but as it were a narrow courtyard, and making a law and ordering each
to keep its own boundaries, and unfolding again and granting to each a great
open space forward, most paradoxical of all and sweetest of spectacles, as
many as are on earth, to sail in and sail out in the same from fair winds for
each, and under the same winds both launchings and landings to happen in this
land alone of all and the

sea; and all things resort hither from everywhere both by land and by sea, and
this is the place of which from most ancient time it is hymned as wealthy by
the poets, both through the multitude of the goods present and through the
felicity residing in it. For it is as it were a marketplace and common court
of the Hellenes and a festival, not that which the Greek nation fills for
itself in two years, as the present one, but that through every year and each
day. And if there was, as for men, so also for cities, the office of proxenos
toward one another, this city would have this name and this honor everywhere;
for it receives all into itself and sends them forth again from itself, and is
the common refuge of all; as it were a road and passage of all men, wherever
one might travel only, and a common city of the Hellenes, as it were literally
some metropolis and mother of this very thing. For among other things there is
nowhere one might rest as in a mother's bosoms, neither more sweetly nor more
dear, as it were itself rest and refuge and safety for all who have recourse
to it. But of its beauty and of its longings and loves as much as the
multitude has been told, so as to bind all to the sweet, and all to burn alike
for it, and to have in itself loves and longings and whisperings and coaxing,
so as to steal the mind even of those who think greatly on themselves, and if
need be anything else besides, all that is said of the drugs of the goddess,
so that the city is clearly Aphrodite's, which it comes upon me to call by
this name; whoever this cestus may be with which the goddess binds all to
herself, and as it were this is some anchor and necklace of all Hellas, and a
precinct of the Nymphs, of all the Naiad Nymphs housed here, and a chamber of
the Hours, in which they sit all the time and whence they come forth to open
the gates, whether you wish to call it of Zeus or of Poseidon. And if ever a
strife about beauty

fell upon the cities, as it is said once happened among the goddesses, it
would have been with Aphrodite. And about the form of the city what might one
even say, when not even the eyes of all are able to go around it? Of its size,
what greater proof might one take than that it is stretched out toward all
seas, and has made its home with them and settled alongside, not with this one
and not with that one, but with all alike. And indeed of good order the city
is still now the starting place, deciding what is just for the Hellenes. Of
wealth and multitude of good things, as much as is likely, drawing it up for
herself from the land from everywhere and the sea from everywhere, as it were
seated in the midst of the goods and washed round by them, as some merchant
ship in a circle full of the goods; and wisdom you would find coming along the
road; and from the things without soul you might learn and hear; so many
treasuries of letters about it everywhere, wherever one might only look, and
along the very roads and the stoas. Still further the gymnasia, the schools,
and studies and histories. What need then to make mention of Sisyphus, or of
the wisdom of Sisyphus, or of Corinth of Zeus, or of Bellerophon of Poseidon,
or any other of the heroes and demigods, or after these again those who
devised weights and yokes and measures, and the just thing in these, and how
this city built as it were a ship, not a trireme only, but the Argo itself I
would say. For at least the leader of its fleet after the voyage up from there
appears to be brought hither with it and to have anchored here; or again the
things on land, the so-called wing of Pegasus, whether you will say as of a
horse, or also as of a bird; and the first who dared upon it, the heavenly
horseman. But these things are old and mythical; yet still now remembered, as
many of them peaceful and warlike

of what of the Hellenic are they not more illustrious? Of what not more
marked, whether by land or by sea? Of whose deeds and actions more
conspicuous? But now is no season of wars, so that not even the memory of such
things would have been uttered in season, the Hellenes celebrating a festival
and being of one mind in the fairest and most famous of festivals, in this
one, coming together through twice two into one, doubling the others and
approaching, and overtaking the rest and again catching up with them,
literally as in chariot races, if only it were possible for men to see the
chariot of Poseidon borne along, and coming to a common krater and pouring
libations and sacrificing together, and carrying away the spectacle for
Poseidon and Amphitrite and Palaemon and Leucothea. These things then it would
be well both to hymn and to speak and for you to pass your lives in speeches
about these things.

The things then of Poseidon himself have been said in a way and hymned, not
blamably as it seems to me; and I see you also so disposed; and by Zeus also
about the city itself, as one might say in the briefest [words]. But I see
left to us the speech about the two gods, the child and his mother, whether
one must call it a speech or a myth. Which I fear and greatly dread and am at
a loss where in the world I must handle it with you, whether as the many
think, and Homer agrees, that we too must have the sufferings of the gods, as
it were the bonds of Ares and the servitude of Apollo and the castings of
Hephaestus into the sea, and so also the griefs of Ino and certain flights, or
this is neither holy nor pious to say, and that especially making a speech
about the gods, but this speech must be set apart for us not only outside the
Isthmus and the Peloponnese, but also outside all Hellas; and we must even go
to both seas and purify, if need be, Athamas, whoever he was, and his madness,
whether it was or falsely bruited by the

:END:

most wretched of those who composed each of these things for us. But Ino
either never was and never came to be whom the many say, or if she was and
came to be, yet nothing akin to the things happening here. But Leucothea was a
god from the beginning straightway and did not begin from where the many
think. And if she was also endowed with speech, as he said who said it, yet
she was not mortal. Homer indeed made even gods as men, and speaking outright
and conversing with men, both Athena and Hermes and Poseidon himself. And
after these things it would perhaps befit us rightly and friendly toward the
gods to speak and hear among one another, that Poseidon fell in love with
Leucothea, and having fallen in love had her with himself; in which way also
Tyro of Salmoneus and Amymone and some other fair one, and hid her under the
wave, and her journeys hither from there, if we must accept even these, to
have come to be not fleeing in flight, but as going to a chamber the sea; nor
that the child, who was from another bed, she carried off having snatched him,
but as a deposit and plaything and gift for Poseidon. But it is clear that
these things have this character, the truth itself and the things about them
still now said and done as about gods. For evils neither are among gods, nor
could they ever come to be; for these must be set apart to the place toward
earth; nor sufferings and misfortunes. So much then would this be the
beginning of something immortal. For evils from evils, but the works of the
gods are good. Otherwise in no way is it lawful to come to be. But if a man
was not fortunate, how then did he become a god, tell me; and if he was god-
beloved, he would never have had ill fortune. And if she was not such from the
beginning, neither would she have come to be after these things; for if the
gods are able to release from evils, it is possible also not to approach nor
leave the evils upon them; and yet if to give again goods for evils, and the

greatest, the portion of the immortal, and to be born straightway so disposed.
Therefore Leucothea was a god straightway from the beginning, and the
misfortunes did not lead her to this, but the being such straightway from
birth prepared her and not at all to have come to be in any misfortune. Nor
would she have been a god among gods, nor would she have had such power as we
hear. But now since one must obey the speech of Homer, the thing about her
risks being a sort of monarchy of the kingdom that is over the sea, and not
even Poseidon himself to be permitted anything without her consenting. For let
it be said, if even the slayer of her child he did not permit him to avenge,
having these things as it were in his hands, as one might say, but to shift
about otherwise, and that it is nothing to confound all goods nor as many
things as he did, having seized the trident toward the protection of this
goddess, whether a veil according to the poetic speech or otherwise somehow
one must say, but it was safety and security in the midst of the terrors,
clearly that these things did not come to be by force nor unwillingly on the
part of the god, but Poseidon yielding to her in all things and letting her do
whatever she wished. And well did the speech do, coming to this, especially
since something beautiful appeared to us from it, Leucothea being for us a
sort of philosopher, not only pitiful and loving the Hellenes; she who even
saved Odysseus, the wisest of the Hellenes and best, as also some other, and
all to whom love of wisdom was, and who wish to be seekers and acute and
intelligent, the things also said of Odysseus. These things then about her,
dear also to Poseidon himself, as I suppose. But Palaemon it is beautiful both
to speak and to name his very name, and to make an oath, and to share of the
rite upon him and of the orgiastic rite, so great a longing attends the child
both to see and even in letters, where borne upon a dolphin, where also upon
the very backs of the sea, where also in the hands of the

mother the tender growth of the child and his age and bloom. For these are
spectacles sweetest of spectacles, both to see and to hear; and the sea of him
to be present in letters and calm, beautiful also this, and the smiles of the
child toward Poseidon receiving him with the robe. And to write nothing else
besides these, such as elsewhere the dreadful and impious things in letters,
which I wonder how ever the first who saw these bore them, and did not come
with anger upon the makers and slayers of these, or even still now bear them
in the midst of the sanctuaries. But it is not perhaps mine to rebuke such
things. It remains then having prayed to Poseidon and Amphitrite and Leucothea
and Palaemon and the Nereids and all the marine daemons both and all to grant
safety and salvation both on land and on sea to the great king and to the
whole race of him and to the race of the Hellenes, and in the other things to
flourish and in speeches to go on to what is fitting for each of us.
