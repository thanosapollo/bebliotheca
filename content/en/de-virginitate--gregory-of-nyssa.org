4.t Chapter 4. That all the absurdities in life have their beginning from
marriage; in which also what sort of man he is who has truly renounced life.
4.1 Rather, why must one petty-mindedly expose the absurdity of such a life,
limiting the enumeration of disasters to adulteries and separations and plots
alone? For it seems to me, by the higher and truer account, that all the
wickedness of life that is observed in all affairs and pursuits finds no
beginning against a man's life, if one does not subject himself to the
necessity of this life. And the truth of the account will be shown to us thus:
he who has perceived the deceit of this life with the pure eye of the soul and
has become higher than the things pursued here, and, as the Apostle says,
regarding all things as some foul dung, and in a way exiling himself from all
of life by withdrawal from marriage, has no fellowship with human evils, I
mean greed and envy and anger and hatred and desire of empty glory and the
rest of that kind. And having the unfulfillment of all such things and being
free through all and at peace with life, there is nothing over which he will
even contend for more, or on what he will move envy against himself among his
neighbors, he who touches none of those things around which envy grows in
life? For having lifted his own soul above the whole world and thinking virtue
his only honorable possession, he will live a life without pain and peaceful
and without fight. For the possession of virtue, even if all men share in it
each according to his own power, is always full for those who desire it, not
like the possession on earth, which those who divide into sections, as much as
they add to one portion, so much they take from the other, and the increase of
the one is a lessening of the one who shares; whence also the fights about
more arise among men through hatred of being lessened. But the greed of that
possession is unenvied, and he who snatches the more brought no damage to the
one who claims to share equally, but as much as one is capable, he himself is
filled with the good desire and the wealth of the virtues is not spent in
those who have it already.


4.2 He then who looks toward this life and treasures up virtue for himself,
which no human limit circumscribes, will he ever endure to bend his own soul
toward any of the low and trampled things? Will he admire earthly wealth or
human power or anything else of the things pursued by folly? For if anyone is
still disposed lowly about these things, he would be outside such a choir and
will have nothing toward our account; but if he thinks the things above and
walks on high with God, he is altogether higher than such things, not having
the common starting-point of the error about such things, I mean marriage. For
the wish to be above others, that hard passion, pride, which if one should
call it especially seed or root of all the thorn of sin he will not miss what
is likely, this especially has its beginning from the cause of marriage.
4.3 For it is not possible, as a rule, that the greedy man not blame his
children, or that the one mad for glory and ambitious not refer the cause of
the evil to his family, so that he may not seem less than those who were
before him, and so that he may be thought great by those after, leaving some
narratives to those from him; and likewise also the remaining sicknesses of
the soul, envy and remembering of wrongs and hatred and anything else of the
kind, hang from the same cause. For all these dwell together with those who
are fluttered about this life, of whom he who has come outside, as from some
high lookout watching human passions from afar, pities for their blindness
those enslaved to such vanity and judging great the well-being of the flesh.
For when he sees one of men notable in something of life, plumed with
dignities or wealths or powers, he laughs at the folly of those puffed up
through these things and numbers the longest time of human life according to
the term spoken by the Psalmist. Then measuring this momentary interval
against the endless ages, he pities for his hollowness the one who lifts his
soul over things so mean and low and temporary. For what among things here is
worthy of blessing? Honor, the thing pursued by many? Because what more does
it add to those honored? For the mortal remains mortal, whether he is honored
or not. Or to possess many plethra of land? And this, to what good end does it
come for those who possess it, apart from the fool thinking that what does not
belong to him is his? For he does not know, it seems, under much gluttony,
that "the earth is truly the Lord's and the fullness thereof" "for God is king
of all the earth", but to men the passion of greed gives a falsely named title
of lordship of things that do not belong to them. "For the earth," as the wise
Ecclesiastes says, "stands to the age," serving all the generations and at
different times nourishing different people who come upon it; but men, not
even being lords of themselves, but according to the will of the one who leads
coming into life when they do not know, and when they do not wish being
separated from it again, think by much vanity that they lord over it, they who
at times come to be and perish, over that which always remains.
4.4 He then who has considered these things and for this reason despises
whatever is thought honorable among men, and has love only of the divine life,
who knows that "All flesh is grass and all the glory of man as the flower of
grass," when will he think the grass worthy of zeal, which is today and
tomorrow will not be? For he who has considered divine things well knows that
not only human things do not have firmness, but that not even the whole world
has


remained at rest forever. For this reason he looks down on this life as alien
and temporary, since "Heaven and earth will pass away," according to the
Savior's voice, and all things necessarily await the change of elements. For
this reason, while he is "in the tent," as the Apostle says, showing the
temporary, being burdened by the present life he laments that this sojourning
is prolonged for him, as the Psalmist also has made in divine songs. For truly
they live in darkness who sojourn in life "with these tents"; for which cause
the prophet, groaning at the stretching of the sojourning, "Woe is me," he
says, "that my sojourning has been prolonged." And he assigned darkness as the
cause of this gloom. For we have learned from the wise that darkness in the
Hebrew tongue is named "Kedar." For is it not truly that men, held as by some
night-blindness, are thus dim-sighted toward the recognition of deceit, not
knowing that all things that have been thought honorable in this life, or even
all that have been taken as the contrary, exist only in the opinion of the
foolish? And in themselves they are nowhere nothing: not some low birth, not
good repute of family, not glory, not distinction, not the ancient tales, not
pride over present things, not ruling others, not being under hand. Wealths
and luxuries and poverties and wants and all the irregularities of life seem
to the uneducated to differ greatly, when they make pleasure the criterion of
such things; but to the one high in mind all things appear of equal honor and
neither is more preferred than the other, because the course of life is run
alike through opposites and equal power toward living well or badly exists in
either lot, "through weapons on the right and on the left," says the Apostle,
"through glory and dishonor." Through which the one whose mind is purified and
who has considered the truth of beings will travel the journey rightly, going
through the time appointed for him from birth until the exit, neither being
softened by the pleasant nor being humbled by the harsh, but according to the
custom of travelers holding to what is ahead he makes little account of the
things that appear. For it is customary for those traveling to press alike
toward the end of the road, whether they go through meadows and well-grown
places or through more deserted and rougher spots, and neither did the
pleasant hold them back nor did the unpleasant hinder. So he too will press
unturned toward the aim set before him, turning toward none of the things
along the way, but looking only toward heaven he will pass through life, as
some good steersman directing the boat toward the aim above.
4.5 But the one thick in mind and looking down and bent in soul toward the
pleasures of the body as cattle toward pasture, living only for the belly and
the things after the belly, "alienated from the life of God" and "a stranger
to the covenants of the promise," thinking no other good to be except to be
pleased through the body. This one is, and everyone of this sort, the one
going through in darkness, as Scripture says, the "inventor of evils" in this
life; for in these are also greed and unchastity of passions and immoderation
toward pleasures, and all love of rule and desire of empty glory and the
remaining crowd of passions that dwell with men. For somehow in evils one
holds to the other, and to whomever the one comes, the rest also altogether
enter together as drawn by some natural necessity, as happens in a chain when
the beginning is pulled; nor is it possible for the remaining


links of the chain to be still, but the link at the other end of the chain was
moved together with the first, the movement always going forward from the
first through the adjacent according to sequence. So also human passions are
interwoven and grown together with one another, and through the one that has
prevailed the remaining train of evils also enters the soul together. And if I
must sketch this wicked chain for you, suppose someone through some pleasure
to have been worsted by the passion of vainglory; but with vainglory the
longing for more also followed. For it is not possible to become greedy unless
that leads the way to the passion. Then the desire to have more and to be
first either kindles anger toward the equal, or pride toward the subject, or
envy toward the superior, and hypocrisy follows envy, from that bitterness,
from this hatred of men, and the end of all these is a condemnation ending in
Gehenna and darkness and fire. Do you see the train of evils, how all hang
from the one passion according to pleasure? 4.6 Since then once the sequence
of such passions entered into life, we see from the counsel of the
God-inspired scriptures one way out of these, to be separated from such a life
which has joined with itself the following of painful things. For it is not
possible for one who loves to stay in Sodom to escape the rain of fire, nor
for one who has come outside Sodom and then turned again toward its desolation
not to be fixed as a pillar of salt, nor will he be freed from the slavery of
the Egyptians who does not leave Egypt, I mean this submerged life, and cross
not that Red Sea but this black and gloomy sea of life. And if, as the Lord
says, unless "the truth frees us," we remain in the evil of slavery, how is it
possible for the one who seeks the lie and "turns about in the error of life"
to come to be in the truth? And how will one flee this slavery who has given
his own life under hand to the necessities of nature? And the account about
these things would become clearer to us through some example. As some river
roughened by winter streams and carried according to its own nature, taking up
in its current woods and stones and all that happens by, is unsafe and
dangerous only to those who come to be in it, but for those who guard
themselves from it from afar it flows in vain, so also the turmoil of life

passions which nature, flowing in sequence with itself, necessarily inflicts
on those who travel through it, flooding them with the evils of life. But if
someone should leave "this torrent," as Scripture says, "and the water that

snare with the wing of virtue.
4.7 For since, according to the example spoken by us of the torrent, human
life, flooding with all kinds of turmoils and irregularities, is always
carried, poured down the slope of nature, and none of the things pursued in it
stands nor waits for the filling of those who desire, but all that falls in
both drew near and, having touched, ran past, and what is always present
escapes perception by the violence of the passing, the eyes being snatched by
the inflow from behind; for this reason it would be profitable to hold
ourselves far from such a stream, so that we may not, clinging to unstable
things, neglect the things that always stand. For how is it possible for the
one


who is attached to something in this life to have the desired thing through to
the end? Which of the things most pursued remains such forever? What prime of
youth? What good fortune of strength or form? What wealth? What glory? What
power? Do not all such things, having flowered a little time, flow away again
and fall over to the contrary name? Who lived through all in youth? For whom
did strength last through to the end? And the flower of beauty, did not nature
make it even shorter-lived than the things that appear in spring? Those at
least sprouted when the season came, and having flowered a little time bloomed
again, and flowed away again, and were leafy again, and showed the present
beauty also toward next year. But the human flower nature showed once in the
spring of youth and then quenched, making it vanish in the winter of old age.
Likewise also all the other things, having deceived the perception of the
flesh for some time, then ran past and were covered with forgetfulness. 4.8
Since then such changes, happening by some necessity of nature, altogether
grieve the one who comes to be in attachment, there is one flight from such
evils, that none of the things that change be attached to the soul, but as far
as possible to be separated from association with all the passionate and
fleshly life, or rather even to come outside sympathy with one's own body, so
that one may not, living according to flesh, become liable to the disasters
from the flesh. And this is to live by the soul alone and to imitate as far as
possible the way of life of the bodiless powers, in which "they neither marry
nor are given in marriage," but their work and zeal and achievement is to
behold the Father of incorruption and to beautify their own form toward the
archetypal beauty through the imitation that is possible. 4.9 We say then that
virginity has been given to man as fellow-worker of this mind and of the lofty
desire, as it seems good to Scripture, and as helper. And as for the remaining
pursuits certain arts were devised toward the working of each of the things
pursued, so it seems to me that the pursuit of virginity also is some art and
power of the more divine life, teaching those who live in flesh to be made
like the bodiless nature.
5.t Chapter 5. That the passionlessness of the soul must lead the way before
bodily purity.
5.1 For all the zeal of such a life is this, that the high thing of the soul
not be lowered through the rising of pleasures, and that instead of walking on
high and looking to the things above our mind, having been carried down to the
passions of flesh and blood, fall. For how can it still look up with a free
eye toward the kindred and intelligible light, the soul nailed down to the
pleasure of the flesh and occupying desire with human passions, when it has
its inclination toward the material from some wicked and uneducated
prejudgment? For as the eyes of swine, turned downward by nature, are without
experience of the wonders of heaven, so the soul dragged down with the body
will no longer be able to look toward heaven and the beauties above, stooping
toward the low and beastlike of nature. So that our soul may especially look
up free and unhindered toward the divine and blessed pleasure, it will turn
itself toward none of the earthly things nor will it partake of the pleasures
thought conceded according to the allowance of the common life,


but it will transfer the erotic power from the bodily to the intelligible and
immaterial contemplation of the beautiful. For such a disposition of the soul,
then, virginity of the body was devised for us, so that it might especially
produce in the soul forgetfulness and amnesty of the passionate movements of
nature, bringing no necessity to be occupied with the low debts of the flesh.
For once freed from such dues it no longer risks that by gradual habituation
about the things that seem conceded by some law of nature it come to be in
turning away and ignorance of the divine and undefiled pleasure, which only
purity of heart of the ruling part in us is of a nature to hunt.
6.t Chapter ʹ That Elijah and John practiced the strictness of this life.
6.1 For this reason it seems to me that the great one among prophets, Elijah,
and he who after him came to life "in spirit and power of Elijah," than whom
"no one is greater among those born of women," if their history also hints
anything else through a riddle, this especially they teach by their own life,
that he who is occupied with the contemplation of the invisibles is separated
from the sequence of human life, so that he may not, having been habituated to
such deceits that come through the senses, suffer some confusion and error
about the judgment of the really good. For both straight from youth were
estranged from human life and as it were set themselves outside nature both by
contempt in food and drink of the customary and recognized nourishment and by
the way of life in the desert, so that hearing too was kept un-echoed for
them, and sight unlifted, and taste remained simple and without business, the
need of each being filled from what came to hand. From this they came to much
fair weather and calm from the noises outside, and for this reason they were
raised to such greatness of the divine gifts as the account remembers about
each of them. For Elijah, having been set as some steward of the gifts of God,
was lord at will both to shut the supplies from heaven to those who sin and to
let them go to those who repent; but as for John, the divine history says he
did no such wonder-working, yet the gift in him is witnessed by the one who
sees hidden things as more than according to every prophet; perhaps because
they dedicated their own desire, pure and unmixed with all material
attachment, from beginning to end to the Lord, occupying it neither toward
affection for children nor toward care of women nor toward any other of human
things; they who did not even suppose the care of daily necessary food fitting
for themselves and, shown better than dignity of garments, improvised their
needs from what came to hand, the one covered "with goat-skins," the other
"with camel's hair," not having reached, as I think, to that greatness, if
they had been softened by the sweet-sufferings of the body under marriage. And
these things are not written simply, but, as the Apostle says, "for our
admonition," so that we may also direct our own life toward theirs. What then
do we learn from these? That according to the likeness of the holy men the one
who desires to be joined to God should occupy his mind with none of the
affairs of life. For it is not possible for the one poured out in mind toward
many things to go straight toward the understanding and desire of God.


6.2 And it seems to me that I could set the teaching about these things more
clearly through an example. For let us suppose some water poured from a spring
to be split according to what happens into different outflows. Then as long as
it is carried so, it will be fit for none of the things useful for farming,
the pouring into many making the portion in each small and without force and
hard to move from weakness; but if someone should gather all its unordered
outflows and collect into one stream what was until then scattered many ways,
he would use the water, sudden and stretched together, for many of the things
beneficial and useful to life. So it seems to me also the human mind, if it is
poured everywhere, flowing and being scattered toward whatever always pleases
the senses, has no noteworthy power toward the journey to the really good; but
if recalled from everywhere and collected about itself, gathered and unpoured,
it should move toward the activity proper to it and according to nature,
nothing will be that hinders it from being carried to the things above and
touching the truth of beings. For as water roofed through a pipe is often
carried straight up to the things above by the force that presses it, not
having where it may be poured, and this although the movement according to
nature is downward-bearing for it, so also the human mind, some narrow pipe of
continence squeezing it from everywhere, will somehow be taken up by the
nature of being moved toward the desire of high things, not having where it
may leak aside. For it can never stand, having received from the Maker the
nature that is ever-moving, and being prevented from using the movement toward
vain things it is impossible that it not go straight altogether toward the
truth, being fenced off from the absurd from everywhere. For in many-roaded
places we see travelers especially not missing the straight, when having
learned beforehand the error in the other roads they turn aside. As then he
who has withdrawn from the wandering paths in traveling keeps himself on the
straight, so our mind by turning away from vain things will recognize the
truth in beings. These things then the memory of those great prophets seems to
teach us, not to be entangled with any of the things pursued in the world, and
one of the things pursued is marriage, or rather beginning and root of the
pursuit of vain things.
7.t Chapter 7. That not even marriage is among the things condemned.
7.1 And let no one think through these things that we set aside the economy of
marriage; for we are not ignorant that this too has not been made alien to the
blessing of God, but since of this the common nature of men is itself a
sufficient advocate, putting of itself the inclination toward such things in
all who come into birth through marriage, while virginity in a way stands
against nature, it would be superfluous to write with labor an exhortation and
counsel on behalf of marriage, putting forward its hard-to-fight advocate, I
mean pleasure, except perhaps because of those who counterfeit the teachings
of the Church there would be need of such words, whom the Apostle names
"having their own conscience seared," because leaving the guidance of the
Spirit through the "teaching of demons" they engrave some scars and burn-marks
on their own hearts, abominating the creatures of God as pollutions and as
carrying toward evil and causes of evils and calling them such things. But
"What


is it to me to judge those outside?" says the one who spoke. For those are
truly outside the court of the word of the mysteries, not "in the covering of
God," but "lodging" in the fold of the evil one, those "taken captive toward
his will" according to the Apostle's voice, and for this reason not
understanding that, all virtue being observed in a mean, the turning aside
toward the adjacent is vice; for one who has taken the middle everywhere of
slackening and stretching has distinguished virtue from vice.
7.2 And the account would become clearer to us when shown on the things
themselves. Cowardice and audacity, two vices thought according to the
contrary, the one according to lack, the other according to excess of
confidence, hold courage in the middle of themselves. Again the pious man is
neither godless nor superstitious; for the impiety is equal on both, both to
think there is no god and to think there are many. Do you wish to recognize
the teaching through others too? He who has fled being stingy and prodigal, in
withdrawal from the contrary passions, has achieved freedom in character; for
freedom is some such thing, neither to be disposed at random toward
measureless and useless spendings nor to be petty toward the necessary. So
also in all the rest, that we may not go through each, the account has made
known the mean of the contraries as virtue. Therefore sobriety also is a mean,
and has clear turnings aside toward vice on each side; for the one lacking in
the tone of the soul and easily worsted by the passion of pleasure and for
this reason not even having drawn near the road of the pure and sober life has
slid "into the passions of dishonor"; but the one who has gone past the
walkable of sobriety and fallen over the middle of virtue has been carried
down as into some cliff "by the teaching of demons," "searing," as the Apostle
says, "his own conscience." For in defining marriage to be abominable, he
brands himself with the reproaches of marriage; for if the tree is evil, as
the Gospel says somewhere, the fruit also is altogether worthy of the tree.
And if man is shoot and fruit of the plant according to marriage, the
reproaches of marriage altogether become those of the one who brings them.
7.3 But those, branded in conscience and beaten with weals by the absurdity of
the teaching, are convicted through such things. And we know these things also
about marriage, that the zeal and desire about divine things must lead the
way, and that one who is able to use the service of marriage soberly and in
measure must not look down on it. Such was the patriarch Isaac, who receives
the cohabitation of Rebecca not in the prime of age, so that marriage may not
become a work of passion, but when youth had already been spent down for him,
because of the blessing of God upon the seed, and having served marriage until
one labor, he was again wholly of the invisibles, having closed the bodily
senses; for this the history seems to me to signify, telling the heaviness of
the patriarch's eyes.
8.t Chapter 8. That it is hard for the one divided in soul toward many things
to hit the aim.
8.1 But let these things have it as it may seem good to those skilled to see
such things; and let us go forward to the things next of the account. What
then was what was being said? That if it is possible neither to stand away
from the more divine desire nor to flee marriage, there is no account that
sets aside the economy of


nature and slanders as abominable what is honorable. For as in the example
spoken before by us of the water and the spring, when the farmer, channeling
the water onto some field, draws it, and there comes also some need through
the middle of a small outflow, he will give only so much to leak aside as is
made commensurate with the need that seeks it, to be mixed again easily with
the stream; but if he should open the outflow to the water without experience
and without stewardship, he will risk that, leaving the straight, the whole be
gullied aside according to the flank: in the same way, since life has need
also of succession from one another, if one should so use what happens that,
the spiritual things leading the way, he uses the desire about such things
sparingly and drawn back because of the contraction of the time, this one
would be the sober farmer who farms himself in wisdom according to the
Apostle's command, not always being petty about the payments of those cold
debts, but setting apart by agreement the purity for the leisure of prayers,
who has feared that through such attachment he become wholly flesh and blood,
"in whom the Spirit of God does not remain"; but the one weakly disposed, so
as not to be able to stand manfully against the carrying of nature, would be
better holding himself far from such things rather than going down into a
contest greater than his power. For the danger is not small that such a one,
knocked aside in the trial of pleasure, think no other good except what is
taken through flesh with some attachment and, having turned his own mind
altogether away from the desire of bodiless goods, become wholly fleshly,
hunting the pleasant in these by every way, so as "to be a lover of pleasure
rather than a lover of God." Since then because of the weakness of nature it
is not of everyone to hit the due measure toward such things, and it is
dangerous for one carried outside the measure to be stuck "in the matter of
the deep" according to the Psalmist, it would be profitable, as the account
guides, to live through untried of such things, so that the passions may not,
on the pretext of the things conceded, take entrance against the soul.
9.t Chapter 9. That habit is hard to change in everything.
9.1 For habit is some impossible thing in everything, having much power to
draw and pull the soul toward itself and to furnish some appearance of the
beautiful, in whatever one acquires some relation and attachment through
habituation. And nothing is so to be fled by nature that, having come to be in
habit, it is not thought both worthy of zeal and to be chosen. Proof of the
account is human life, in which, there being so many nations, the same things
are not pursued by all, but other things are beautiful and honorable among
others, the habit among each making the zeal and desire about something. And
not only according to the nations is it possible to see such variation of the
same pursuits being admired among some and slandered among others, but also in
the same nation and the same city and family, in which it is possible to see
much difference coming to each from habit. So brothers who have come into
birth from one labor have been split very far from one another in life by
their pursuits; and this is not yet wonderful, since also each man as a rule
does not have the same judgment about the same thing, but as he is disposed
about each from habit. And that we may not speak the things far from the
matter, we have known many lovers especially of sobriety who appeared so

10.t Chapter 10. What is the truly desirable?
10.1 For how great is the damage to come to be in falling away from the
possession of the really beautiful, what account could set it forth? And what
excess of mind would one use? How could one show and bring to a sketch what is
both unspeakable in word and incomprehensible in thought? For if anyone has
had the eye of the heart purified to such a point as to be able in some
measure to see what was promised in the beatitudes by the Lord, he will
condemn every human voice as having no power toward the setting forth of what
was thought; but if anyone still sitting in the material passions has the
seeing part of the soul plastered as by some rheum of the passionate
disposition, every force of words is vain in this way too. For in those who
are without perception it stands in the same whether one lessens wonders
through the account or lifts them, as also in the case of the sun's ray, for
one who has not seen the light from first birth the interpretation of the
light through words becomes idle and without profit; for it is not possible to
shine the brightness of the ray through hearing. So also in the case of the
intelligible and true light each has need of his own eyes, so that he may
behold that beauty, which the one who has seen it by some divine gift and
inspiration has the astonishment uninterpreted in the secret of conscience;
but the one who has not seen will not even know the damage of the things of
which he has been deprived. For how could one set forth to him the good that
has escaped him? How could one bring under sight the


unspeakable? We have not learned our own voices significant of that beauty.
There is no example of what is sought among beings; it is impossible for it to
be shown from comparison. For who likens the sun to a small spark, or sets a
short drop against the infinity of the abysses? For the relation which the
small drop has toward the abysses, or a little spark toward the great ray of
the sun, so also all the things admired as beautiful by men are disposed
toward that beauty which is contemplated about the first good and that beyond
every good. 10.2 What thought then will show the greatness of the damage to
the one who has suffered this damage? The great David seems to me to have
shown this impossibility well, who when at some time his mind was lifted by
the power of the Spirit and as it were going out of himself he saw that
impossible and unthinkable beauty in that blessed ecstasy, and he saw it
altogether as it is possible for a man to see, having come outside the
coverings of the flesh and having entered through the mind alone into the
contemplation of the bodiless and intelligible, when he desired to say
something also worthy of what was seen, he cried out that voice which all
sing, that "Every man is a liar"; and this is, as my account has it, that
every man who entrusts to voice the interpretation of the unspeakable light is
truly a liar, not by hatred of the truth, but by the weakness of the telling.
For the perceptible beauty, as much as turns below about our life, whether
appearing in soulless materials or also in ensouled bodies through some good
color, our power according to perception is sufficient both to admire and to
accept and to make known to another, the such beauty being written through the
writing of words as in some image in the account; but of that whose archetype
escapes understanding, how could the account bring it under sight, finding no
device of sketching, not having color to say, nor shape, nor size, nor good
fortune of form, nor any such nonsense at all? For the altogether formless and
shapeless and alien to all magnitude and seated far from all things that are
contemplated about body and perception, how could one make it known through
the things grasped by perception alone? Yet we must not for this reason
despair of this desire, because it appears higher than comprehension, but by
as much as the account has shown the thing sought to be great, by so much more
must the mind be lifted and raised together with the greatness of the thing
sought, so as not to come altogether outside the participation of the good;
for the danger is not small, that by the too high and unspeakable we slide
altogether from the thought about it, supporting our understanding of them on
nothing known.

12.t Chapter 12. That he who has cleaned himself will see in himself the

12.1 But this perhaps no one is ignorant of; and it is likely that some seek

the aim set before it is possible for each to gather richly from both of the

12.2 This rational and thinking living being, man, having become a work and
imitation of the divine and undefiled nature (for so it has been written about
him in the cosmogony, that "He made him according to image of God"), this
living being then, man, did not have according to nature nor co-essential in
himself at the first birth the passionate and perishable (for it was not
possible for the account of the image to be saved, if the imaged beauty had
itself contrary to the archetype), but later the passion was brought in upon

reason also in the self-ruled of choice he had the likeness toward the one who
has authority over all, enslaved to no necessity from outside, but
administered toward what seemed good by his own judgment and choosing what

himself, himself becoming inventor of wickedness, not finding it having come
to be from God; "for God did not make death," but in a way man was set as
founder and craftsman of evil. For as of the sun's light the participation
lies common before all who have the power of seeing, yet one can, if he should
wish, having closed the eye, come outside the grasping of the light, not the
sun departing elsewhere and so bringing the darkness upon that one, but the

things beside nature

that falls to such an aim, for whom it is dear to learn through exactness,
there are also written teachings teaching these things, but more working than
the teaching from words is the guidance through the works, and there is no
difficulty present to the matter, as needing either to undergo a long journey
or much sailing to hit the one who educates, but "Near you is the word," says
the Apostle, the grace from the hearth. Here is the workshop of the virtues,
in which such a life going forward toward the most extreme of exactness has
been cleaned. And there is much authority both of those silent here and of
those speaking to be taught this heavenly way of life through the works, since
also every account contemplated without the works, even if it happen to be
beautified as much as possible, is like a soulless image showing forth some
well-flowered character in dyes and colors; but the one who does and teaches,
as the Gospel says somewhere,


this one is truly a living man and seasonable in beauty and working and
moving.
23.2 Therefore one must go as a student to this one who is going to hold to
virginity according to the choosing account. For as the one eager to learn
thoroughly the voice of some nation is not a sufficient teacher for himself,
but is educated by those who know and so becomes same-voiced with those of
other tongue, so I think also of this life not going forward according to the
sequence of nature, but made alien by the newness of the way of life, that one
not otherwise learn the exactness than being led by the hand by the one who
has achieved. And all the other things too, as many as we pursue according to
life, would rather be achieved by the one going after them, if one should
learn thoroughly the science from teachers of each of the things pursued, than
if he should put his hand to the matter from himself; for this pursuit is not
evident, so as of necessity to entrust to ourselves the judgment of the
beneficial, when even to dare the trial of the unknowns is not set outside
danger. And as men found the medical art, unknown before, through trial, it
having been unveiled by some observations little by little, so that both the
benefiting and the harming, recognized through the witness of those who have
tried, were so taken into the account of the art and the observed in those who
went before became a command toward the future, but now the one eager toward
this art has no necessity to distinguish by trial according to himself the
power of the drugs, whether it is some harmful or some defending, but having
learned the things known from others he himself has achieved the art: in the
same way also of the medical art of souls, I mean philosophy, through which we
learn the healing of every passion that touches the soul, there is no
necessity to go after the science by some guesses and suspicions, but with
much authority of the learning from the one who has acquired the habit through
long and much trial. For as a rule also upon every matter youth is an unsafe
counselor, and one would not easily find something of the things worthy of
zeal achieved, with which grey hair has not been taken along toward fellowship
of the consideration; and by as much as the aim lying before those who go
after it is greater than the remaining pursuits, by so much more also we must
take thought of the safety. For upon the rest, youth not administered
according to account has altogether brought the damage into money, or has
prepared to fall from some worldly distinction or also dignity; but upon this
great and high desire it is not money that is risked, nor worldly and daily
glory, nor any other of the things that follow us from outside (of which,
whether administered according to judgment or otherwise, the account is small
for the sober), but the ill-counsel touches the soul itself, and the danger of
this damage is not to be damaged some other thing, of which perhaps the
taking-up also appears possible, but to destroy himself and to be damaged his
own soul. For the one who has spent the paternal substance does not despair
perhaps by some thoughts to return again upon the ancient plenty, as long as
he is among the living; but the one who has fallen from this life has had
taken away together all hope of the change toward the better.
23.3 Therefore since the many who take hold of virginity are still young and
incomplete in mind, this before all would be for them to pursue, to seek a
good leader and teacher of this road, lest somehow through the ignorance that
is in them they innovate for themselves some pathless ways and wanderings from


the straight. "For two are good above the one," says Ecclesiastes; and the one
is easily worsted by the enemy who lies in wait according to the divine roads,
and truly "woe to the one when he falls," because he has not the one who sets
him up. For already some have used a right impulse toward the desire of the
solemn life, but as having touched completeness together with having chosen,
they have been tripped by another fall through pride, having deceived
themselves through some mind-harm to think that beautiful toward which their
mind may incline. From these are those named by Wisdom idle, who have strewn
their own roads with thorns, who think harm of the soul the eagerness about
the works of the commands, who have written off the apostolic counsels and do
not eat their own bread well-shaped, but gaping toward the alien make idleness
an art of life; from here the dreamers who make the deceits from dreams more
faithful than the evangelical teachings and name the phantasies revelations;
"from these are those who slip into the houses" and again others who think the
unmixed and beastlike a virtue and do not recognize the command of love nor
know the fruit of long-suffering and lowliness of mind.
23.4 And who could go through all such falls, into as many as one is carried
by not wishing to add himself to those well-reputed according to God? For from
these we have also known those who endure hunger until death, as "God being
well-pleased with such sacrifices," and again others who have stood away
diametrically toward the contrary, who having pursued unmarriedness as far as
a name differ in nothing from the common life, not only granting the things
toward pleasure to the belly, but also cohabiting with women according to the
manifest and naming such a living-together brotherhood, as covering the
suspicion toward the worse with a more solemn name; through whom also this
solemn and pure pursuit is exceedingly blasphemed by those outside.
23.5 Therefore it would be profitable that the young not legislate for
themselves the road of this life; for the examples of the good have not left
our life, but even now exceedingly, if ever, the solemnity has flowered and
dwells in our life, having been made exact toward the most extreme by the
additions little by little, of which it is possible for the one walking around
in such tracks and following behind the smell of this myrrh to be filled "of
the good-smell of Christ." For as when one torch has been lit the handing-over
of the flame comes to be into all the lamps that draw near, the first light
neither being lessened and coming to those lighted through participation
according to the equal, so also the solemnity of this life is handed over from
the one who has achieved it upon those who draw near; for true is the
prophetic account, "that the one who lives with the holy and harmless and
chosen becomes such."

good example, the sketch is easy. If you see a life of a man showing in the
middle of death and life, neither turned altogether toward death, nor having
stepped with the whole foot upon life, but in the things in which life of
flesh is tested numbered among the dead, but toward the works of virtue,

still far from the turmoil of the waves, having put forward as some beacons
from a high beacon-place the brightness of their own lives to those who
follow. Therefore we have toward whom having looked we may sail through the
surge of the temptations safely.
23.7 Why do you busy yourself with me, if some of those who have thought these
things have been worsted, and for this reason despair as of an impossible
matter? Look toward the one who has achieved, and taking heart dare the good
sailing, being steered by the in-breathing of the Holy Spirit under Christ as
steersman. For neither have "those who go down into the sea in ships and do
work in many waters" made the shipwreck that happened to someone a hindrance
of the hopes, but putting forward their own good hope they hasten upon the
bound of the achievement. Or would it not be of all things most absurd to set
it as wicked that someone has slipped in the exactly-lived life, but to think
it better counsel to grow old the whole life in slips? For if it is terrible
to draw near sin once, and for this reason you think it safe not even to put
the hand to the higher aim, how much harder is it to make sin a pursuit of
life and for this reason to remain altogether without share of the purer life?
How do you hear the crucified, you who live, of the one who has died to sin,
you who are healthy according to it, of the one who commands the following
behind him, bearing the cross upon the body as some trophy against the
opposed, you who are not crucified to the world and do not receive the
deadening of the flesh? How are you persuaded by Paul who calls you "to
present your body a living holy sacrifice well-pleasing to God," "you who are
conformed to this age and not transformed by the renewal of your mind" nor
"walking in the newness of this life," but still treating the sequence of the
life of the old man? How do you priest to God though you have been anointed
unto this very thing, unto offering a gift to God, and a gift not altogether
some alien nor substituted from the things that follow you from outside, but
what is truly yours, which is your inner man, owing to be complete and without
blame according to the law about the lamb, released from every stain and
damage? How then will you offer these things to God, you who do not hear the
law that prevents the unclean from being priest? And if you also long for God
to appear to you, why do you not hear Moses commanding the people to be pure
from marriage, so that they may have room for the appearing of God? And if
these things seem small to you, the "being crucified together with Christ,"
the "presenting yourself a sacrifice to God," the "becoming a priest of God
the most high," the being counted worthy of the great appearing of God, what
shall we think up higher than these for you, if perhaps to you


small even the things from these will seem? For from the being crucified
together, both to live together and to be glorified together and to reign
together come; and from presenting himself to God, it is possible to be
transferred from the human nature and worth into the angelic; for so Daniel
says that "A thousand thousands stood beside him." And the one who has taken
hold of the true priesthood and has ordered himself with the great high priest
"remains altogether himself also a priest unto the age, no longer being
prevented by death from remaining unto the continuous." And of being counted
worthy to see God himself there is no other fruit than this very thing, to be
counted worthy to see God; for the peak of every hope and the achievement of
every desire, and of blessing of God and of every promise and of the
unspeakable goods that have been believed to be above perception and
knowledge, the bound and the head is this, which Moses also longed to see and
many prophets and kings desired; and only the pure in heart are counted
worthy, who for this reason are truly blessed both being and being named,
because they will see God, of whom we wish you also to become one, "having
been crucified together with Christ," having stood beside God a holy priest
and having become a pure sacrifice, having prepared himself in all purity
through chastity for the presence of God, so that you also "may see God in the
pure heart" according to the promise of God and our Savior Jesus Christ, to
whom the glory and the power unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
