51, lab Words of Ecclesiastes, son of David, king of Israel in Jerusalem. This
Solomon who is assembling and saying such things as it is fitting for an
assembly to say, is "son of David." He came to be after Saul, whose kingdom
was slandered and cut off. Of this "king," of this "David," Solomon is "son"
according to both manners. For in two ways some are "sons" of some: "according
to flesh" and succession, and according to education. Paul at any rate, saying
that "through the gospel I begot you," "I am your father," "for even if you
have countless pedagogues, yet not many fathers; for through the gospel I
begot you." David then was father of Solomon in both ways: both "according to
flesh" (for from him he had the succession, "and David begot Solomon"), but
also according to education he was his father; for "wise," if anyone else was,
and this very thing God-wise, was David. He says at any rate: "the hidden and
secret things of your wisdom you made clear to me." And if someone should say:
"more boldly than all he speaks, praising himself, he is not altogether such,"
I hear that God also says: "I found David the son of Jesse a man after my
heart, who will do all my wills." And the doer of "the wills" of God, as of
"all" the virtues, is "wise," if anyone else is. To him God has manifested
"the hidden and secret things of his wisdom." And Solomon also became "wise,"
if anyone else. He became then from a wise man "wise," just as also a human
from a human. According to both manners of generation then Solomon was "son"
of "David." And he is especially, in those things in which he assembles,
primarily "son of David," not so much through the "according to flesh" as
through that according to education. For the "sons" of the wise, themselves
also being wise, beget others. But not altogether does the one who is "son" of
someone "according to flesh" himself also become a father. Many at any rate
having become "sons" were not shown to be fathers. But the "sons according to
spirit" themselves also become fathers. What he says then, "words of
Ecclesiastes," are Solomon's; for according to different aspects Solomon had
the names. "Ecclesiastes" indeed, when he says such things as fit an assembly,
that is a multitude; and an assembly is nothing other than a multitude.
According to this signification God also says to Moses, "assemble the people
toward me," and Jesus then, when he speaks "the parables" "to those outside."
When he does not assemble, then "in private to his own" he speaks and says
"the mystery" this to me and to those of mine. Since then here he is
"Ecclesiastes" (for the lessons almost all these, which throughout his whole
book he says, he says to those "poor" in understanding), he speaks the
"temporary" and "vain" things to those astonished at them and thinking them
great and worth fighting for. In the Proverbs (and this is first book 1–2 in
order of the present one) he is a teacher of ethical lessons. And since the
thoughts ready at hand are quickly despised, he says them with concealment
proverbially. He says at any rate: 6 "and good perception to all who do it"
and "beginning of wisdom fear of the Lord," "hear, children, discipline of a
father." The Proverbs then are ethical. But in the Song of Songs he speaks the
things above the physical, the supercosmic things that lead up into the
supercosmic and superheavenly. And because of this there nothing is added
showing him a human; it has not been said "son of David the king of Israel in
Jerusalem"; for supercosmic is the education spoken in the Song of Songs.
Question: was not


the addition superfluous? No; for in the same book both lie. The Ecclesiastes
speaks physical things. I say, twofold is the nature of the beings: "God"
...... made "things visible and invisible." And these "visible and invisible,"
distinguishing them from themselves and making them clear, the apostle himself
says: "the things seen are temporary, but the things not seen eternal." And
the "eternal" then, those like God and dedicated toward God, and the
"temporary" have a certain wise reason according to which they came to be. For
even the sense-perceiving and "visible" things came to be by a certain wise
reason. We need then teaching of both. The greater part in Ecclesiastes is
spoken about the sense-perceptible and seen things, not only created things
but also pursuits. But in the Song of Songs all things are intelligible,
contemplative, superheavenly. No one at any rate, not even those very "poor"
in understanding, takes the Song of Songs at the letter. Only Seras the Arian
wanted to interpret it thus, ...... he said this daughter of Pharaoh was the
"bride" being hymned in the book. And we examined her often, bringing forward
texts which cannot be taken at the letter. For when he says that "your two
breasts are as two fawns of a gazelle feeding among the lilies, until the day
breathe and the shadows be moved," what beauty do the "breasts" have, when
they are compared to these? And since they are compared simply to "fawns of a
gazelle," because of this he says: "feeding among the lilies"; for they are
otherwise, when they "feed among the lilies." And there are many texts. Here
then the things that have gone above the physical, the eternal things, which
those who look at "the things above," those "storing treasure in heavens,"
must consider. Question: here he speaks about the sense-perceptible things
with a riddle? He wishes to teach that even the sense-perceptible things are
administered by the providence of God and that all the pursuits concerning
"human things" are "vain," rather ...... not so as to be thrown out
altogether; but by comparison with the supercosmic they are slight. For both
"the things seen" and "the things not seen" are "works of God"; but "the
things seen," being compared toward "the things not seen," would seem to be
"vanity." Question: as toward those who attend only to "the visible"? Yes;
toward those who think these things great and nothing more. And so that ......
having used a word ...... the understanding; those who say "let us eat and
drink, for tomorrow we die" have been struck with amazement at the "temporary"
things alone. Those "loving the world and the things in the world," not
reckoning that "the world is passing and its desire," have their judgment upon
"vain things." If however the 7 seen things are compared with the invisible,
by comparison they appear as nothing, not simply nothing. And I have such
things to show from the scripture. The holy one in the 72nd psalm, having said
many things, then having shown that he condemned the things spoken by the
worthless and himself ...... became toward the contemplation of these, says:
"I became beastlike beside you." For he did not become so once for all but
"beside you." And again Moses: "but I am speechless," as toward you, the true
Word; for he would not choose as educator and general and lawgiver one who had
become "speechless." And in a way also the "but I am a worm and not a human"
suggests this understanding, and Abraham: "since I began to speak toward the
Lord, and I am earth and ashes." Question: "words of Ecclesiastes" from the
person of the writer? Properly, upon the "God-inspired scriptures," the writer
is the spirit that suggested that they be spoken, but it is served by some
wise one. For neither did the spirit invisibly inscribe the letters and set
the words, but it inspires these things into a certain soul. And either
Solomon himself is the one writing them, or some of the wise wrote them. And
perhaps


we rather prefer this, so that the one saying these things may not seem to be
speaking about himself. "Words" then instead of discourses are "of
Ecclesiastes." For in many ways the "words" are taken through discourses. As
it is when ............. they say .......... toward a discourse ........ We
call the word the brevity ............... but ............. discourse, when
again he says a word ... ... into the one who spoke: "the Lord will give a
word to those who evangelize," ......... to say Lord "came to be toward
Jeremiah"; ............. him. And it can be from parallel, that from the short
things the one practicing begins to try a complete discourse, instead of
"words of Ecclesiastes" discourses "of Ecclesiastes"; "of Ecclesiastes"
according to the aspect of the "Ecclesiastes." As it is "to know Christ
according to flesh" and "no longer" "according to flesh," if he himself
especially was the "Ecclesiastes" now, in that he "speaks the parables" to the
many "outside" who crowd, he is "Ecclesiastes." But in that he "explains" "the
parables" inside "to the disciples" with an inner discourse, he is "no longer"
"Ecclesiastes," but Solomon singing the Song of Songs, the supercosmic
discourses that lead up into "kingdom of heavens." 1,1b Words of Ecclesiastes.
I said that "son of David" is spoken in two ways, both according to education
and according to race. And it is not in that he is his "son" that he says
these things, but in that he is "wise" "son" of a wise man, since Absalom also
could have said these things, having become even a sense-perceptible "king"
and from this. But of a seeing "Israel" that has a mind seeing God he is
"king," and of the intelligible Israel he is "king." And not in a chance
place, but "king in Jerusalem"; and he will reign also in the "upper" one, the
spiritual city that sees the peace. And let this too be said in addition to
what has been said, since often some falsely write books and misuse titles.
Clement at any rate says many things ...... he presented 8 falsely inscribed
books through many; for the man, having also read many things and remembering
and knowing, showed that some added to foreign books certain strange things,
and some even composing whole discourses wrote them down. Because of this at
any rate our discourse also forbids the reading of the apocrypha, since many
things were falsely written. And someone having written inscribed it a gospel,
if it happened, according to Thomas or according to Peter. And well a certain
ancient bishop, an ecclesiastical one, said: "because of this," he says, "we
hinder the reading of the apocrypha because of those not able to distinguish
the things mixed in them by heretics." And he says a general account toward
the contentious and those saying that one must read them, and he says: "if
they say the same things as those published, we have without contradiction the
published ones and we do not take on in addition to them the things that can
have contradiction. But if in truth they do not say the same things as those
published, we think them to be declined." Because of the deceit then that can
come to be from false inscription there lies both the race and the beginning
and the nation and the city; but for all to run together at once is
impossible. This Solomon the "Ecclesiastes," in that he is "Ecclesiastes,"
puts forth "words," being "son of David king of Israel in Jerusalem." ........
to speak about nature, so that conversing with the one who has a mind that
sees he may lead others up through the sense-perceptible upon the
intelligible. For of the "works of God" some are "visible," some "invisible."
And the "visible" are "temporary" and "seen," but the "invisible" happen to be
"eternal." Here then he makes the discourse primarily about the "visible" and
"temporary"; therefore he also named them "vanity of vanities," not


reviling them according to themselves, but by setting them beside the
intelligible and supercosmic. Often at any rate even someone introduced into
education and having spent time in the introductory discourses and having been
active, as is possible, and having advanced much in them, when he has come to
be in the wisdom above, says "I knew nothing before." And the nothing is
spoken in comparison of the greater things. If then he speaks about the
"visible" created works, of the ordering of the world, he calls them "vanity
of vanities" as toward the intelligible substance, as toward God, as toward
the "angels," the "principalities," the "authorities"; even if a "visible"
"sun" is spoken, as toward that "of righteousness" it is "vanity" and nothing;
and if a sense-perceptible "rock" is spoken that "gave drink" to the "people"
of the Jews "in the wilderness," as toward the "spiritual rock" (and this "is
Christ") it is "vanity." He does not then revile the creation. Question: about
this ordering? Not altogether about this; for neither are these "the all," but
also the things indicated through them. If we do not wish to take "God saw all
things whatsoever he made, and behold they were very good" about the ordering
alone, the things spoken as lesser and "vain" by comparison of greater things
have not been taken away from the lessening; 9 and I say at least: as toward
the first intelligible and "invisible" "works" the "visible" creation of God
is cheap. But this according to itself is great; "from the greatness" at any
rate "of beauty and of created things analogously their generator is
contemplated." I say: often there are sciences shown through symbols; and the
symbols themselves according to themselves are great, but being set beside the
words of God indicated through sons they appear to be very slight. And if
every discourse is slighter than the word of God indicated through a son, yet
it was not known that it is short; as toward that it is short once for all.
Even if then the things appearing in the ordering were "very good," simply
toward the supercosmic they are nothing. And so that I may take it from an
apostolic saying: the one "being given milk to drink" because of being "an
infant" "in Christ" and "fleshly" receives something after this "milk."
Question: as toward whom then is the discourse, those thinking the nothing to
be great, or toward those who have gone up? Those being assembled; and among
those being assembled are these very ones being introduced and educated, and
others being educated in these things, so that through them they may go
forward upon the greater. Will you not say that in each place of a problem
there are both those beginning and those perfected? There are those beginning,
and there are also those so perfected as to have to pass over into the second
already; some then he teaches to despise the "vain" things, and others he
educates to go forward through them and to hasten upon the greater, so as to
say: "as we look not at the things seen, but at the things not seen." Yet such
a one "looks at" both. When different things, greater and lesser, are spoken
to the same person, they seem to be opposed. He cannot at any rate be in
science of both at once and at the same time. But when he speaks toward a
multitude (Paul at any rate writes to Corinthians, to the whole church: "I
give thanks that in everything you were enriched in him in every word and all
knowledge"), he does not say: "I gave you milk to drink, not food." Those
"being given milk to drink," "not able" to receive "food," because "you are
not yet able," are not the same as those to whom he says that "I give thanks
that in everything you were enriched," since they are opposed. Then again,
accepting to speak "wisdom of God" upon perfection to such, he says:
"fornication is altogether heard among you, and such fornication as is not
even among the nations"; and he still slanders them as "not knowing" the
account concerning "resurrection" and the contemplation of the "spiritual"
thoughts.


If then they were being said toward one and the same at the same time, they
would be fighting; announced by voice to a multitude they have no fight. So
also the things said here by the "Ecclesiastes" to the assembly; not all the
things announced in this book must be fitted to one and the same person, but
to a whole multitude of an assembly. Yet even those who have gone above are
educated through the sense-perceptible things and because of this they need
"Ecclesiastes." 10 Do you not say that the "disciples" of Jesus were present
among those in the "crowds" "outside," when "the parables" were being said?
They were not moved in the same way; at any rate at least, having entered into
the "spiritual" "house" of Jesus and having heard the inner things (thereafter
they "ask" about the "parables"), they "knew" that something other besides the
wording is signified ...... and some perhaps were only moved, but others even
remained in the history. If then he says these things about one and the same
person, they are fighting and contrary; but in a multitude, as of a city or
assembly or people, they are not fighting. And take a voice of the apostle
himself: "to those without law," he says, "I became without law, to the Jews a
Jew, to all I became all things." Not to each at the same hour "all things."
1,2ab Vanity of vanities, said the Ecclesiastes. He does not say that "Solomon
said," but "the Ecclesiastes." Not that he is other and other in hypostasis,
but in aspect; in that he is "Ecclesiastes," he has spoken these things.
Question: was it sufficient? the 1,2b "vanity of vanities, all things vanity"?
This he says: there is a difference of the "vain" things; some are such
intensively, others not so. ...... of the example those of the "vain" things
taking a beginning of education ............... going out ... of the
sense-perceptible we all have an apprehension ...... philosophies not going
out beyond the sense-perceptible ...... of "vanities" ...... intensive
"vanity" is "vanity" "of vanity," as if you should say science of sciences,
virtue of virtues. Even if "vanity" has the difference spoken, as those just
now taking trial of the contraries and those who have advanced in them, yet
"all things are vanity." Question: about the same? About both, both the simply
"vain" and those that are "vanities of vanities." And the intensive in being
"vain" and the lesser "vain" "all" are "vain" toward the truth itself. The
newborn infant and the child and the youth are incomplete; yet toward the
grown boy they are incomplete; yet "all" are incomplete as toward the man. The
aim of education is "to speak wisdom among the perfect." "Vain" in the manner
spoken is to educate the "Jews," "so that he may gain" them, the one
educating, and to "become under law" or to "become without law." These have
much difference toward themselves. But "all things" as toward the right
education, according to which someone can "present every human perfect to
Christ," "he became to all," since he is "perfect." Toward him all the other
educators are incomplete. Even if then the same one has all, we take him now
in one aspect, now in another. Question: did he not call "the visible" and
sense-perceptible "vanity of vanities"? The untrained and the mathematician do
not see the sun in the same way. And to see the sun mathematically and
according to physiology has fallen much below "the God who is invisible" and
the knowledge that is "from God." 11 And consider: "because what is knowable
of God is manifest," the one grasping "the eternal power of God and the
divinity" from the "things understood" similarly receives the things seen and
that itself which is shown from them, I mean the divine "power,


and accepts it. The zeal concerning the "vain" things is to live thus and to
be a citizen thus as one spending time in the things seen and in seeing the
sun; as toward "seeing God with a pure heart," they are "vain." And so that I
may say it from the scriptures: "circumcision" has been legislated; and not
all know the meaning of the verbal "circumcision"; for others take it
otherwise and for a different cause; yet the contemplation and grasp of this
as toward the "circumcision in heart" is "vanity." For the "circumcision in
heart" is this "purity," that one is not. Why does it come to be? But the
sense-perceptible, in whatever way it comes to be, comes to be because of the
prototype, and the one is for the sake of teaching, but that one for the sake
of seeing it and being qualified according to it. 1,3ab What surplus is there
for the human in every toil in which he toils under the sun? ... I was saying
that having attended to the sense-perceptible ordering he speaks also about
the things pursued and practiced by "humans"; now then he speaks about the
pursuits practiced by "humans." And he says that what more does the one
"toiling under the sun" have? For the one hastening to gather wealth does not
have something more from gathering wealth; in that he is "human," he remains
mortal, receiving diseases, subject to passions. And these things are clear
even from sense itself, that those who are rich have nothing more than those
praised according to the natural things. For they do not become
"longer-lived." The rich and the poor are in the same things, quickly passing
through life and remaining not a long time. "In every" then "in which the
human toils under the sun" he has nothing more, there comes to be for him no
addition of nature. But the one "toiling" and being zealous about those things
that lead up into the "eternal," into the "not seen," has something more; from
"human" he becomes god and from careless steadfast and from cowardly brave.
Question: what more does the one "toiling" have? "Under the sun" is the one
who has this life. For those "having their citizenship in heavens" have come
to be above the sense-perceptible "sun"; ...... having turned away from the
"temporary" and having come to be in the "eternal" things that do not appear,
they are above "the sun." I do not say above this "sun," that they are
greater, but they have life "above." The air is "under the sun" and the earth.
Those then living and conducting life according to the earth and the air are
"under the sun." For I am not mad, as some say, that the "human" is "greater"
than the "sun." They say this, that he is a slave, he says, "of the human."
But not altogether is the one "serving" "lesser" than that one whom he
"serves." And Paul writes that "ourselves slaves of you through Christ"; for
if the educators are slaves of those being educated, they are not 12 "lesser"
than they. They do this by a good choice; "in love they serve one another,"
the holy ones. 1,4ab A generation goes and a generation comes, and the earth
stands into the age. This he says: none of those "toiling under the sun" has
something extra, so as to remain until the "consummation" of the "world."
Humans subsist from succession; and of the created works of God these are
continuous and without interval, but those that have interval and have
existence from succession; as the sun, from when it came to be and for as long
as God wishes it, is the same. But if I say again that the human always
remains, I do not say one and the same, but the whole succession. So also
about the other mortal animals and plants; the first "generation goes" into
death, another "comes" being born. But "the earth has stood into the age."
"Into the age" then this


he says, about which it is said: "consummation of the age." And according to
anagogy we say this: in many places of the divine lessons "generation" is
spoken otherwise. As the "crooked and perverse generation"; and he means the
Jewish one. And "a generation that is coming will be announced to the Lord,"
that from the nations. He says then that the old Jewish one "goes," and there
"comes" instead of it the one called by the savior through the gospel, and
upon this very "earth," according to which both the one that has gone away and
the "coming" one spend time, it remains "into the age." And this is the
"God-inspired scripture." Question: what then toward the "what surplus in
every toil"? Interpreted into another theorem, if you take them toward the
history, this he says, that since "a generation goes," and "a generation
comes" ("goes" the one being destroyed by death, and "comes" the one being
born), the human will have nothing more from the "toiling," whether he goes
away or "comes." 1,4b And the earth has stood into the age. This he says, that
this "earth," upon which the successions of those who have come to be spend
time, remains until "the consummation," "until it pass away" with the
"heaven"; "into the age" of this age "it has stood"; and if "consummation"
comes to be, the "age" also is cut off. 1,5ab And the sun rises and the sun
sets and it draws to its place. I was saying that the "vain" things and the
"vanity of vanity" are taken in two ways, once upon the sense-perceptible
ordering, otherwise upon the human pursuits. He said the things about the
pursuits, saying that the one "toiling under the sun" has nothing "of
surplus," but is subject to the same things to which even the one not
"toiling" is. Thereafter he speaks about the seen creation, of which the
apostle also says: "the things seen are temporary"; and he says: "the sun
rises and sets," and nothing more. Again next this comes to be; and "it turns
upon its circles," "it circles toward the north," it comes to "south" and "the
spirit circles circling" and "upon its circles 13 it turns." And "spirit" now
he says either the as-it-were soul of the "sun" or the "sun" itself. Hear then
further: some of the more superficial humans, who neither physiologize nor
have made the sense-perceptible things nor the human affairs, think that the
"sun" does not accomplish its own journey according to a circular turning, but
they say thus: from here, he says, it comes and bends back and goes "to the
place." But here he says that it "goes" "upon its circles." And in Esdras this
theorem, being physical, is shown still more clearly: "great is the earth and
high the heaven and swift in course the sun, and it turns in the circle of
heaven and again runs up to its own place in one day." This then he says: even
if this theorem, being physical, the one about the "sun," has something very
beautiful, yet even it, since it is sense-perceptible and of the ordering
here, is "vanity of vanities" as toward the "above" ordering, as toward the
"sun of righteousness." The "sun of righteousness" "rises" in a soul; and the
beginning of its rising is more introductory and a more introductory
illumination. But when it has turned about the whole soul and has illuminated
it whole so that nothing thereafter is outside of light, then it has come to
be in perfect illumination. For it often "sets," so that it may "rise" again;
for according to progress a setting comes to be and a rising. And the setting
and the rising, taken by relation, have been united; the second toward the
illumination that went before is a setting, but a rising toward the one
beginning. 1,6ab Rising, it itself goes out there toward the south and circles
toward the north. The climes


he says, which it goes around. From the winds the climes are signified. At any
rate at least those measuring the earth say that also upon "north" this. This
then he says, that it travels through both the western and the midday and the
evening clime, and thus it comes "to its own place" being carried circularly.
And perhaps he named it "spirit" because of the sharpness of the course; and
nothing is sharper than spirit. 1,6c The spirit goes circling circling. And I
said that the "spirit" either because of the swiftness of the motion or
because of the "sun" also having something as it were mind and soul; and this
he signifies through the title of "spirit." 1,6d And the spirit turns upon its
circles. "Upon its own circles" "the spirit goes," since each day it makes one
"circle." Many are the days of the year, many are the "circles." At any rate
at least always the one going around heaven and turning in its appointed
"circle" makes the same yearly hours; for they are not greater and lesser. For
even if we say that this spring is wintry or the summer autumn-like, we look
to the nature of the air; 14 in an appointed way it makes the same courses,
the same days and the same months. 1,7abcd All the winter-streams go into the
sea, and the sea will not be filled; to the place where the winter-streams go,
there they themselves turn back to go. And this again must first be taken
according to history. The "winter-streams" are not self-generated rivers, not
having the beginning from a spring but from rains. "All" then these flow "into
the sea" and the "sea" does not appear to have any more water besides the flow
of the
"winter-streams." He mixed in then, as according to the history, something
forced, so that the discourse may also take anagogy. And this is not bare, he
has set something else; the "winter-streams" "to a place," from where they
start, bend back again, which does not come to be according to history.
Nevertheless, since one must also advocate for the text as far as is possible,
let us see according to what is possible as upon history. And this too must be
known, that the universal proposition is not ...... taken universally, but
sometimes upon the things as for the most part. For when the apostle says:
"for they all seek their own," he does not mean "all" humans; Timothy was not
"seeking his own," Titus, Luke, the other apostles. The universal then here
instead of the most. And many such things are borne in the scripture; as if he
says: "all turned aside"; altogether David himself; Nathan the prophet, being
in his days, had not "turned aside." And here then it can be. Even if not
"all" flow "into the sea" but some are spent on the dry land, he means the
most. These then of the "winter-streams" flowing their own rain "into the
sea," the "sea" is not increased, "is not filled"; for again it receives its
own rain. Then he says another thing: "to a place," he says, "where the
winter-streams go, there they themselves will turn back." But they cannot
"turn back"; for they are borne from mountains and mix "into the sea"; how
then do they "turn back"? According to what is possible then we will advocate
also in this wording for the text. For since exhalations come to be from the
