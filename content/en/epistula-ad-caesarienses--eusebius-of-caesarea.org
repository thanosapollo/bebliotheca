* Letter to the Caesareans

Letter of Eusebius of Caesarea, of Arian mind, to those of his
parish.
1 The matters concerning the ecclesiastical faith that were treated at
the great synod assembled at Nicaea you have, beloved, likely already
learned from elsewhere, since rumor is accustomed to outrun the accurate
account of what is done. But lest from such hearing the truth be
reported to you otherwise, we have of necessity sent you first the
writing concerning the faith that was put forward by us, then the
second, 2 which they have published after laying additions upon our own
wording. The letter from us, then, having been read in the presence of
our most God-beloved emperor and having been declared to be sound and
approved, has this form: 3 "As we received from the bishops before us,
both in the first catechesis and when we received the washing, and as we
have learned from the divine Scriptures, and as we both believed and
taught in the presbyterate and in the episcopate itself, so now also,
believing, we present our faith to you; and it is this:"
4 We believe in one God, Father, Almighty, maker of all things visible
and invisible, and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Word of God, God from
God, light from light, life from life, only-begotten Son, firstborn of
all creation, begotten of the Father before all the ages, through whom
also all things came to be; who for our salvation was made flesh and
lived among men and suffered and rose on the third day and went up to
the Father and will come again in glory to judge 5 the living and the
dead. And we believe also in one Holy Spirit. Believing that each of
these is and exists, the Father truly Father and the Son truly Son and
the Holy Spirit truly Holy Spirit, as also our Lord, sending his own
disciples to the preaching, said: "Go, make disciples of all the
nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of
the Holy Spirit." Concerning which we also affirm that we so hold and so
think and have long so held, and until death 6 contend for this faith,
anathematizing every godless heresy. That we have always thought these
things from heart and soul, from the time we have known ourselves, and
that we now both think and speak them in truth, we testify before God
Almighty and our Lord Jesus Christ, being able to show by proofs and to
persuade you that in the times past also we so believed and so preached.
7 When this faith had been set forth by us, there was no place for
contradiction for anyone, but our most God-beloved emperor himself first
bore witness that it contained things most orthodox. And he confessed
that he himself also thought thus, and he exhorted all to assent to it
and to subscribe to the dogmas and to agree with these very things, one
word only having been added, that of homoousios, which he himself also
interpreted, saying that <the Son> is not called homoousios according to
the passions of bodies, nor therefore that he came to subsist from the
Father by division or by some cutting-off; for the immaterial and
intellectual and bodiless nature cannot undergo any bodily passion, and
such things ought to be understood by divine and ineffable reasons. And
our most wise and most pious emperor philosophized in such terms. But
they, on the pretext of the addition of homoousios, have made this
writing:

8 [The faith dictated in the synod.] We believe in one God, Father,
Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible, and in one Lord
Jesus Christ, the Son of God, begotten of the Father as only-begotten,
that is, from the essence of the Father, God from God, light from light,
true God from true God, begotten not made, homoousios with the Father,
through whom all things came to be, both those in heaven and those on
earth, who for us men and for our salvation came down and was made
flesh, was made man, suffered and rose on the third day, went up into
the heavens, and is coming to judge the living and the dead. And in the
Holy Spirit. But those who say "there was when he was not" and "before
he was begotten he was not" and that "he came to be from things that
were not," or who allege that the Son of God is from another hypostasis
or essence, or created, or changeable, or alterable, the catholic Church
anathematizes.
9 And when this writing had been dictated by them, we did not leave
unexamined how they meant "from the essence of the Father" and
"homoousios with the Father." Questions and answers, therefore, were
stirred from this point, and the discussion tested the meaning of what
had been said. And "from the essence" was confessed by them to be
indicative of his being from the Father, yet not as existing as a part
of the Father. 10 In this way it seemed good to us also to assent to the
meaning, the pious teaching dictating that the Son is from the Father,
yet is not a part of his essence. Wherefore we also agreed to the
meaning, not even declining the word homoousios, the aim of peace being
set before our eyes and the concern not to fall away from the right
meaning.
11 In the same way we also accepted "begotten and not made," since they
said that "made" is a common appellation of the remaining creatures that
came to be through the Son, of which the Son has nothing similar;
wherefore he is not a thing-made resembling those that came to be
through him, but is of an essence better than every thing-made, which
the divine oracles teach has been begotten from the Father, the manner
of the generation being inexpressible and beyond reckoning for every
generated nature. 12 Thus also, when examined, the discussion
establishes that the Son is homoousios with the Father, not according to
the manner of bodies nor similarly to mortal animals, for neither by
division of the essence nor by cutting-off, nor yet by any passion or
change or alteration of the Father's essence and power. For of all these
13 the unbegotten nature of the Father is alien. And "homoousios with
the Father" is indicative that the Son of God bears no resemblance to
the generated creatures, but is likened in every way to the Father alone
who begot him, and is not from some other hypostasis and essence, but
from the Father. To this also, having been interpreted in this way, it
appeared well to assent, since we have also known certain of the
ancients, learned and distinguished bishops and writers, who used the
name homoousios in the theology of the Father and the Son.
14 Let these things, then, have been said concerning the faith that was
set forth, to which we all agreed, not unexamined, but according to the
meanings given, which were examined in the presence of the most
God-beloved 15 emperor himself and were confessed together with the
reasonings that had been spoken. And the anathema also, which was set
forth by them after the faith, we considered to be without grief,
because it forbids the use of unwritten words, through which almost all
the confusion and instability of the Church has come about. Since, then,
no God-inspired Scripture has used "from things that were not" and
"there was when he was not" and the things next said, it did not appear
reasonable to say and teach these things. To this also, seeming well, we
agreed, since neither in the time before this were we accustomed to use
these words.
16 Further, that "before he was begotten he was not" should be
anathematized was not thought out of place, because it is confessed by
all that the Son of God exists before the generation according to the
flesh. And already our most God-beloved emperor was constructing by
argument that he also exists according to his divine generation before
all ages, since even before he was begotten in act he was in the Father
in power unbegottenly, the Father being always Father, as also always
King and always Saviour, being all things in power, and always remaining
the same and in the same way.
17 These things we have of necessity sent to you, beloved, making plain
to you the judgment of our examination and assent, and how reasonably we
then, even until the last hour, stood our ground, when what had been
written otherwise offended us, and then without contention accepted the
things that caused no grief, when, as we examined the meaning of the
words with good will, it appeared to run together with what we ourselves
had confessed in the faith previously set forth.
