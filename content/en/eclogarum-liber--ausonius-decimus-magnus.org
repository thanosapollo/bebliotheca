/To whom do I give this charming new little book?/ 1
So the poet of Verona once asked, and as soon as he found him he gave it to
Nepos. But this graceless, rough little book of ours, this trash, these
sweepings and this nonsense: in whose lap shall we leave it to be kept warm? I
have found him. Be still, you anxious trifles: a man no less learned, and more
kindly, than the one Gaul gave to Catullus. None of my own people is dearer to
me than this man, whom the nine Sisters prize above all the rest, Virgil alone
excepted.

/ Pacatus, poet, you mean him without a doubt? /

He himself. Fly without fear, verses, and make your nest in a safe lap. He
will love you, he will want to protect you; he will cover what needs pardon,
and hand on what is approved. After him, fear no judgment. Farewell.

WHAT path of life shall I follow, if the forums are full of uproar, if the
house is anxious with cares, if care of the house follows those who go abroad,
if fresh losses always await the merchant, if shameful want forbids him to
rest; if toil harasses the farmer, if the horror of shipwreck brands the sea,
if a celibate life has heavy penalties and cautious husbands a still heavier,
useless watch; if the work of Mars is bloody, if the filthy gains of usury and
swift interest slaughter the poor? Every age has its cares; each man dislikes
his own time of life. Nursing infants have no understanding; then come the
hard first lessons of boys, and the rash prime of young men. Fortune afflicts
grown men through wars, through the sea, through angers and ambushes and
chained labors 1 that must always be exchanged for heavier ones. Old age
itself, long awaited and wished for with grudging prayers, exposes a body that
countless diseases can tear. We all alike scorn the present; it is well known
that some do not even want to become gods. Juturna cries out: "Why did he give
eternal life? Why was the condition of death taken away?" So under the
Caucasian crag Prometheus calls the son of Saturn to witness, and does not
cease to accuse Jupiter by name, because lasting life was given. Look also to
the cultivation of the soul: so, surely, an unlucky care for chastity
destroyed chaste Hippolytus. On the other side, whoever likes to live a life
stained with allurements, let him look at the punishments and crimes of kings,
incestuous Tereus or soft Sardanapalus. Three Punic wars warn us to shun
treachery; but ruined Saguntum forbids us to keep faith.

Live, and always keep friendships. For that very charge the learned school of
Pythagorean sages perished. Fearing this, then, keep none. For that very
charge Timon was once stoned in Pallas's Athens. The mind that meets
conflicting wishes is always at odds with itself, and for a man it is not
enough to have wished: he refuses what he wanted. To be in honor pleases, then
it irks; and that they may rule, they want to serve; the same man, raised in
honor, is thrown to envy. Night-long care belongs to the eloquent; but the
untrained man lacks the ornament of life. Be a patron, and defend the accused:
but a client's thanks are rare. Be a client: the patron's person is heavy with
commands. The prayers of fathers occupy this man; soon a harsh anxiety of
cares comes on; childless old age is despised, and the man without an heir is
the legacy-hunter's prey. Live sparingly: you will be torn by the talk of the
greedy, and censure will brand the lavish giver more heavily. Everything is
against you by contrary chances. Therefore the best saying of the Greeks is
this: they say it is good for a man not to be born, or, once born, to seize
death quickly.

This, indeed, is the Pythagorean pronouncement, according to the distich set
down as follows:

First, not to be born among men is best,

second, to pass the gates of Hades as quickly as possible. 2

But, against this, learn by reading what a follower of another doctrine
teaches in reproof, in the lines set below:

"Since, then, there is nothing in this life that we should love,

and yet it is not right to believe we were born in vain,

if we believe the author of life to be just,

another life must be won for us by living for him

with whom we may live after this life.

Let those, indeed, hurry down to the Stygian shades

who, following the stupid doctrine of the Pythagoreans,

would rather not be born than live once they are born."

reason? what did I omit? why did this opinion settle, which it would have been
better to change? Pitying a needy man, why did I take his pain so much to
heart that it broke me? what did I want that it would have been good not to
want? Why, being wrong, did I put the useful before the honorable? Was anyone
stung by a word or even by a look? why does nature draw me more than
discipline? So, walking through all words and deeds, and rolling all things
back from evening to morning, offended by the crooked he gives the palm and
the prizes to the straight.

YES and No: everyone constantly uses these well-known monosyllables. Take
these away, and there is nothing that the speech of men can toss about.
Everything is in them and from them comes everything, whether there is any
business or any leisure, whether things are troubled or quiet. Sometimes
equally in both, often apart, they stand against our pursuits, as contention
has found our manners and our nature easy or hard. If there is agreement, no
delay comes in: /Yes, yes,/

but if it is disputed, disagreement will throw in /No./

Hence the forums leap apart with shouting, hence the mad

quarrels of the circus, hence the wide sedition of the wedged theater, and the
senate too is stirred by such disputes. Wives and sons with fathers sow these
words in quiet pursuits, without harm to piety. Hence also the school,
harmonious in peaceful disciplines, stirs dogmatic disputes in a calm contest;
hence the whole dialectical crowd of sages contends. Is it day? Then it is
day! That does not fit; for as often as by many torches or by lightning there
is night-light for a man, that light is not the light of day. There is, then,
Yes and No, as often as it must be granted that there is light, but that it is
not day. A thousand contests rise from this; hence many, turning such
questions over in a hoarse murmur they keep shut in, gnaw at a mad silence.
What a life men lead, which two monosyllables toss about!

TWICE three years, and nine times ten besides, make up the just span which a
life of aging men fills. The chattering crow surpasses these nine times in
living, and the stag outstrips the crow's ages four times; the raven three
times beats the swift-footed stag, and the Phoenix, the bird that is restored,
multiplies that nine times.

We, the Hamadryad nymphs, whose life is longest, outstrip the Phoenix ten
times with our perpetual age. This limit holds the long-lived fates of living
things. The rest the god who judges secret time knows: 1 what periods Stilbon
rolls, what ages Phaenon, what circuits Pyrois has, what Jupiter with kindly
fire, with what return fostering Venus hurries, what labors await Phoebe, how
great those that await Titan, until, when the so-called Great Year is
consumed, the wandering stars come again into their ancient course, as they
stood from the beginning of the ordered world.

YOU who wonder that the huge bodies of the world remain, girded by the high
circuit of the sky, and that no decay comes between so great a mass, hear what
you may wonder at more: the thinnest beginnings of such great things, which
our sight cannot take in. They stand connected in a series of small atoms;
but in the small things there is a solid segment that none can cut. From
this the vigor and the powers remain, and the eternal mobility of things,
never to be overcome by any age. We may compare human things with divine. So
it is

with the solid as, the full Roman pound of twice six equal parts, and yet in
the smallest parts one force remains. For if anything is lessened in number,
the sum totters and the torn parts collapse when the body is shaken. As, if
someone pulls out the middle stone from an arch, on which all the rest lean,
the sloping others will follow at once and drag a common fall from the crown:
the pound is no different. If a single ounce is missing, the whole will not be
an as, and the hollow name left is already a deunx, eleven twelfths. Nor does
the dextans, ten ounces, keep its name if the two-ounce sextans is taken away;
and the dodrans, nine ounces, whose parent is the three-ounce quadrans, will
lack that source; and the torn four-ounce triens forbids the eight-ounce bes
to stand. Now you will have no five-ounce quincunx, if you pluck away a
scruple. 1 And to whom will a half still be a half, once the parts of the as
are perishing? And whose seventh part of the pound is the septunx? The pound,
then, is the whole, if it totters in no part. Of weight and number, of manners
and works and of waters, there is a balance: nor is there a measure you may
not mark by this name. The earth, which hangs in the middle of the air, is a
balance, 2 and its own balance holds the paths of sun and moon.

The Balance marks equal hours of day and of sleep, 1 the Balance holds the
Caledonian tides without a shore: you too, remain for me a sure balance of my
manners.

ALL that we measure in the shifting business of things, the lordship of the
stars drives. Only the earthly limbs belong to man; fortune and spirit come
from the upper airs, and a sevenfold choir must govern them. Over that choir
the golden Sun presides, allotted the rule of the bright aether. Nor does he
only govern the times of our life while we draw out the short span of an
anxious age. He is believed, by his gaze, to shape the hidden sowings and the
times of life that must be spent in the mother's womb, and to set the laws of
a life not yet come forth. For when conception has settled in the generative
field, the Sun without doubt stands in some sign. When he has taken his
station in a neighboring star, he pours no light across into the births that
lie next to him. But when, after a month's time, with reins turned, he already
climbs the third signs in his purple chariot, an oblique line sends down a
thin light, breathing slight motions through inert weights. In the fourth seat
the first Sun's kindness grows strong,

and, poured in, persuades the tender young to grow together. By a bright trine
aspect the vital look flashes, 1 shining clear and gentle; at the hinge of the
fifth sign it strikes the astonished mother when the infant is quickened. In
the sixth place there is no force, because no line of an equal side is marked
for Phoebus to watch. 2 But when, in the mid region of the sign-bearing host,
the seventh boundary has received the camp glowing with flames, set full in
front, he already sees in full light that the swelling birth is ready, and,
not stretching a sparing flash, he fosters it with the whole fire of his
crown. Hence it is that Lucina comes before the lawful labors and feels the
heavy throes steal on, a hurried childbed before the expected vow; because
unless, when he has poured light from the seventh star, he has driven the
resisting bars of a slow birth, the later month could not, the earlier could.
Or is it because the sixth, rival of the eighth star's useless aspect, does
not know how to form matching figures of the sides? But, leaning on the ninth
sign, he eases the lingering vows of mothers, the following trine joining its
forces. But if difficult Ilithyia draws the labor out again, he will loose the
bonds of doubtful delays by a square.

THE names the year gives to the seven days as they come, the same number of
wandering planets made, whom the world, rolling with unwearied turning, bids
wander in the oblique station of the signs. The rayed Sun has the first day
and the last. The Moon succeeds next to her brother's crown. Third, Mars
follows Titan's lights. Mercury claims for himself the stars of the fourth
day. Jove's golden stars light the fifth zone. Fostering Venus follows sixth
her health-bringing parent. Passing over all, the seventh light is Saturn's.
The revolving orbit restores the Sun on the eighth.

YOU, Janus, begin the Roman Kalends first. Numa established the Februa in the
neighboring month. March brought forth the beginnings of the ancient year.
Fostering Venus claims fruitful April. May is named from the greater fathers.
June is next by the title of age. Julius increases Quintilis with a Caesarean
name. August follows the Caesarean name. September fattens your autumn,
Pomona. October enriches the fields with wheat's increase. You hurl the stars
into the sea, untimely November. You, festive December, keep the genial
winter.

NEW Janus, you who give your names to the first month, two-faced Janus, you
look at two times at once. After the worship of the gods above, in the
neighboring month Numa gives the Februa as offerings to kindred shades. March,
president of the Roman race and of the year, you used to give the first times
to the Latin consuls. Venus, mother of the sons of Aeneas, you give your name
to neighboring April: for Aphrodite is companion to Mars. Whether the goddess
Maia or greater age made you May, I am in doubt; but either author is good for
the month. June follows this, to be celebrated with double honor, whether Juno
or Juventa gave the name. Then Julius, shining with Dione's star, holds the
sure times of midsummer. August follows a kindred name from Caesar, next in
the order of the year as in that of the family. After these they are joined
and mark the number in order: September, wetting the presses with Bacchus's
gift, and October, who through the times of sowing, glad with increase,
fosters the greedy farmer's hope, and November, who, plunging the solemn signs
in the salt sea, hurls them down, soon to return to their own sky. December,
closing the number, ends the genial feasts, so that from the winter solstice a
new year may soon go forth.

LUCINA restores twice six Kalends in the year, and Jupiter lets as many
mid-Ides run, and the eight following days make the day of the Nones below.
These are only three names for the Roman month; the rest of the days take
their surnames from numbers.

JUNE and April and November with September fill thirty lights through each
monthly span. Add one more piled on thrice ten days through seven months: the
Kalends of Janus and of March, and those May keeps, those July and August, and
those October and December placed at the end. One only will be of twenty-eight
days, the one Numa wished to follow Janus set in front. So the year counts for
itself three hundred, plus ten times six, plus a quarter, plus five days.

BUT now the fourth light opens the Nones, now the sixth brings them back. The
sixth brings them back in the return of May, October, and March, and of July,
who brings in his times at the solstice. The rest of the months are furnished
with Nones on the fourth; but all the Ides come round on the eighth light.

AFTER THE IDES, which each month has as its own in order, the Kalends come
back diverse, with varying number, while, as the year turns, 1 they are called
again and again, so that at last they may come forth at the rising of the
wished-for time. For three times six days and one it stretches the genial
feasts, that the new winter solstice may summon lingering Janus. By this
number returns the month of Numa and September, Bacchus's nursling, bringing
back the beginning of autumn. July and May and December placed at the end and
October are called back one day later. Then four more return one day less,
whom I will add by number: Sextilis, June, and April, after whom November is
the last-but-one turning-post. By three times five days and one, Mars of Juno,
you are summoned to return and bring back the first beginnings. By this number
the turning year will be restored to the full.

THE Sun completes ninety-four days and a half while he passes from tropic star
to tropic star, going from Phrixus's Ram into eight-footed Cancer: in this
space is the course and the turning-post of the summer day.

And a half-day and two days and nine times ten from Cancer into the Claws, the
times of equalized night and day, the golden Sun finishes in a second course,
mixing at once the borders of autumn and of summer. From there, running
through the autumn months in order, he goes on to the tropic sign of icy
Capricorn, adding eight days to ten times eight besides and a quarter of a
day, which at once in the fifth year at the end of Numa's month takes the name
of intercalary. From there, hastening to the horns of Agenor's Bull, the
golden Sun climbs the tropic star of the Wool-bearer, holding ninety days with
a decreed limit. This will be your circuit always in the turning years: three
hundred and six times ten and five days.

THESE four single lines set below will tell you the eternal months and the
four seasons of the year. March, April, May are the times of spring. July,
August, and June as well are summer. September, October, and all November make
autumn. January, February, and December belong to midwinter.

ACHAIA celebrated four ancient games. Two are of the heaven-dwellers and two
are feasts of men, the rites of Jove and of Phoebus, of Palaemon and of
Archemorus, whose wreaths are pine, apple, olive, parsley.

THE first Olympia are celebrated for great Jove at Pisa. Parnassus consecrated
the Pythia to Clarian Phoebus. High Corinth, between two seas, dedicates the
Isthmia to Portunus. Thebes keeps the Nemean funeral rites of Archemorus.

JUPITER first consecrated the feast of the Olympic crown at the longest
stadium of the Argive circus. Next Alcides consecrated the Nemean honor. These
too are five-year rites to be marked in time. The Isthmia were given to
Neptune and the Pythia to Phoebus, with a twofold worship, for gods and for
buried men. 1

Elis dedicates a sad honor to Pelops, son of Tantalus. Thebes keeps the

for dead Palaemon. Delphi set up the Pythia to appease the dragon.

NOW I will speak of the Apolline games through Tiber's mouths and the secret
rites of the Megalesian Mother and Vulcan's days, the beginnings of first
autumn, and I will set out the Quinquatrus of the goddess Pallas and the
mid-Ides in the return of May and of August, which Mercury and which Diana
dedicate to themselves; what rites matrons keep for their husbands' glory when
the first days of Mars have come back. I will recall the famous feast on the
Caprotine Nones, when the stola, taken from matrons, will cover the
slave-women. Those four also, in distinct parts of the year, the solstices and
the lights equal by night and day; nor is it right to keep silent the joyful
Roman day of the Flight of the King, when the tyrants were driven from the
city. Do you want me to speak first of the rite of Ops or the Saturnalia and
the slaves' feast, when the masters serve? and the feasts that never return on
fixed days, when through the neighborhoods each keeps his own crossroads?

or the double worship which they call the Neptunalia and that which they name
from Consus and from counsels? these feasts for ships, or those celebrated
with four-horse teams that join Roman leaders and neighboring ones? Shall I
add the worship and the foreign rites of the gods, Hercules's birthday or that
of Isis's boat, and also the glad Floralia of the wanton theater, which those
who deny they wished it still want to watch? Now even the old Equirria games
are celebrated: these are the first names the Roman circus bears, and Rome
keeps the Dionysiac games under a Latin surname, which Liber dedicates as
vows to himself. The plebeian aediles also and the curule aediles keep the
rites called by the name of the Sigillaria, and it is known that gladiators
fought funeral battles in the forum: now the sand claims its own who already
at the last end of December appease the sickle-bearing Heaven-born with blood.

THE first labor endured was the Cleonaean lion. The next crushed the
Lernaean hydra with sword and torch. Soon a third force struck down the
Erymanthian boar. In the fourth he bore the golden horns of the bronze-footed
stag. He drove off the Stymphalian birds at the fifth hazard. In the sixth he
stripped the Thracian Amazon of her belt. The seventh labor was spent on
Augeas's stables. The eighth glory is counted when the bull was driven out.
The ninth victory was in the four-horse team of Diomedes. Spain gives the
tenth palm when Geryon is destroyed. In the eleventh triumph the apples of the
Hesperides were plucked. Cerberus is the last turning-post of the final labor.

MERCURY always approves of nails kept sharp for theft and does not let the
edge be lessened at the joints.

A beard is Jove's grace, hair is Venus's: so they cannot wish removed the
thing by which each is pleased. Mars, you have loved the beardless, and you,
Moon, the bald: they do not then forbid the head and cheeks to be groomed. The
Sun and Saturn raise no objection about nails: so take away this little single
line if it has not pleased the gods.
