* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Chabrias the Athenian. This man too was held among the highest leaders and did
many things worthy of memory. But from these there shines most his invention in
the battle which he did at Thebes, when he had come as aid to the Boeotians. For
in it he frustrated Agesilaus, the highest leader confident of victory, the hired
bands already having been put to flight by him, in that he forbade the remaining
phalanx to yield from the place and taught them, with knee pressed to the
shield, the spear thrown forward, to receive the onset of the enemy. Agesilaus,
looking at this new thing, did not dare to go forward and recalled his own
already running in with a trumpet. This was so celebrated by fame through all
Greece that Chabrias wished a statue of himself to be made in that stance, which
was publicly set for him by the Athenians in the forum. From which it was done
that afterward athletes and the other craftsmen used those stances in setting
statues in which they had obtained victory.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
And Chabrias administered many wars in Europe, when he was a leader of the
Athenians; in Egypt he waged of his own will: for having set out to help
Nectenebis he established a kingdom for him. He did the same at Cyprus, but
given publicly by the Athenians as a helper to Euagoras, nor did he depart from
there before he conquered the whole island by war: from which thing the
Athenians obtained great glory. Meanwhile a war was kindled between Egyptians
and Persians. The Athenians had an alliance with Artaxerxes, the Lacedaemonians
with the Egyptians, from whom Agesilaus, their king, was making great preys.
Chabrias looking at that, since he yielded to Agesilaus in no thing, having set
out of his own will to help them was set over the Egyptian fleet, Agesilaus over
the infantry forces.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
Then the prefects of the Persian king sent ambassadors to Athens to complain
that Chabrias was waging war against the king with the Egyptians. The Athenians
set a certain day for Chabrias, before which unless he had returned home, they
announced they would condemn him on a capital charge. By this message he
returned to Athens, nor did he delay there longer than was necessary. For he was
not willingly before the eyes of his own citizens, because he both lived
sumptuously and indulged himself more freely than that he could escape the envy
of the crowd. For this is a common vice of great and free cities, that envy is a
companion of glory and they willingly detract from those whom they see standing
out higher, nor do the poor look with an even mind at alien opulence. Therefore
Chabrias, as long as it was permitted him, was away the most. Nor indeed was he
alone willingly away from Athens, but almost all the first men did the same,
because they thought they would be as far from envy as they had withdrawn from
the sight of their own. Therefore Conon lived most at Cyprus, Iphicrates in
Thrace, Timotheus at Lesbos, Chares at Sigeum, Chares indeed unlike these both
in deeds and in manners, but yet at Athens both honored and powerful.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
And Chabrias perished in the Social War in such a way. The Athenians were
attacking Chios. Chabrias was in the fleet as a private man, but he went before
all who were in magistracy by authority, and the soldiers looked up to him more
than those who were set over. Which thing hastened death for him. For while he
is eager to enter the harbor first and orders the helmsman to direct the ship
there, he was himself a destruction to himself: for when he had penetrated
there, the rest did not follow. By which deed, surrounded by a running-together
of enemies, when he was fighting most bravely, the ship, struck by a beak, began
to sink. From here although he could have fled, if he had thrown himself into
the sea, because an Athenian fleet was near which would receive those swimming,
he preferred to perish than, arms having been thrown away, to leave the ship in
which he had been carried. The rest did not wish to do that, who arrived by
swimming into safety. But he, thinking an honorable death to stand before a
shameful life, fighting at close quarters was killed by the weapons of the
enemy.
