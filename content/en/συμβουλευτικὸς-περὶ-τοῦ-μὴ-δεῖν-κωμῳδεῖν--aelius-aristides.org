It is no small advantage, men of Smyrna, for one who means to persuade, that
his speeches should be pleasant to the hearers. But as for those who wish to
admonish, the many not only do not readily take their advice; they do not even
endure

* 505
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

them at all. Still, whatever matters of themselves invite us to take them
seriously need no counselor; nature leads us everywhere toward our own
interest. But those things which we must pursue or avoid only after speech has
taught us are left to the counselor. So if someone refuses to listen, he
abolishes counseling, so far as his own share is concerned. Further, some
others might well behave like rustics. But for you, who seem so far ahead in
intelligence and education, not even to be well disposed toward hearing
speeches, and then, if someone advises what is best, not to follow it with
gratitude: that, as I judge it, is among the most absurd things. If I were
about to propose some labors or troubles, I would perhaps first need a
contest, and then I would try to teach that what will benefit the whole is
preferable to the entertainment of the moment. As it is, I am so far from
proposing anything unpleasant that I have come to counsel these very things:
that we neither say nor hear anything unpleasant. So far am I from doing, or
even counseling, any of the unpleasant things. I shall begin from the point
from which you too would follow best, and from my own custom. I say, then,
that we must keep the festival for Dionysus, and, by Zeus, for Aphrodite too
and for all the other gods, pouring libations, sacrificing, singing paeans,
wearing garlands, and omitting nothing that belongs to piety. But one thing
attached to these rites, which reasonably pleases the many and is of all
things most painful to the decent, this we must put out of the way: I mean the
slanders and these daytime revels, and, by Zeus, those at the all-night
festivals as well; and there should be neither poets of these things nor
competitors, nor should we play at what is not better. For the case stands
thus: to hear abuse unwillingly is not pleasant, but to endure it willingly is
a practice of malice. And this is among the first steps toward wickedness

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

and one could accuse neither a private man nor a city of anything greater than
that he delights in evils. Envy seems the last of human faults and admits no
excuse. Anyone weaker than this desire must share in it and foster it in
himself. Here is a sign: no one, at any rate, is pleased when those whom he
loves, and to whom he counsels some good, are spoken of badly.

Now of assault and harm and such things one can say that someone did them
unwillingly. But malice differs so far that, first, it is named from vice, as
admitting no pardon, and next it is not the name of any action, but of a
nature that would counsel nothing useful to anyone. And just as malice itself
has the greatest share of vice, beginning from the name, so abuse in turn has
the greatest share of malice, beginning from the likeness in the name. Thus
both belong to bad and base men. Always, I think, we should choose the best
things both to think and to say. But in the holy months and at the festivals,
is it not reasonable to be eager for all fair speech, for every most urbane
disposition, and for all to be as closely bound to one another as possible,
since the gods award this friendship and concord? We should think of the
festivals, almost, as tokens of friendship. No sacrifice is so splendid, and
no libations so pleasing, that the gods would not more gladly have us present
the best disposition we can. And indeed, what greater token of reverence for
the gods would there be than neither to say nor to hear anything discordant in
their presence? Or would we hesitate to say many things when a respected
friend is present, even if we might justly abuse someone, yet, putting forward
the hearing of the gods, shall some of us readily speak, and others hear,
things which we ourselves would not even say belong among the noble, but which
we mock as shameful? And that you may

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

know that I say nothing new, but that these things have long been agreed both
in the laws and in the common customs, consider the first utterances of the
heralds bidding fair speech at the festivals, and consider the priests and
those who begin the rites for us when we sacrifice; and further how we
ourselves are most eager for this in our prayers. And yet what sense is there
in holding fair speech the finest of all things and most fitting for sacred
rites, yet granting license to whoever wishes to slander? When we sacrifice we
are so orderly; yet on the pretext of the gods for whom we make the sacrifices
we hear and say the most shameful things. The same people are displeased at
abuse, and at the same time they make the gods patrons of the ill we speak of
one another. And indeed, if we think abuse friendly to the gods, we judge the
opposite for ourselves, since we guard against this very thing whenever we
approach them. But if we think it hostile and still delight in it, how are we
piously disposed? Or how do we honor the gods through the very things it was
reasonable to flee for their sake?

It seems marvelous to me that we think the voices of birds should be as
auspicious as possible for us, even in the wilderness, or wherever we happen
to be, yet we will not even in the theaters keep our own voices from being
unpleasant. When we approach the altars of Kledon we would wish to hear the
fairest speech possible, so strong a thing do we hold this goddess to be. Yet
we shall assign to the festivals the very things we ought by no means to
endure. We bid the children to speak fairly, teaching them in advance both in
the schools and at home that what is shameful to do is not even noble to say.
Then we gather children and women and every age together, set prizes for
abuse, and arrange profit for those who have practiced it best. And things
which, if we have done or suffered them, it is not lawful to go inside

:END:

the lustral basins, these we sing in the midst of the sacred rites. We hold it
impious to sacrifice what one must not, yet we count as part of piety honoring
the gods with what one must not. We honor propriety in other things, but of
the shameful we readily look upon every posture and readily accept every
utterance. If one of the chorus members sings out of tune, we throw him out;
but if the whole chorus sings so unmusically, we make it a profit. If others
speak of us so badly, we are angry; but if we do this to ourselves, we hold it
a true festival. So strangely are we disposed both toward the gods and toward
ourselves, far contrary to what is fitting. And yet some dare to say that it
is a good thing that people be permitted to speak ill in the theater, for
those who have lived badly are exposed, and the others, from fear of being
made a comedy, keep themselves temperate. I would think drunkenness worth a
great deal if it could educate people. But it is a hard thing for drunks
themselves to make others temperate, and, before they have even stopped, to
sing to the rest how they will live beautifully. For who of you does not know
that, first, teaching such things is not for many, no more than making laws or
speaking opinions in the assembly? Or shall we believe that the voyage to
Corinth is not for every man, yet that every man will know the course of a
whole life, what kind it should be and through what pursuits it must be made,
and that every man will sit at these tillers, carrying the young this way and
that as he thinks fit? We hold selections of the athletes, so that whoever
among them is base may go away having shamed himself and paid a penalty from
his body. Shall we then so cheaply select the leaders and teachers of the most
serious things from every workshop and from all the most serious occupations?
We ourselves shall then seem to need teachers. We do not use all as
doorkeepers, but only the most trustworthy, so that no shame may befall the
house. Yet children and women and all the city's

:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

complements, and, in a word, our dignity: shall we give these over to whoever
wishes to handle them? And those we have condemned when sober, shall we trust
when they are drunk?

And indeed the occasion is not of those who educate, but of those who play: if
you like, that of the Dionysia; if you like, that of the sacred Sama and the
all-night festivals. So how is it reasonable, in the very time when we release
the children from their real teachers, to set over them men who have no
business there and men who adopt themselves into the role, and not even to
learn from the place itself that the pretension is irrational? A teacher, at
least, must not walk into the theaters and admonish there. Those places are
dedicated to pleasures and entertainments. There are places, I suppose, named
after the title, where one must philosophize; and not even in these, I think,
in this manner, mocking and speaking ill without restraint, but educating as
free men ought to be educated, and teaching them in advance, among the rest,
to guard against unseemliness. And indeed, if in making comedy of the wanton
they kept off the others, there might perhaps be some argument. As it is, the
matter begins from a beginning fair in appearance, but it ends in no way well.
Many who have no business with it are spoken of badly, and there are some who,
though they escape no one's notice, still slip past the abuse in the open.
Why? They do not use the ways of teachers, or of chastisers, or of those who
wish to make people better; they would have made themselves better first. They
blame from enmity and from favor to others: one man because he asked for money
and did not get it, another because he fell in love and did not persuade. And
again they are silent for the opposite reasons. So they do not make those who
live shamefully conspicuous. Consider it this way: whom is it most likely
would buy off the abuse? Would you not say those who are conscious of such
things in themselves? And who should care least of these? Would it not be
those who have confidence in themselves and in how they have lived? It is
clear, I suppose. Therefore the opposite of the promise comes about: the
wanton and shameful are unharmed in the speech, and those are made comedy of
whom one least ought. Come then,

let us suppose that both, mixed together, are spoken of badly (for you at
least would not say that no one who is decent in his ways will ever be spoken
of badly). Then those who have neglected reputation and have been utterly
corrupted by shameful desires are not harmed in the least, I suppose; they
would even count it a gain, exulting that they become conspicuous to all. But
those who contend undeservedly: how do they not greatly miss the prizes that
ought to belong to temperance? So it is not in order that the young may be
better for you that one must make comedy. If for no other reason, one must
stop this making of comedy, so that the young may practice the better things
without fear. The base, I suppose, one can punish in other ways as well. But
when we so outrage the decent, what argument shall we give? Those to whom it
is not even fitting to obtain a hearing of a lawsuit: how is it fitting that
whoever wishes should be given leave to dishonor them without accountability?
If, then, someone among you has also supposed this, that even among the decent
it will seem preferable to many to spend money so as not to be spoken of
badly, and so much the more the more shamefaced they are in their ways, how is
it not clear from every side that comedy is unpleasant and better forbidden by
you once for all? If, at any rate, the good will suffer one of two things:
either they will be spoken of badly because they did not pay, or they will be
fined so as not to be spoken of badly, buying the slanders with money. But if
they care nothing when they hear themselves spoken of badly, what is it that
they will suffer? And in general the city as a whole cannot help sharing the
reputation of private men. Whatever sort those who are made comedy of may be,
the whole have often seemed to be of that sort. Consider also, referring back
to your fathers the Athenians. Almost all the other writings, so to speak,
adorn them; comedy alone tore them down and still furnishes a hold even now to
those who wish to slander. For they seemed to be exposed by themselves. And
how great, O earth and gods, is the difference between the present counterfeit
and how much admonition and education there was at least in the so-called
parabases, I forbear to say. But at any rate the proverb's point was
available, if it should befall someone: even to suffer from a worthy wood;

but of this, such wickedness of diction, such wantonness of songs, and further
shame of postures: no comedy would reach the evils that belong to these. What
manner of pleasure is it? And will you endure these as if they were noble? And
what is more shameful than these? Or as if they were advantageous to hear or
to watch? And indeed they are tokens of corruption. For when a man or a woman
has fallen into the habit of being spoken of badly and of bearing the worst of
reproaches, the mind readily slackens and everyone is taught to be base, even
if he was not so before, especially in these things of which nothing has to do
with Dionysus. But to me at least the proverb also seems to apply not least to
such follies. So be it. But as regards reputation they are marvelous, when in
the baths, in the alleys, in the marketplace, and in the houses, women,
children, everyone in turn sings such things over. And will it be permitted to
make comedy only of the citizens? Then it is terrible if you will know how to
spare the foreigners, but will not dare to spare yourselves. You will be so
unlike the Lacedaemonians that they made all their own affairs secret, while
you will give even what in no way belongs, to be said against you, to those
who wish it. But, by Zeus, shall we lay hold of the foreigners as well? Then
we shall have much kindness from all and much intercourse; I suppose the
fathers will readily bring their sons here, and elder brothers the younger,
for education, among people whose songs are of this sort. Will you not stop
posting yourselves as on a stele? Yes, by Zeus, but the jest is witty. Then so
much the more it is not for many, if at least the witty ought not to seem to
be many. And I would gladly ask those who delight in abuse whether they are
playing or in earnest. For if they are playing, why do they pretend to
admonish? But if they are in earnest, it is again good to inquire of them
whether they abuse these things as true or as false. For if true, why on earth
do they not use the laws? But where one must speak they are silent, and where
one must be silent they speak. And indeed if they are lying, it was reasonable
to be content not to suffer badly, not to be indignant if they will not be
permitted to speak

ill. I think, then, that in counseling these things I would do rightly for
