* On that saying of Isaiah, I the Lord God made light
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

On the prophetic saying which says: "I am the Lord God; I made light and
darkness, making peace, and creating evils."

=56.141=

1. Few are the things that have been said, but sweet is the spring of honey,
of honey that has no satiety. For this sensible honey ends its pleasure on the
tongue and passes into corruption; but the honey of teaching is stored in the
conscience, furnishing a continual gladness, and leading us by the hand toward
incorruption. And the one is composed from plants, but the other is woven from
the divine Scriptures. This today has filled you, the one who has spoken well,
and he has snatched the prize of obedience, and has shown the strength of love
and the nobility of faith.

Come then, let us also set before you again the accustomed table with much
eagerness; for we rejoice exceedingly that, though a splendid horse-race is
taking place, so great a multitude has arrived here, having despised the
pastime there. Therefore we too set up the mixing-bowl with much abundance, a
mixing-bowl that does not produce drunkenness, but fashions temperance. For
such is the wine of the Scriptures, such are the dishes of this table: they do
not fatten =56.142= flesh. And we say these things not dishonoring the nature
of the flesh, but preferring the nobility of the soul; nor casting out the
use, but chastising the excess. For even if we philosophize, we must
philosophize in such a way as not to give a handle to the mouths of the
heretics. For this body is less than the soul, yet not contrary to the soul;
but the soul is simple, and is served by the desires of the body. The master
craftsman God did not compose this universe from one and two and three
substances, but introduced varied and different natures, displaying the
abundance of his own wisdom in the difference of the things that come to be.
For he did not make heaven only, but also earth; not earth only, but also the
sun; not the sun only, but also the moon; not the moon only, but also stars;
not stars only, but also air; not air only, but also clouds; not clouds only,
but also aether; not aether only, but also lakes, and springs, and rivers, and
mountains, and glens, and hills, and meadows, =56.143= and gardens, and seeds,
and plants, varied kinds of herbs, and different shapes, and different
operations, and different natures, which one might see going about everywhere
in the world; and running with his reason over the body of the inhabited
earth, he will say with the Prophet: How magnified are your works, O Lord. In
wisdom you have made them all.

So if you wish to see a theater, leaving that satanic one, come to this
spiritual one; if you wish to hear lyres, leaving that melody, and straining
the vigor of your mind, come to this one that wakes your thought, nerving your
understanding. See different notes and strings interchanged, sending up from
every side one wholly harmonious [melody. For as] some [sound of the spirit]
composed from different notes has one melody, the doxology toward the Maker;
and the strings sound by themselves, and they sound also with one another. And
that you may learn how they sound by themselves, strike with your reason the
string of heaven, and you will hear it sounding great things, and sending up
glory to God.

Which also the Prophet, perceiving, said: The heavens declare the glory of
God, and the firmament proclaims the work of his hands. From that string pass
over to the string of day and of night, and you will see these too


uttering notes sweeter than every lyre and cithara; and most of all, when
there is someone who knows how to strike these strings. And how do they sound?
he says. Heaven does not move a mouth, nor a tongue, nor a palate, nor teeth,
nor lips; how then does the voice go forth? And how does the day speak? For
these too are not vocal organs, but day and night are a course of sun and
moon, a passage of time. Therefore, lest any of the coarser sort, hearing
these things, be disturbed and troubled, hear how the Prophet contends for
what has been said. For having said that The heavens declare the glory of God,
and that Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night proclaims knowledge,
he did not stop at this, but added: There are no speeches nor words, whose
voices are not heard.

And what he means is of this sort: not only do day and night and heaven have a
voice, but even such a voice as is more distinct than the human, and clearer,
and more vigorous. How and in what manner? Hear the saying itself. There are
no speeches nor words, whose voices are not heard. What then is this? An
encomium of the voice, praise of the sound. For my voice is known to one of my
own tongue, but no longer to one of another language. I mean something of this
sort: if I converse in the Greek tongue, then if someone knows the speech,
that man will hear me; but the Scythian, and the Thracian, and the Moor, and
the Indian no longer; for the difference of language does not allow my
discourse to become distinct to him.

2. Again I, when the Scythian and the Thracian converse, will not be able to
hear, nor will another hear another's language; but in the case of heaven, and
night, and day, this is not so, but such is their voice that it is heard by
every tongue, every speech, every nation, and is evident and clear. Therefore
having said that The heavens declare the glory of God, and, Day unto day
utters speech, he added: There are no speeches nor words, whose voices are not
heard. And what he means is of this sort: day and night and heaven and all
created things have such speech and such voice that their voice is heard by
all speeches, that is, by all tongues, by all nations. For there is no speech,
he says, that is, there is no nation, there is no language, where the voice of
heaven is not heard; but the Scythian, and the Thracian, and the Moor, and the
Indian, and the Sauromatian, and every speech, and every tongue, and every
nation will be able to hearken to this voice. How and in what manner? Hear
this next, that you may learn how heaven, keeping silence, speaks.

For when you see its beauty, its greatness, its position, its permanence, its
brightness, and gathering all these things together with yourself you glorify
the Creator, you praise the Maker, heaven has sent forth a voice, sending up
glory to God through the tongue. And this is what was said, that The heavens
declare the glory of God. How and in what manner? By sending the spectator,
through the beauty of their own brightness, into wonder at the Creator. For
when, having seen such a work, you say, Glory to you, O God, how great a body
you have wrought, and have set it in the midst; the heavens have offered up
this glory, using your tongue, and sending the wonder through the sight. Thus
keeping silence they offer up glory to God, and all hear this voice. For since
these things cannot be learned by hearing, but by sight and contemplation, and
sight is one for all, even if the tongue is different, both Barbarian, and
Scythians, and Thracians, and Moors, and Indians hear this voice, that is,
seeing the wonder, being struck by the beauty, the brightness, the greatness,
and all the other things that belong to heaven, those who think rightly offer
up glory to the Creator. The same can be said also concerning day and night.
Just as this, by its beauty, its position, its


greatness, its brightness, the permanence of its time, its use, its operation,
and all the other things, sending the spectator into wonder, makes him offer
up glory to the Creator; so also night and day.

For when you see the good order of these seasons, and how the day, having
completed its own measure, does not strive to drive night out of her own
bounds, nor displays any greed, nor, because it is brighter than she, forces
its way to occupy the whole time, but withdraws; and night again does the
same, having completed her course, yields to the day, and this has happened
for so many years, and there is no confusion, nor disturbance, and neither has
this one thrust that one out, nor has that one overreached this one, although
the one is brighter, the other more gloomy; having again wondered at the good
order, will you not send up glory to God? And just as two sisters, being well
disposed toward one another, having divided a paternal inheritance with
measures and scales, neither overreaches the =56.145= other even in the least;
so also night and day, having divided all time, keep so great an equality,
overreaching one another not even in the least, as you know, having learned it
through experience itself. Let the greedy hear, and those who thrust their
brothers out of the inheritance; let them reverence the good order of the
seasons, the equal speech of night and day, and let them put down their own
disease. Thus then Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night proclaims
knowledge, not sending forth a voice, but through the good order, and the
rhythm, and the equality, and the unhindered measures, proclaiming the Creator
more clearly than a trumpet, not in one corner of the inhabited world, but
over as much earth as the sun looks upon.

For these voices are carried everywhere in the inhabited world, since heaven
is everywhere, and day is everywhere, and night is everywhere, and they
stretch out their teaching both on land and on sea. Therefore the Prophet did
not say simply that the heavens speak the glory of God, but, They declare,
that is, they also teach others, and they have the race of men as disciples,
and they put forward in the midst a very great school, instead of books and
letters offering the beauty of their own nature for both the unlearned and the
wise and all to go over, as in a book the teaching laid up in them concerning
the wisdom and power of God. Thus also men, not speaking, but even keeping
silence, glorify God through others; wherefore Christ also said: Let your
light shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your
Father who is in heaven.

Just as, then, someone seeing a life that shines, even while the one living it
keeps silence, offers up glory to God; so also, seeing the beauty of heaven,
he glorifies the Maker. Therefore he said: The heavens declare the glory of
God, through the spectators; Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night
proclaims knowledge. Knowledge: of what sort? That concerning the Maker. For
just as the day leads man out to work, so the night, succeeding, rests him
from countless toils, and lets him go from cares, and putting the weary pupil
to sleep, and lowering the eyelids, prepares him to receive the ray again with
strength at its height. So that the usefulness of this too is not small, but
even very great. For if it did not rest the man, receiving him from countless
toils, there would have been no profit from the day that led him into work;
for nature refusing the continual labor, the living creature would have been
destroyed and perished, and nothing more would have come to him from the ray.
She, then, who makes the day useful to man, she most of all is the one who,
through her own ministry, leads the one who enjoys her choir toward the
knowledge of God. For when someone considers how great is the usefulness of
the day, and how great that of the night, and


how this succeeds that, and alternately, as in some chorus, succeeding one
another they preserve our race in turn, even if he be more sluggish than all
men, he will be able, stirring up his own reason, to know the wisdom of the
master craftsman God, which he displayed through day and night, assigning the
former to us for work, and the latter for rest from labors.

3. But we have gone out into saying all these things from the preludes; and
since some of the things read to us today have perhaps disturbed =56.146= many
of those who do not attend, and of those who have the Scriptures without
experience, come then, let us go on to these things with much rush. For there
was read also the woman with the issue of blood, who by the touch stopped the
fountains of her blood, and by the power of faith plundered so great a
treasure; for what happened was a theft; but a theft that found favor, and she
who plundered was praised after the exposure. For Jesus himself, the one
plundered, proclaims the little woman. There were read also the marks of Paul,
the wounds, the prisons, the leadings away, the shipwrecks, the bonds, the
chains, the various and continual plots, the daily deaths, the hunger, the
thirst, the nakedness, the daily conspiracy. But what am I to do? I must flee,
using much vehemence, lest Paul again, having caught me, lead me away from the
matter at hand.

For you know that often, when I was walking and turning elsewhere, he met me
and caught me in the midst of the discourse, and so took me that he persuaded
me to finish the discourse in him. Therefore, lest we suffer this today also,
throwing a bridle, as it were, upon the discourse as it is being carried there
with much vehemence, let us thus draw it, and lead it to the prophetic saying.
What then is the saying? I am the Lord, he says, who made light and darkness,
who makes peace, and creates evils. You see that it was not at random nor in
vain that we made our course to this passage, but, running past all the other
things, we were eager to meet here. For what has been said produces much
disturbance for the one who does not attend.

Rouse yourselves then, and strain your hearing, and casting out every worldly
care, attend to what is said. For I wish now to pay you this wage of your
presence here, and having filled you with the spiritual foods, thus to send
you out, so that those also who were left behind may learn by the deed how
great a loss they endured; and they will know, if, having received what is
said with accuracy, you should also be able to carry it over to them. I am the
Lord God, who made light and darkness, who makes peace, and creates evils. I
turn it round continually, so that it may be set into your mind, and then
bring on the solution. For not this one only says this, but another prophet
also, uttering things in concert, said: If there is evil in a city, which the
Lord has not made? What then is what has been said? For one solution must be
brought upon all. What then is the solution? If we should learn the force of
these words. But attend with accuracy; for it is not at random nor in vain
that we continually charge you this, but because we are now going on to the
depth of the thoughts. Of things, some are good, some evil, and some
intermediate; of which some seem to many to be evils, but are not, but are
only called so and suspected. But that I may make what I say clearer, come,
let us also exercise the discourse upon examples. Poverty seems to many to be
an evil, but it is not; but if someone is sober and philosophizes, it is even
destructive of evils. =56.147= Wealth again seems to many to be a good, but it
is not simply a good, unless someone should be using it as he ought.

For if wealth were simply a good, those who have it ought also to be good; but
if not all who are rich are virtuous, but those who have used wealth well, it
is quite clear that wealth is not simply in itself a good, but is a certain
material of virtue


lying in the midst. But consider. There are qualities in the body, from which
those who have them are named. I mean something of this sort: white is not a
substance, but a quality, and an accident coming upon the substance; if then
this is added to someone, we call such a one white. There is sickness again,
this too being a certain quality and accident; and if it is added to someone,
we call such a one sick. If then wealth also were a virtue, the one who has
the wealth ought to be and to be called virtuous; but if the rich man is not
in every case virtuous, wealth is not a virtue, nor simply a good, but this
comes about according to the purpose of the one who uses it. Again, if poverty
were an evil, all those in poverty ought to be evil; but if many of those who
are in poverty have laid hold of the heavens, then poverty is not an evil.

4. What then, that many blaspheme because of poverty? he says. Not because of
poverty, but because of their own folly and smallness of soul they do this.
And the proof of these things is the blessed Job, who, being in extreme
poverty, and having been brought down to the very pit of poverty, not only did
not blaspheme, but continued blessing God, saying: The Lord gave, the Lord has
taken away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so also it has come to pass; may
the name of the Lord be blessed forever. But also because of wealth many, he
says, seize and overreach. But not because of wealth, but because of their own
folly; and of this again this same man is witness, who in so great wealth not
only did not seize the things of others, but even [gave] his own, and prepared
a harbor for strangers, saying thus: My house was open to every stranger who
came.

And Abraham, having so great wealth, spent all on those who passed by; and
wealth made neither this man nor that man a greedy one, just as neither did
poverty make that man a blasphemer, nor Lazarus; but both shone so, not even
abounding in necessary food, that the one received the testimony from God, who
knows clearly the secret things; and the other was carried away from here with
angels going before, and came to share the patriarch's tent, and enjoys the
goods which that one enjoys. These then are among the intermediates: poverty
and wealth, health and disease, and life and death, glory and honor, slavery
and freedom, and as many such things; for one must not go over all, so as not
to lengthen the discourse, but, furnishing you starting-points in these, go on
to the urgent things. For give, he says, occasion to a wise man, and he will
be wiser.

These then are all among the intermediates, so that those who use them can use
them both for good and for evil. For that these things, of which wealth is
one, are among the intermediates, Abraham showed, using it =56.148= as he
ought. And the rich man in the case of Lazarus also showed it, spending what
he had upon the destruction of his own head. Therefore wealth is neither
simply a good, nor an evil. If it were simply a good, but not intermediate,
the rich man in the case of Lazarus would not have been so punished; if it
were an evil, Abraham would not have found such favor, being rich. Disease is
something of this sort too. For if disease were an evil, the one who has it
ought also to be evil. Then Timothy too would have been evil according to
this, wrestling with extreme disease. For use a little wine, he says, for your
stomach's sake, and your frequent infirmities. But if he was not only not evil
from this, but even received an addition of much reward, having borne the
sickness nobly, it is quite clear that disease is not an evil. And another
prophet also continued having his eyes diseased; but he was not evil according
to this, but even prophesied, and foresaw the things to come, and the disease
became no hindrance to virtue. But neither is health simply a good, unless
someone should be using it as he ought, but for wicked work, or for mindless
idleness; for this too is not without blame. Wherefore Paul also said: If
anyone will not work,


let him not eat. But these things are among the intermediates, becoming now
this, now that, according to those who use them.

And why must one speak of health, and disease, and wealth, and poverty? For
this very thing which seems to the many to be the chief of goods, and the
crown of evils, death, I mean, and life, not even these will be simply so, but
these too are among the intermediates, each of these becoming so according to
the purpose of those who use them. I mean something of this sort: to live is
good, when someone uses it as he ought; but when for sins and lawless deeds,
it would no longer be good, but it is better for such a one to die. Again,
what seems to many to be a thing to be fled can become a cause of countless
goods, when it has the fitting cause. And the martyrs show it, who through
their end are more blessed than all. For this reason Paul did not simply
desire to live in Christ, but because this was for him fruit of work.

For what I shall choose, he says, I do not know; but I am hard pressed from
the two, having the desire to depart and be with Christ; for that is far
better; but to remain in the flesh is more necessary for you. For this reason
also the Prophet: Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his holy
ones; not death simply, but such a death. And again elsewhere, The death of
sinners is evil. Do you see that this too is among the intermediates, not
simply good, nor simply evil, but from the purpose of those who receive it?
For this reason also the most wise Solomon, listing the usefulness of these
intermediates, and philosophizing about them, and showing that this is not
simply good, and that evil, but they become good with the fitting time, even
if they seem burdensome, when they do not have the fitting time, A time to
weep, and a time to laugh, he said, a time to live, and a time to die. For
neither is rejoicing good everywhere, but there is a place where it is even
harmful; =56.149= nor is grieving good everywhere, but there is a place where
it is even deadly and destructive. And showing this very thing again Paul
said: The grief according to God works repentance unto salvation not to be
regretted; but the grief of the world works death.

5. Do you see that this too is among the intermediates? Therefore its opposite
also is among the intermediates, rejoicing, I mean. Wherefore he did not
command simply to rejoice, but to rejoice in the Lord. But the account of the
intermediates has been sufficiently distinguished by us, if at least it has
hearers who attend; and one must go on next to the things that are not
intermediates, but to the goods, which could not become evils, and to the
evils, which could not become goods.

For these things that have been said become now this, now that; for example,
wealth is at one time an evil, when it is spent upon greed, and at another
time a good, when it is expended upon almsgiving; and the other such things
according to this rule. But there are certain things which could never become
evils; and others, contrary to these, always evils, and which could never
become goods; for example, impiety, blasphemy, wantonness, cruelty,
inhumanity, gluttony, and as many such things. I do not say that a bad man
could never become good, nor that a good man could never become bad; but that
the deeds themselves could never become so. For let these remain in their own
rank, both the goods and the evils; but the man, when he chooses those, is
good, and when the contrary, bad. Therefore these are three portions: for the
goods could not become evils, such as temperance, almsgiving, and as many such
things; and the evils could never become goods, such as wantonness,
inhumanity, cruelty; and the things that become now this, now that, according
to the purpose of those who use them.

For wealth at one time is made to serve greed, and at another time almsgiving;
but according to the purpose of the one who uses it. Poverty at one time is
made to serve blasphemy, and at another time praise and philosophy. Since then
many


of the more irrational (for one must go on to the solution at last) call not
only the evils, which could never become goods, but also some of the
intermediates evils, such as poverty, captivity, slavery, which we have shown
to be not of the evils, but of the intermediates; and many, as I said before,
call these evils, though they are not evils; concerning which the prophet
speaks, concerning the things called so according to the suspicion of the
many, not concerning the things that are truly evils; concerning captivity,
concerning slavery, concerning famine, and as many such things. For that these
are not evils, but even destructive of evils, let us first bring famine into
the midst, which seems to all to be dreadful and fearful. How then it is not
an evil, learn, and be taught to philosophize. When the people of the Hebrews
had run aground upon the extreme of lawlessness, then the great Elijah, even
worthy of the heavens, wishing to take away the disease from sloth and to
correct it, brought it on, saying: As the Lord lives, before whom I have
stood, there shall be no rain, except by my mouth; and he who possessed only a
sheepskin shut up the heaven; so great was his boldness toward God. Do you see
that poverty is not an evil? The poorest of all men would not have enjoyed so
great a boldness, as, walking on the ground, to display so great a power by
one word only.

Having said =56.150= this, then, he brought on a famine, as an excellent
pedagogue, and a corrector of the evils that were happening; and just as when
a violent fever has fallen upon a body, even the very veins of the earth were
dried up, and torrents failed, and herbs, and the flanks of the earth were
thereafter barren. And the people then reaped no small fruit, being delivered
from the rush toward lawlessness, and being contracted, and becoming more
reasonable, and tractable to the prophet. For those who were running to the
idols, and slaughtering their sons to the demons, these, when so many priests
of Baal were being destroyed, were not displeased, did not grow angry, but
bore what was happening in silence and fear, having become better from the
famine.

6. Do you see that famine is not only [not] an evil, but even destructive of
evils, having corrected the diseases in the rank of a medicine? If you wish to
see captivity itself also as not being of the evils, consider what sort the
Jews were before the captivity, and what sort in the captivity, that you may
learn that neither is freedom simply a good, nor captivity an evil. For when
they were in freedom, and had their own fatherland, they did such things that
every day their prophets cried out against them, the laws being trampled, and
the idols being worshipped, and the commandments of God being transgressed;
but when they were carried away into a strange land, and spent their time in
the country of the barbarians, they were so contracted, and became better, and
guardians of the law, as one can learn this from the psalm, which it is
necessary to bring into the midst today, that you may learn the fruit of
captivity.

And what is the psalm? By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, and we
wept when we remembered Zion. Upon the willows in the midst of it we hung our
instruments. For there those who had taken us captive asked us for words of
songs, saying: Sing us from the songs of Zion. How shall we sing the song of
the Lord upon a strange land? Did you see how captivity contracted them? For
before this, even when the prophets were sounding that they should not
transgress the law, they did not endure it; but after this, even when the
barbarians were pressing, and those who were masters were compelling and
urging them to transgress, they did not obey, but said: We will not sing the
song of the Lord upon a strange land, since the law did not permit this. See
also the three children not only suffering no harm from the captivity, but
even becoming more splendid from it; and Daniel likewise. And what of Joseph?
What evil did he suffer, having become both a stranger, and a slave, and a
prisoner? Was it not through this alone that he found favor and shone? And
what did that barbarian woman,


spending her time in wealth and pride and in freedom, gain from this? Was she
not more wretched than all, since she was not willing to use these things as
she ought?

Therefore these things have been clearly shown by us, which are evils, which
are goods, and which are intermediates; and that the prophet speaks concerning
the intermediates, not the things that are truly evils, but the things that
seem =56.151= to the many to be so, captivity, slavery, leading away. And for
what reason these things have been said, it is necessary to say. God being a
lover of men, and swift toward mercy, but drawing back from punishing and
chastising, so as not to hand the Jews over to punishments, sent prophets,
frightening them through words, so as not to chastise through deeds, which is
what he also used to do in the case of the Ninevites. For there too he then
threatened to overthrow the city, not in order to overthrow it, but in order
not to overthrow it; which then also came to pass. This then he was doing then
also, and he sent prophets, threatening an inroad of barbarians, wars,
captivities, slaveries, leading away, the sojourn in a strange land. And just
as an affectionate father, having an undisciplined and slothful child, wishing
to chasten him, looks for straps, threatens cords, saying: I will bind, I will
scourge, I will destroy, and becomes fearful in the words, so as by this to
check the wickedness of the youth; so also God threatened continually, wishing
by the fear to make them better.

The devil, seeing these things, and wishing to dissolve the correction that
comes from such a threat, sent down false prophets, and when the prophets were
threatening captivity, and slavery, and famines, those men said the contrary,
peace, plenty, and enjoyment of countless goods. Wherefore the prophets also,
reproaching them, said: Peace, peace; and where is peace? And as many as are
lovers of letters know this, that all the things prevail which came about for
the prophets against the false prophets, who were dissolving the zeal of the
people. Since then, dissolving thus, they corrupted the people, God says
through the prophets: I am God, making peace, and creating evils. And what
sort of evils? These that have been said, captivity, slavery, and as many such
things; not fornication, and wantonness, and greed, nor any other of such
things. Therefore the other prophet also, having said, If there is evil in a
city, which the Lord has not made, means this evil, the famines, the diseases,
the God-sent blows. So also Christ, when he says, Sufficient to the day is the
evil thereof, means the sweats, the toil, the hardship.

7. This then the prophet says: Let not the false prophets dissolve you; for
God is able to give you peace, and to hand you over to captivity; that is, Who
makes peace, and creates evils. And that you may learn that this is true, let
us test the very words with accuracy. For having said above, I am he who made
light and darkness, then he added, Making peace, and creating evils. He has
set two contraries above, and two contraries after these, that you may learn
that he is not speaking about fornication, but about calamities. For what is
set in opposition to peace? It is quite clear that it is captivity, not
wantonness, nor fornication, nor greed. Just as, then, above he set two
contraries, so also =56.152= here; and contrary to peace is not fornication,
nor adultery, nor wantonness, nor the remaining vices, but captivity and
slavery. But what they suffer in the case of the elements, this they suffer
also in the case of the things. I mean something of this sort: just as he made
light and darkness, and to many the light seems to be pleasant, but the
darkness burdensome, and they slander the night as being something wicked; so
also in the case of the things. But neither is the night to be slandered, nor
the darkness, nor simply slavery, and captivity.

For tell me, what bad thing does the darkness have? Is it not a rest from
toils? Is it not a relaxation of cares? Is it not a taking away of grief? Is
it not an addition of strength? And if there were not darkness


and night, when would we have enjoyed the light? Would not this living
creature, man, have been destroyed and perished? And just as the darkness
seems to the foolish to be an evil, but is not, but contributes to us toward
the day, and makes those who have rested more fit for the works in it; so
neither is captivity a bad thing; concerning which the prophet was discoursing
when he said, Who makes peace, and creates evils; but more useful to those who
use it as they ought; and it makes them more temperate and more reasonable,
cutting away arrogance. For virtue is unenslaved, and nothing could prevail
over it, not slavery, not captivity, not poverty, not disease, not that which
is more tyrannical than all, death itself. And all who have endured all these
things show it, and have shone more greatly through them. For what harm did
slavery do to Joseph (for nothing hinders us from bringing the same man into
the midst again); for what did the bonds? And what the chains? And what the
slander? And what the plot? And what the sojourn in a strange land? What did
the destruction of the herds and the flocks do to Job, the violent and
untimely death of the children, the plague of the body, the fountains of
worms, the pain that could not be borne, the sitting upon the dunghill, the
plot of the wife, the reproaches of the friends, the insults of the household
slaves? And what did it do to Lazarus to lie in the gateway, and to be licked
about by the tongues of the dogs, and the continual famine, and the disdain of
the rich man, and the wounds, and the unbearable disease, and the desertion of
those who stand before one, and the disdain of those who help? And what did
those snowstorms of prisons, of leadings away, of deaths, of drownings, of the
remaining trials, which it is not even possible to number in speech, do to
Paul? Having considered all these things, let us flee vice, let us pursue
virtue; and let us pray not to enter into temptation; but if ever we fall into
it, let us not be distressed, nor be displeased; for these things are weapons
of virtue for those who use them as they ought, and we shall be able to find
favor through all things if we are sober, and to enjoy the eternal goods;
which may it be that we all attain in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the
glory unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
