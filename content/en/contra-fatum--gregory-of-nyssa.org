OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, AGAINST FATE
You remember in every way what happened to me, when recently among you the
great mountain of unbelief, to speak evangelically, was moved into faith, the
most wise Eusebius having deliberated in old age worthily of his gray hair, if
indeed one must reckon this to be a counsel of a man and not some divine
alliance of the one who administers the human for good. Then indeed, as I was
struck at the paradox of the wonder, how he who was so great formerly in
unbelief surpassed the measure of unbelief by a manifold greatness of faith,
when as our conversation was proceeding some discourse of the things inquired
concerning fate =32= fell in, you ordered, O head honorable and holy to me,
that the discussion that took place for me toward one of the philosophers in
the great city of Constantine concerning this subject be related to you in
writing through a letter. Having then obtained a little leisure, cutting the
discourse short through a few words, as far as possible, I set it out for you
in a plain and unadorned narrative, guarding as much as possible that the
discourse not fall far from epistolary measures by being stretched out into
the length of a written oration. I was bringing some words concerning our
piety to a certain man educated in the outside philosophy, as it was possible
to guess from what was said, and I was trying to persuade him to change over
from Hellenism to the assent of our doctrine. But since he was much
constructing that the choice according to judgment does not lie by authority
for those who wish, but attaching the life of men through some necessity,
without which nothing of the things that come to be in us would come to be,
and by this argument he was knocking aside my argument, as if, if it is fated
for him to become a Christian, he will become so in every way, even if we do
not wish it, but if he should be prevented by the necessity of fate, it would
not be possible to find some device that will force fate; as he said these
things I, as was likely, thought that he was fleeing to learn something about
the faith, being deeply set in =33= Hellenism, and in this way knocking aside
the sequence of our argument. But since he did not leave off, persisting in
the same things, saying that all things are yoked under the necessity of fate
and that it presides over the things that are and that all the things that are
bow to the inclination of this, both measures of life and differences of
characters and choices of lives and constructions of bodies and the
irregularities of dignities, so that the one who according to fate comes
forward to rule rules in every way, and serves for the same cause and is
likewise rich and is poor, and again is strong in body and is weakly, and that
the short-lived and the long-lived have the same cause (for both he who has
shared in life in a little and he who has stretched out his life in a long
time come to be each in each not according to their own impulse but from that
necessity), and that both the spontaneous and the forced death are allotted
not otherwise than from that, and the many-formed differences of violent death
brought on in accidents or hangings or a judicial vote or even according to a
plot, and the passions still more universal and more comprehensive than these,
earthquakes and shipwrecks and floods of waters and the disasters from fire
and as many such kinds of evils, attaching all to that cause he added also
that nowhere of the pursuits according to life is the reasoning of the one
choosing master, but that all, serving the power of fate, either philosophize
or practice rhetoric or farm or sail or live in marriage or choose the
unmarried life, and he strongly asserted that of virtue and vice there is the
same necessity; so that


according to this inviolable =34= allotment one attends to the higher life in
a possessionless and free-living life, and another grave-robs or pirates or
lives dissolutely or is made womanish in the courtesan life. And going through
all such things he thought he had shown a strong cause for not receiving our
argument, that it is not in our power to choose by authority whatever we may
wish, but that one must wait for that necessity, which, when it has furnished
the start toward such an impulse, it is necessary to add oneself to the
argument even if not willing, and without that, even if one strongly chooses,
it is not possible to come to be. But I, as he was going through these and
such things, asked him whether he thinks there is some god who has the power
over all, the one called by the name of fate, by whose will he has believed
the things according to each to be administered. But he, having condemned much
folly of mine in the question, You do not seem to me, he says, to have
understood anything of the heavenly things. For otherwise you would have known
the power of fate, from where and how the power of the things that come to be
inviolably according to a sequence was grasped. And as I was finding even this
argument foreign-sounding and asking to learn something more clearly, whether
he imagines fate to be some choosing power, self-ruled and without a master,
contemplated in some overlying authority, or has supposed something else
besides this, taking up the same argument again, He who has examined, he says,
the motion of the heavenly things, both the zodiac-bearing circle and the
twelve segments in it, standing apart from one another equally by the =35=
outline of the signs contemplated in it, and having understood the power of
each of the stars, what strength each has from nature by itself, and what
their conjunction works from the qualitative blend toward one another, the
property according to the power of each of them both mixed by the approach
toward the other and separated through the distance, and again what the
sinking of the lower works and what the eclipse of the overlying according to
the passage and again the restoration, and the varied configuration of those
coming together and standing apart, drawn in triangles or scalene figures or
even completing some other of the figures contemplated according to geometry;
he who has understood these, he says, and such things will know the meaning of
fate, that what is necessarily completed by some inviolable sequence according
to the qualitative intertwining of the stars is interpreted by the name of
fate. And since this again also was foreign to me (for I understood nothing of
the things said, as not having been educated in this education), I asked that
through those things by which it was possible for me to know the sense of
fate, through these he make the thing said clear to me; for that the starry
circles lie one in another and that the contrary motion is unrolled within
against the fixed revolution and that all these are whirled in the zodiacal
circle, these things I said I had heard also from others =36= and from the
consequence not to doubt that the rays of the lights, according to the
circular motion peculiar to each of the poles, both approach and stand apart,
and that the lower sinks and the overlying is hidden from our sights, if it
should come to be at the back of the sinking one, and all that it is likely to
reckon from the consequence; whether some figure is completed through these,
whenever the circle leads around through its own motion the star lying upon
it, so as either to come to be in a straight line of the overlying or to go
sideways according to the passage, whether through a little of the temporal
interval or through more the period of each of these comes to be according to
the magnitude of the proportion according to each of the circles, the return
necessarily coming to be either more quickly or more slowly. All these and
such things I asked him to keep silent, and only to uncover clearly for me the
power of fate, whether there is some god who is understood by the name of fate
and who has hung from himself the power of the whole and administering all by
authority toward what seems good to the


overlying of the power, or he supposes the power of fate to be a servant of
some other overlying energy, so that it too in a certain way lies under
another fate, being disposed together with the overlying cause. For if this is
believed to have the power over all, the consequence of the argument gives one
to think that nothing overlies this; but if he strongly asserts that fate,
following the motion of the stars, itself rules all things by some violent
necessity, it was safer to bear witness this almighty authority rather to the
=37= preceding than to the following, and to say that the stars are causes of
these things, or the fixed revolution, or the circles contemplated within
this, or the circle engraved obliquely on the pole. For if one should grant by
hypothesis that these are not moved and do not come to be through the
everlasting circular motion either in one another or from one another, but
always hold in the same way upon the same figure, fate would not be
constituted. If then the motion of the stars begets fate, then in vain is
this, which is enslaved to the overlying cause, thought to prevail over the
others, and it would not even be, if the motion were not. But this is not it,
says that philosopher, not thus does our argument think, that fate is of
itself contemplated in some subsistence; but since there is some one sympathy
in the things that are and the whole is continuous with itself and the things
according to each contemplated in the whole are grasped as upon one body in
one breathing-together, all the parts inclining toward one another, for this
reason, the upper lot being more originative, all the things around the earth
are disposed together with the preceding and incline toward that, the affairs
here also being moved together of necessity according to the upper motion,
each of the stars being contemplated in a different =38= power, as has been
said. As in the medical pharmacy the qualities of the kinds, mixed together by
some technical reason, have worked out something else, the thing blended from
the common, and not what each was before the mixture toward one another, so
also of the starry powers contemplated in different properties, the varied
intertwining of the different peculiarities that comes to be according to
their approaches and the distances works out the varied differences of the
things completed according to life, as of some efflux reaching from there to
us unbreakably. For this very reason also the predictions of the things that
will be, from those who more carefully philosophize such things, are not
missed. For as the one skilled in medicine, if the hot or narcotic drug should
be mixed with the salty or the mildly astringent, predicts the property that
will be constituted from the mixture of things of other natures, and what it
works, and up to what it has the strength, and for whom it becomes deadly and
for whom a protective, so also he who has attended to the things above through
care and has understood the nature of each of the things that are will know
what the power from their qualitative intertwining will complete. And this
efflux does not even in a little hold similarly toward itself, but because the
motion of the stars that are intertwined with one another is always unstable,
that too is necessarily altered together with the continuity of the otherness
according to the motion, being always mixed along with the variety of the
motion and exchanging its own energies together with the motion of the stars;
of which each of those brought forward into life =39=, having drawn according
to the lot that met him in the instant of the interval, becomes that which the
property of that part both foretold and completed; for there is every
necessity, just as in an impression of a seal the form according to the
engraving is molded around the wax that has been put in, so also the life of
the man, with whichever portion of the power flowing out from the motion of
the stars he may be brought together, is molded according to the property of
that part and becomes that which the lot of the efflux which he drew right as
life was beginning had in itself; having been once sealed in which, according
to the power defined from there, he is necessarily conformed in the property
according to life, doing or suffering these things whose beginnings or causes
the first encounter


of that starry efflux laid down. But I, as these things were being said, Will
you not cease, I said to him, going through babbling arguments and nonsense to
me, constructing that partless part of the instantaneous participation of the
things flowing upon us from above, as you say, as cause of all the things in
us, neither saying whether this is ensouled and choosing nor able to show how
the soulless and insubstantial, having no impulse of its own from nature,
prevails over the ensouled, but having set over the argument, as some tyrant
or master, the soulless =40= and unchoosing, both unstable and passing and
partless and insubstantial, over all counsel and the foresight from reasonings
and education and care and the pursuits according to virtue? You attach the
constitution and administration of the things that are from the power of that,
and you do not see into what absurdities the argument is carried out? For if
that lot of the efflux has so great a power as to complete according to itself
those who have come to be at the first passage, it is in every way that it
will lead the constitution of the things that are not followingly but
precedingly. Therefore that will lead forward the one being born according to
its own power and will not serve the coincidence of the one brought forth. For
it is unclear here which is more originative than which, both being brought
together with one another equally in the instant. For the man even before the
exit from the inward parts is somehow in motion, being moved [in] nature
through decrease and increase (since this too is a form of motion), and the
star even before the man draws the air is carried and does not stand; and the
coincidence equally of those who meet one another through motion makes the
judgment of the preceding aporetic. For those too are moved through the
circles and this, journeying through nature, is carried. And if the
coming-together toward one another comes to be for both in one part of time,
what is the difference of these toward one another, so as to think the one
more causal than the other? For if the man is because of the stars, nature
would always flow through generation, showing not even some instant interval
of the human passage. But if there are many intervening distances of those who
come to be, it is clearly =41= demonstrated that the human generation does not
follow the motion of the stars, for the one is always moved and the other is
not always generated, but this is carried by its own sequence and that again
peculiarly upon itself, no necessity joining toward one another the things
that stand apart by nature. And if your argument constructs this, that the
cause of all the things according to life lies upon that lot of time, consider
how many myriads of masters and tyrants every day and night likewise work
according to those fine and instantaneous cuts of time, night and day having
been divided, as you say, into four and twenty hours, and each hour being
partitioned into sixty segments, and again each of these segments being
crumbled according to the equal number; and as those of you who slip through
by argument into the finer observation of such things say, that each again of
these so-called fine segments is made finer by an equal-numbered division. The
multitude then gathered from these of those instantaneous gods or masters or
tyrants or I do not know how one must say, is more than one and twenty
myriads. And if one hour makes so many myriads for us, the four and twenty,
multiplied in every way according to the ratio of the one, generate the
infinite myriads of lots. But indeed your argument strongly asserts that each
of these has inviolable power. Therefore it follows that none of them is
inactive. For you would not say that inactivity is proper to the power, but
the power is in every way seen in the energy. Therefore equal also are the
completions of the power =42=; so that as many as there are segments, so many
also generations of men will of necessity be constituted according to each
hour. And if it is fitting that the possible be likewise borne witness to each
lot, it works all equally kings, long-lived, powerful, well-fated, happy, and
as many things of the more honored lot


are thought to be, the equal-honor of the power. For the lack of some one of
these becomes an accusation of the incomplete according to the power. For one
would not bear witness equal power to both the one who has energized great
things and the one who has energized small. For example, one has passed a
hundred years in a sleek and happy old age, flourishing with children,
escorted by descendants and rejoicing in the generation after these, without
disease, unharmed, in honor, without grief, having wealth abundantly, and if
any other of the things according to this life has been thought among the
honorable, being blessed through all; another together with being brought
forward into light was smothered, as indeed many of those born are courtesan
or adulterous infants whom the mothers who conceived unlawfully, handling with
their own hands, steal the proof by death. Where is the power of the lot upon
these? How did the strength of fate not suffice unto the same measure of life?
For if your argument thinks it must bear witness the possible to it, its
strength will appear equally to all the things that are. For it will not be
that the one will have strength and the other will not be able, if indeed it
is truly grasped in power; and the power is known through the completions.
Therefore life will have no irregularity, all =43= likewise showing in
themselves the highest measure of happiness, because all according to your
argument lie under fate, and you say that this can always do all things. If
then this fate can both all things and always, it will be able to do all
things upon all. But indeed there are many and varied differences of life for
men according to both the dignities and the abundances and the measures of the
times and the blends of the bodies and all as many things through which
someone is named either blessed or wretched. Then the inequality of the
completions clearly shows that that lot or fate molded by the argument cannot
do all things. For if we think the long-lived a work of power, the short-lived
is in every way of weakness. Therefore it is fitting to dogmatize that the one
of the fates is weak, and the other can. For since the short-lived is contrary
to the long-timed, each of these in every way subsists through the contraries.
For one would not attach both blessing and wretchedness from the same cause,
but if this should be accomplished through being able, the other in every way
did not stand together because of the lack of the power. For wretchedness is
grasped by no other than in not having been able to become blessed. But indeed
the wretched are more throughout life. Therefore the weakness of fate is shown
more beside the power. Where then is that unconquered and almighty and
inviolable necessity, so as to set upon that the authority of all the things
administered according to life, which has been convicted through the
consequence of the argument as weak in the more? But you will say that for
this one it wishes this, and for the other it does not wish the equal, and it
can upon both whatever it wishes. =44= In every way then you will also add the
cause of the difference of these choices. This is a man and this is a man, you
see no variation according to nature upon both, neither for this one the
beautiful or the base from choice nor for the other, but this one, having
anticipated a little of the time, leaped out of the maternal belly, nature
having thus according to the happening packed him off, and that one after this
either at once or after a little. And because of this the same life is not
allotted to both, but the one is happy or a king, if it should so happen,
being wrapped in gold and purple at the first generation, and the other of
some of the poor or slaves is not even swaddled in rags by those who generated
him. What then did he wrong, either having anticipated or having come later by
a little in time, not according to his own purpose but according to the motion
of nature, so that for this reason the dishonor of life was allotted to him?
What defense of that mistress of yours will you find upon such things? Where
is the just? Where the pious? Where the holy? Or do you say that fate cares
for none of these nor looks toward virtue nor has care of any of the goods?
Therefore if these are not through zeal


for it, it will in every way be occupied about the contrary; for the
alienation of the good clearly shows the kinship toward the evil. But you say
that neither this perhaps nor that is through concern for it? Therefore you
say that it is soulless and unchoosing and unperceiving of the beautiful and
the worse, if at least it has neither soul nor choice nor is contemplated
according to its own =45= subsistence. This is borne witness among you to have
so great a power as to preside over choosing animals and for the insubstantial
to rule the subsisting, the unsharing over those who share in life, the
soulless over the ensouled, the unchoosing over those who live together with
choice, that which is unreceptive of virtue over those who pursue virtue, in
general the not-being over the things that are? For in what is the subsistence
of this much-talked name grasped? It is not an animal, it is not seen in
outline, it is not thought a god. For how is he a god who does not look toward
virtue and the just? And what is that which is none of these? But it seems
that by you the always-present of time is called fate; for time is coextended
with every motion, whether of rivers or of stars or of men. And it differs
nothing whether sitting by water one notes by the motion of the stream the
instantaneous segments of the always-present time, or of a ship being carried
through wind, or of men journeying, or of stars being moved. For there is one
definition of motion upon all things that are moved transitively, the change
from that in which it is to that in which it is not. And if neither the
carrying of the streams nor the motion of the ships nor the journey of men,
noting the intervals of time, make fates, how do you mold the temporal signs
of the motion of the stars into a generation of fate and say that this hour or
the fraction of the =46= hour, which was signed by this sign of the motion of
the stars, becomes fate? Why then are generations of men not in every way also
brought together with every sign? For I will take up the same argument again.
For from those from whom is the cause of the man being well or badly, from the
same is in every way also the cause of being. If then what is after the
generation is from those, in every way also the generation is from the same;
and if this is not in those, neither in every way what is after this. But
indeed that the generation is not in those is clear from this: from the human
generations of those being born not flowing together uninterruptedly in the
manner of a river with the continuous of the passing flow of time, but the
births being in intervals; and of the generation of those lots which time
flowing works by the ever-moved of the carrying of the stars there is not even
anything of an interval to grasp either according to sense or by thought. What
then is that fate, whether then one more generic or whether many that are made
fine together upon the segments of time, the argument has not yet been able to
grasp through some consequence. Do you say that the stars are moved? For we
too bring forward in turn the motion of the rivers. But those through all. And
these likewise through the whole. But those in time. Nor are these outside of
time. But it is not possible to find upon those a temporal beginning of the
motion. And what beginning from times will you think of for these? But those
are always moved according to the same things and in the same way. Nor would
one bear witness to these the contrary motion, the water naturally always =47=
flowing down from the overlying toward the slope. So that either grant also
that the motion of the rivers generates some fates, or we too will not yield
this power to the motion of the stars. But of this power, he says, is the Ram
or the Bull, and of this each of the planets. Whenever then one of those that
have wandered, being led around by the circle, comes to be in one of these
either alone or with others periodically, the conjunction of the powers has
worked this life for the one brought together with this hour according to the
generation. O the vain-talk. You say the Bull is slavish, if it should so
happen, because this animal is tamed under a yoke, and the Ram beneficent
because of the


bearing of the wools; and that one that has come to be in one of these [or]
this one that is wrapped according to the contrary, according to the power
naturally inherent in it, through the mixing of the peculiarities, constructs
such a lot of those being born. What then? Tell me, is the bull willingly
under the yoke? And do you say that being sheared is according to the ram's
heart? And does the harmful and narcotic to the one circulating the highest
pole, or the hot to the fiery one, or to some other of those contemplated, the
inherent power belong willingly or according to the =48= unwilling? If they
are willingly in evils and become harmful from choice, he who says these
things votes misfortune of them outright, in that, the better lot lying before
them by authority, they set the more dishonored in pleasure; but if each of
these is not from choice what it is thought, but came to be from some
necessity, then some other fate overlying these again has spun the
peculiarities of the natures and powers; so that it is necessary to seek other
stars superior to these and a motion peculiar upon themselves, whose
qualitative intertwining according to some necessity of fate has worked the
Bull to be slavish or the Ram injurious or the Crab headless or if any other
thing about each of these the nonsense of the wise goes through. And if these
things that appear to us are such according to a necessity of some fate, in
every way also the argument will further invent another necessitating cause of
such a constitution of that overlying necessity, and another of that, and
again another of the other, and thus the argument going forward into the
infinite will nowhere stand, molding in addition fate of fate and lot of lot
and necessity of necessity. And as I was going through these things and
throwing the argument out toward the absurd through such thoughts, the
philosopher conversing with me, having cut off in the middle the argument
going forward through an attack, Why do you fight, he says, with the evident?
And why do you not follow those who through the numbers have observed the
truth of the things that are, to whom if the hour of the generation of the one
brought forth should be taken toward exactness, they =49= foretell through
consequence the whole life of the one born; the time, the manner, the
peculiarities of the characters, the dangerous circumstances, marriages and
child-makings and successes of dignities, or also the reverse, childlessness
and diseases and dishonors and shortness of life and the evils from poverty?
These things then having been signified beforehand through exactness and the
truth having been witnessed by the outcome, what argument still is there for
you disbelieving the necessity of fate? And I, having said that I would seek
the causes of such predictions a little later, asked: whether the cause of
such outcomes is irrational and coincidental, or they also invent some reason
together with this observation, so that what comes to be not come to be
outside the likely? And very much indeed, he says, reason too is upon the
things that come to be. For whenever from the qualitative coincidence of the
qualities according to the stars such a blend of body is constituted for the
one being born, he who has grasped through exactness the contemplation of the
heavenly things has also understood beforehand that this blend will be
constituted about the one born, and that the life can suffice up to this
measure, more upon the stronger, and again less upon the more diseased blend
of the body. And I, having laughed upon the things said, You had escaped us, I
said to him, in thinking the fates to be offspring of Galen and Hippocrates
and such, since those too, having left the motion of the stars farewell,
predict the future stochastically from the underlying. Whenever eyes are
hollow and temples collapsed and the brows hardened around with the skin, they
foretell death. And to many, I said, more diseasedly disposed in their bodies
and expected as good as already =50= to die, the threads of such a fate were
unspun toward the reverse from medicine; as indeed also the trainer Herodicus
the argument of Plato has related, that through gymnastic experience he was
not able to lead the body back into complete health, the disease being at the
point of death, and always


putting off the present death through a device, he prepared for himself not a
security of life, but a prolongation of death. Whence also he lasted into old
age, having sophistically contrived not to die by a long death. Therefore not
even according to this argument is fate grasped as unbreakable for you, if
indeed some art is found that loosens the necessity. But nothing, he says,
does such an argument dissolve the demonstration concerning fate from the
predictions. For it would also be possible for this to be signified beforehand
by the one who has examined through exactness the motion of the heavenly
things, so that death be signified beforehand as fixed for the one and
doubtful for the other. And I, Not thus, I said, does the argument of
necessity strongly assert; for other is the argument of the contingent,
whichever of the two may happen to come to be, and other that of the
unchangeable, which it is not possible to take upon the contingents; but
either this in every way or the other; and to be shaken in hopes toward both
outcomes is far from the arguments of necessity. Except this, I say to him,
from where does he think the prediction of the things that will turn out has
the credible? And he, Having heard me going through many times, he says, do
you still even now ask about the same things? That there are some peculiar
powers of the stars; and the ever-moved of the carrying works out countless
differences of the powers from their qualitative intertwining. He then who has
been brought forward into =51= life through generation, having been impressed
according to the lot of time that fell together with him, which was configured
thus by the stars, whatever the power of that hour should be grasped as
having, these things he will also have about his life according to necessity
and otherwise he does not have. What then, I said, the disasters according to
war and the earthquakes and the fallings of the cities and the shipwrecks of
the ten-thousand-burden merchant ships with the men themselves, floods and
burnings and chasms and as many such kinds of total destruction, how will
these save the argument of the prediction? How many passions has life shown in
the former times, and how many also in the times according to us; the flood
upon Noah and the burning of Sodom, or the Egyptian army having become
underwater in the Red Sea, or the slaughters of the aliens after these, those
countless man-killings, or the spontaneous death in a little time of the
Israelite people broken in many myriads, or the hundred eighty-five thousands
of the Assyrians made dead in one inclination of time, and the histories of
the evils next in both Median and Hellenic disasters that fell together in
sea-fights and in great land-fights, and all as many such things of which the
memory becomes a teacher to those next. And even if we pass by all these, as
many things as the life according to =52= us has related would also become
sufficient toward the witness of the things said. For who does not know the
great metropolis of Bithynia numbered among the outstanding of the cities? Who
is ignorant of the broad and spacious Thrace? How war utterly wiped out the
one, and earthquake together with fire the other, in an instant of time. How
many children there, how many infants, those of middle age, those past age,
both free and slaves, ruling and subjects, rich, poor, strong, being nursed in
disease, all were destroyed in one inclination of a moment. The fire fed
equally upon all; for all the houses became tombs. Where are those
