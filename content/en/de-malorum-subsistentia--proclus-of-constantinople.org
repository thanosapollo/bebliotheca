A SINGLE BOOK ON THE SUBSISTENCE OF EVILS
1 The nature of evil, what it is and whence it has its generation, some even
of those before us have inquired, –––having examined evil itself by itself,
whether it is or is not, and if it is, how it is and whence it has come into
being and subsistence; ... but we ... having recorded the things that ... have
been contemplated by the divine Plato concerning the nature of evil; –––and we
shall in every way be nearer to the apprehension of the things sought, having
found the thought of Plato, and from there as it were having kindled a light
for our inquiries. Whether then evil is, or is not, must first be examined;
and if it is, whether it is among the intelligibles or not; and if among the
sensibles, whether it has come to subsist according to a preceding cause or
not; and if not, whether some substance must be given to it, or its being must
be set down as altogether without substance; and if this is so, how it came to
subsist, the principle being other, and whence it begins and up to what it
proceeds; and further how and whence, providence existing, there is evil;
–––and upon all of these–––we think we have accomplished nothing, if we should
miss the contemplation of that. 2 Let the beginning of the contemplation
concerning them be according to nature <let it be>, whether evil is among the
beings or not; and how is it possible that this be, which is altogether
without share in the principle of the beings? For neither can darkness partake
of light, nor vice of virtue, nor evil of the good. ––– <thus> –––evil must be
nowhere among the beings; for if this too is from the good, how is that still
cause of all things fair and good, which brought forth the nature of evil? <or
if not from there>; the good <then was> not cause of all the beings, nor
principle, if <evil> ranked among the things that are escapes the procession
from that. And in general, if everything that in any way comes to subsist,
having partaken of being, came to subsist, and that which has partaken of
being must also partake of the one–––, and it neither was nor will be lawful
for the second things to make what they make otherwise than together with the
things that lie above them, intellect together with life, life together with
being, and all things together with the one: then surely evil too must have
suffered one of two things: either it has not partaken of being at all, or
having in any way partaken of being it also partakes at the same time of the
cause beyond–––. Either the principle is not even, <or> evil has neither come
to be nor is. –––and from both sides it is necessary to say that evil is
nowhere. And if ... the good is beyond <being and fountain> of the beings–––.
How <is> evil <one of the beings, if> it is to be deprived of such an
appetite? ––– 3 ––– (evil) ... still more distant from the good even than
not-being itself; –––and what is farther from this is more without substance
than what is near. That which in no way is, then, is more than the so-called
evil. ––– And if also, <as the account of Plato>, the father of this cosmos
not only brings to subsistence the nature of goods, but also wishes that there
be no evil anywhere, what device is there for evil to have come to subsist
toward subsistence when it is unwilled by the demiurge? For it is not lawful
that he should wish some things and make others, but the will was at the same
time also making in the case of the divine <substances>; so that evil is not
only unwilled by him, but also without subsistence, not as of him not making
it (for this is not even lawful to think), but as of him making it not even to
be; for the will was not of evil not being made by him, but of it not being at
all; what then is there still that will make it be, when the one who brings it
toward not-being is the hypostasis of all things and father? For what is
contrary to him and from where? For neither is the evil-making from him, for
it is not lawful for him; and if from elsewhere, it is absurd; for all things
in the cosmos are from the father, some immediately, as has been said, and
some as of others acting. 4 The account then that banishes evil from being
would be some such, and would put forward such things to us speaking
persuasively. But the one that has sent forth the contrary to this


voice having uttered––– must surely say one of two things: either that virtue
is not contrary to vice, the whole to the whole and the partial ones to the
analogous, or that what fights against the good is not in every way evil. And
yet what of either of these would be <more paradoxical or> more unfitted to
the nature of the things? For the vices fight against the virtues; and it is
clear that they fight both from the rest of human life, according to which the
unjust are opposed to the just, and the intemperate to the temperate, and also
from the division in the souls themselves, if you will; as when in the
incomplete the reason leads one way, but the passion forces, and when both
fight the better is defeated by the worse; for what else is there in these
than the temperate of the soul in faction against the intemperate character?
––– <and what in> the other evils, in which we perceive the soul in faction?
For <in general> the apparent oppositions of the evils toward the good people
are much earlier in the souls themselves invisibly; and this is the last
ignorance and disease of the soul, whenever the better in us <and the good
logoi existing in it> are overpowered by the earth-born and dishonored
passions. ––– And if the vices are contrary to the virtues, as we said, and
evil is <altogether> contrary to the good, for the nature of the good is not
of a nature to be in faction toward itself, but as offspring <being> from one
henad and one cause, it is held <toward itself> by likeness and unity and
friendship, and the greater goods are preservative of the lesser, and the
lesser are ordered by the more complete; then surely the vices must not be
vices in word only, but each <is> really evil; and not a lesser good; for
nowhere is the lesser contrary to the greater good, just as neither is the
less hot contrary to the more hot, nor the less cold to the more cold. And if
the vices of the soul should be agreed to belong to the nature of evil, it
would have been shown that evil belongs among the beings. 5 <And not in this
way only, but also> because evil is the destructive of each; –––and the good
of each is the preservative of each, <wherefore also> the desire of the good
is in all; for being and being preserved is for all <from there, just as
again> not-being and being destroyed is through the nature of evil. It is
necessary then either that evil be, or that there be nothing destructive of
anything; ––– for if there are no destructives, neither can there be
destruction; and if there is no destruction, neither generation, since every
generation comes to be through the destruction of another; and if there is no
generation, the whole cosmos will be incomplete; for it will not have mortal
kinds of animals in it, but it must, if it is going to be sufficiently
complete, <says the Timaeus> [Pl. Tim. 41bƒc]. <If then the cosmos must be a
happy god [ib. 34b 8], it must preserve perfectly the likeness toward the
complete living being [cf. ib. 31b 1]; if this, the mortal kinds [ib. 41b 7]
must also fill up the all; if this>, there must also be generation <and
destruction>; and if this, there must also be the destructives of the beings
and the generatives, some of some things, others of others; for neither
generation nor destruction is for all from the same things. And if there are
destructives, which coming to be in those that have obtained generation
destroy their power, there must also be evil. For this was evil, the
destructive of each of the generated things, in which it is primarily by
itself. For of soul there are other destructives, of body others, and what is
destroyed is other; and the manner of destruction is not the same, but of one
substantial, of the other vital; and of one the substance being led into
not-being and destruction, of the other the life fleeing altogether from being
toward another not-being. The same account then will both preserve the whole
cosmos complete for us, and will set evil among the things that are; so that
not only will evil be because of the good, but there will also be a good for
it by its very being. This then is the most paradoxical, which will also be
more known from what follows. 6 ––– every good that admits the difference of
more and less, according to the more is more complete and is established
nearer its own fountain, and according to the less, becoming both dimmed and
more incomplete through the deficiency, stands away toward the below of <its
own henad>. ––– Do we not then also say that injustice itself and


intemperance are more and less, or are all intemperate and unjust in the equal
manner? We shall in no way say so. What then? Is it not that, insofar as
injustice is more and less, by as much as it is less injustice, by so much it
has less stood away from the nature of the good, and by as much as it has the
affection of injustice more greatly, by so much it is more greatly without
share in the good? One must surely concede. But we said that every good, both
the lesser and the greater, when increased comes to be nearer the first of
goods, and the completely good is the same as the most good. But the increase
of injustice was in every way a deficiency of the good; therefore it is no
longer fitting to call injustice a good, neither greater nor lesser, but in
every way evil. For the lesser good when increased becomes more greatly good,
<for also the less hot and the less cold each become more>; but the unjust
when increased does not become a greater good. Being then contrary toward the
good, how would it not be among the evils? These things then this account also
will say and will speak freely concerning the subsistence of evils. ––– 7 –––
If then these things too are of necessity because of the all-powerful and
all-good activity <of the first causes>, neither would the good always be in
all the beings in the same way, nor would the generation of evil be driven out
of the beings. For if there is what is able at one time to partake of the
good, and at another time again to be deprived of the participation of that,
there will of necessity be a privation of the partaking of the good; and the
privation, not being able to be by itself nor altogether released from the
nature of which it is privation, being in a way empowered from it, through the
entanglement with it is established into the rank of things contrary toward
the good; –––the good then ... also empowers its own privation. For as the
good generated in all the beings the first power, so also the good everywhere
in each of the beings generates its own power, with which the privation of the
good, as has been said, being entangled and having strengthened its own
feebleness by the power of that, becomes contrary to the good, having been
strengthened by the mixture toward it and having taken power for fighting
toward what is near; ––– for there is not a wicked form of life, such that the
power of reason has been altogether extinguished; for reason remains within,
speaking faintly, even though passions of every kind have been poured around
it, and understanding does not leave the soul above. ––– 8 ––– <and somewhere>
unmixed toward the privation, <and somewhere already> with such a mixture. For
what at one time partakes of the primarily good is filled with the
interweaving of the not-good; since also being-itself <and the nature of being
above> is really being and is being only, but in the last <of the beings> it
is somehow mixed with not-being; for what is in one respect being, in another
not-being, and at one time being, and infinitely often not being, and this
being, but all other things not being; what would one say it is more than that
it is not, filled altogether with not-being; and not-being itself, one the
in-no-way being, but even falling short of the last nature, which is
accidentally, able to be neither by itself nor accidentally, for the in-no-way
being is not in one respect and is not in another; and the not-being together
with being, whether to call it privation of being or otherness [Pl. Parm.
160d] <is lawful>; and the one is altogether not-being, and the other above is
no less than being, <as the Eleatic stranger says> [Pl. soph. 258b 1–2], but
in the things that at one time are, at another time are not, <more dim than
being>, but itself too in a way overpowered by being. 9 Just as then, if
someone asked concerning not-being, whether it is or is not, we said that the
altogether not-being, having partaken of being from nowhere, is in no way
being; but we conceded to the one who asked this to rank the not-being in a
way among the things that are. According to the same things also evil, since
this too is twofold, one solely evil, and the other not unmixed toward the
good, the one we <shall set beyond the in-no-way> being ..., and the other we
shall rank among the things that are; <for neither of being through the
mediation of the good>, nor of the good through being is it able still to
remain empty; for at the same time it is being and it is good. And the
altogether


evil, being a falling-away from the first of goods and as it were an exit,
reasonably has been deprived also of being; for how would what is not able to
partake of the good have a passage into the beings? And what is not altogether
evil, being subcontrary [Pl. Theaet. 176a] to some good and not to every, is
also ranked and is made good through the excess of the goods of the wholes;
and it is evil to those to which it has been opposed, but it depends on the
others as goods; for it must not fight against those, but all things must
follow according to justice or not even be at all. 10 <Rightly then> Plato in
the Timaeus according to the demiurgic will says that all things are good, and
nothing is base [Tim. 30a 2–3]; but in the discourses toward the geometer he
says that evils neither perish, but of necessity [Theaet. 176a] <he proposes
that they have come to be among the beings. For all things are made good by
the will of the father> and nothing is evil toward that one's demiurgy,
neither of the beings nor of the things that come to be. ––– since also
darkness, the one altogether unmixed ... and without share in light is not,
but the one that has come to be in the light and is bounded by it is
<everywhere> among the beings. ––– All things then are good to the father of
all, and evil is in those that are not able to stand altogether according to
the good. ––– How then evil is and how it is not is clear from these things.
For all, both those who say that all things are good and those who do not, in
one respect speak rightly, and in another not rightly. For that all the beings
are, is true; but also not-being is interwoven with being. All things then are
good in that there is no unordered and unmixed evil; and evil is, for those
for whom it is also evil; and it is of those whose nature is not of a nature
to remain unmixedly in the good. 11 In which of the beings evil is and how it
came to subsist and whence, would be next after these things to contemplate,
because its nature too has appeared to us among the beings. We must begin from
above and see, if somehow we are able, where evil is. 30 ––– we must speak
also concerning matter, whether it is evil, or not. For it is in no way
possible that evil belong to it as an accident, because by itself it is
qualityless and formless and a substrate, but not in a substrate, and simple,
but not one thing in another. And if it is evil at all, as some say, it is
evil according to substance, in the way they also say, making matter the
primarily evil and <what the gods hate [Hom. Y 65]>. For what else is evil
than unmeasure and indefiniteness and all such as are privations of the good?
For the good is measure of all things and limit and bound and completeness; so
that evil is unmeasure and infinite-itself and incomplete and indefinite. And
all these are in matter and primarily, not being other things besides it, but
it and what its being is. Therefore the primarily evil and the nature of evil
and the last of all things is matter. And if good also is twofold, one the
good-itself and nothing else than good, <and the other in another and some
good and not primarily good, evil also will be twofold, one as it were itself
and primarily evil and nothing else than> evil, and the other in another and
some evil and evil through that and by participation or likeness of that evil.
And as the good-itself is first, so also the evil-itself is last of the
beings; for neither of the good is it possible that anything be better, nor of
evil worse; for we also call all other things better and worse because of
these. And the last of the beings is matter; for all other things are of a
nature to act or to be acted upon; but it neither, having been deprived of the
power of both. Therefore the evil-itself and the primarily evil is matter. 31
And if also in bodies the contrary to nature is of matter prevailing, as has
been said, and in souls evil and weakness ... by being drunk with the
indefiniteness around it, being likened toward it, why leaving this do we seek
another principle of evils to blame and fountain of their subsistence? <But
if> matter is evil ..., one of two things is necessary, either that the good
be cause of evil, or two principles of the beings. For everything that in any
way has come to subsist must either be a principle of the wholes or from a
principle; and matter, if it is from a principle, itself too has from the good
the passage into being, but if it is a principle, it submits to us two
principles of the beings fighting against one another, the primarily good and
the primarily


evil; but this is impossible; for neither are the first things two; for from
where at all, if there is no monad? For if each of the two is one, there must
be before both the one, by which these are both one, and one principle. Nor is
evil from the good; for as the cause of goods is more greatly good, so also
what is generative of evil would be more greatly evil; and the good will no
longer even have its own nature, bringing forth the principle of evil. ––– <so
that> the good will be evil as cause of evil, and evil good as brought forth
from the good. 32 And if matter is necessary toward the all and this all-great
cosmos and happy god [Pl. Tim. 34 b 8] would not be if matter were absent, how
is the nature of evil still to be referred to this? For evil is one thing and
the necessary another, and the one is such without which it is impossible to
be, and the other is privation of being. If then it also furnishes its own use
toward the demiurgy of the whole cosmos and has been brought forth primarily
to be a receptacle of generation and as it were nurse [Pl. Tim. 49a 6] and
mother [ib. 50d 3, 51a 4], how would it still be called evil and the primarily
evil? And if we also say unmeasure and the indefinite and the infinite and
each of such things in many ways, for both as fighting against measure and <as
absence> of it and removal and as underlying it and lacking measure and limit,
and matter is not of a nature to fight nor to do anything at all, for which
there is not even being acted upon according to nature through deficiency of
the power of being acted upon, and again it is not a taking-away of measure
and bound because it is not the same as privation; for privation is not when
those are present, but matter both is and receives the impression of them:
then surely its infinite and unmeasured must be lacking measure and bound. And
how would what lacks these be contrary to them? And how is what needs the good
still evil? For evil flees the nature of the good, and in general every
contrary the contrary state. And if ... it also conceives generation, and, <as
he himself says>, it nourishes, and nothing evil is from it, it being mother
to those from it, or rather those generated in it. 33 ––– And what would it do
to others, not being able to act? And how again will the qualityless by itself
have acting? And does matter lead the souls toward itself or are those led
from themselves <and become separable> by their own power and powerlessness?
For if they are led from themselves, this was their evil, the impulse toward
the worse and the appetite, but not matter; for to every the flight from the
better is evil, and much more the toward the worse; and through weakness they
suffer what such ones must suffer, having chosen badly. And if they are led
from matter, where is the self-moved and the choices of the soul? ––– if
matter leads all toward itself and troubles and forces––– 34 –––For in the
Timaeus calling it mother [51a 4] and nurse of generation [52d 4–5] and
joint-cause [46c 7] of the demiurgy of the cosmos––– but in the discourses of
the Eleatic stranger he refers the cause of the disorder of the all <to an
underlying nature, saying that the cosmos has acquired all things beautiful
from the one who composed it, but from the former state the contraries of
these have generation in it [Pl. pol. 273bc]>. And in the Philebus bringing
even matter itself and all the nature of the infinite from the one and in
general <setting> the divine cause before the distinction of limit and
infinite, he concedes that it is inspired and good through the participation
of god and the generation from god and in no way evil. Of evils one must <he
says> seek some other causes, and not god, <as has been said in other places
[resp. II 379c 6–7]>. Therefore the disorder and evil is not through matter,
but through what is moved irregularly and without order [Pl. Tim. 30a 4–5];
for this is the body-like, <which the Eleatic stranger says> is cause of the
disorder [Pl. pol. 273b 4] ... and not being able to be matter, –––matter
being unmoved by itself. ––– For the traces of the different forms leading to
one motion and another, show the whole motion irregular. <This then is the
former state; for not> being able to be mastered by the forms, it shows itself
unordered and unbeautiful. ––– 35 –––That it is not from


matter that evil is, nor in bodies, is clear from these things; for matter and
what is moved irregularly are not the same. And that matter also is not <to be
set down as> the primarily evil <the Socrates in the Philebus, I think, has
sufficiently shown>, <generating> the infinity from god [Pl. Phileb. 23c 9].
And if the infinite is to be called matter immediately, matter is from god, if
indeed the primarily infinite <must be said to generate> all substantial
infinity depending on one cause from god, <and especially> that which is not
able to make the mixture with limit, as god is cause of their subsistence and
of the mixture. This then also leads the nature of body as body to one cause,
god; for he himself is the one who generated the mixed. Therefore neither is
body evil nor matter; for these are offspring of god, the one as mixture, the
other as infinite. ––– <and he himself saying in those>: therefore becoming
things and those from which they become, all three genera he has furnished to
us [Pl. Phileb. 27a 11–12]; body then, this too one of all things that are,
since it is mixed, and the one in it limit and logos, and the other infinite,
will be from there in two ways, both according to the whole and according to
the parts. For what else is the infinite in it than matter? And what the limit
than form? And what from these than the composite? If then also all the
becoming things themselves and those from which they become, it is mixed and
limit and infinite; and what crafts all these is another and fourth, <as he
himself says> [Phileb. 27 aƒb]; we would say that neither matter nor form nor
the mixed is brought forth from elsewhere than from god <as mixed>. And what
coming to be from there would be evil? For neither does heat produce cooling
nor the good produce evils; therefore neither matter nor body is to be called
evil. 36 ––– that matter is neither good nor evil. For if good, it will be an
end and not last of all things, and that for the sake of which and object of
desire; for every good is such, since the primarily good is end and that for
the sake of which all things and object of desire for all the beings. Or if
again evil, it will be a god and another principle of the beings and in
faction toward the cause of goods, and there will be two fountains let go to
flow [Pl. leg. I 636d 7–8] from the contrary, another of goods, and another of
evils, and not even for the gods themselves will there be a life without pain
nor outside mortal distress, for whom there is something hostile and alien and
as it were troubling. And if it is neither, what would it be by itself? Or
this which has often been said must be said of it, the necessary? For the
nature of the good is one, and that of evil another, and these are contrary to
one another; and a third is another, being neither simply good, nor evil, <but
necessary. For evil> leads away from the good and flees the nature of that;
but the necessary <for the sake of the good> is all that it is and has the
reference to it and through that has had whatever generation it has; and if
for the sake of generation of something <...>, and nothing else for the sake
of it, in which case we would also call it end and good; it must surely be
called necessary toward generation and not evil, and to have come to be from
god as necessary and to be necessary for the forms that are not able to be
established upon themselves. For the cause of all goods had to bring forth not
only the goods that are such also from themselves, but also that nature, which
is not simply and from itself a good, but reaches after a good, reaching after
which it also gives to others generation into being and in whatever way upon
itself. For its lack of goods contributes toward the demiurgy of the
sensibles, since being too does not bring to subsistence only the beings, but
also those that reach after participation of being, for which being is
according to the appetite of being. At any rate the primarily desired is one
thing, that which reaches after that and has the good in this another; another
is all the intermediate, which is desired by some, and reaches after others,
namely those before it and those for the sake of which it is. 37 Matter then,
examining it from this account at least, we would find the same, as neither
good nor evil, but necessary only; and as having come to be for the sake of a
good, a good, but simply not good; and as last of the beings evil, if indeed
what has stood farthest from the good is evil, but simply not evil, but, as
has been said, necessary. And in general that evil itself is somewhere by
itself is not true; for it is not


the unmixed nor the primarily evil. For if evil were subcontrary to every
good, there would have to be, the good being before that in another by itself
and primarily, also evil twofold, one itself, and the other in another. But if
evil is contrary to those of the goods whose being is in another, much more
surely also is evil in another, and is not by itself; for neither is the good,
to which evil is subcontrary, but in another and not separate. And what would
there be contrary to the primarily good, not that it is evil, but even any
other of the beings? For all the beings are through that and for the sake of
that. And to be contrary because of the contrary nature is impossible, but the
contrary itself not to be; for contraries are destroyed by one another, and in
general all contraries depend on one summit and genus; and of the primarily
good what genus would there be? For what is beyond the nature of the good? And
what of the beings would become of the same genus with that? For there would
have to be something other before both, of which those would be a part; and
the good would no longer be principle of the beings, but that would be the
common of both. Therefore there is nothing contrary to the primarily good, nor
even to all the participated, but to those for whom the participation is not
in the same way. 38 ––– <since also> when privation is present it is not yet
evil, but when it has become complete the nature of evil too has departed, I
mean as when disorder is present, but not all, there is disease in body; for
the privation of all order at the same time destroyed both the substrate and
the evil in it. And when it has not yet become, it is privation, but it is not
evil; –––none of these is yet evil. And in general disorder and the
unmeasured, as has been said, must be taken in one way as the absence <of
those>, of measure <I mean> and of order, and in another way as the nature
contrary toward them; for the one fights against measure and order, and the
other is only a removal of those and nothing else than negation, or rather
those when present are what they are, and when absent leave behind their own
privations. If then evil is contrary to the good and is in faction toward it,
but privation neither fights against its own state nor is of a nature to do
anything, whose being even is such, altogether dim and feeble, as their
account; how would we still refer the evil-making to this, from which all
acting has been taken away? For this belongs to form and power; ––– In which
then of the beings evil is and is not, is clear from these things. 39 And
since it is in one way in souls, and in another in bodies–––; whether the evil
in souls is greater than that in bodies ...? ––– <and to a greater good the
greater evil is contrary; so that> the evil in souls is greater than in
bodies, not for all, but for those whose power is of a nature to suffer; but
for those whose activity only, evil is faded and an absence of the whole
completeness and a lowering only; for the one is contrary to virtue, the other
to the goods of body, and the one to that according to intellect, the other to
that according to nature. By as much then as intellect is better than nature
and that according to intellect than that according to nature, by so much
surely also that contrary to intellect is beyond that contrary to nature
<toward evil>. And if the one is destructive of substance, and the other of
power only, what wonder? For whenever of the same thing the one destroys the
substance and the other the power, then the destructive of substance is more
greatly evil; but whenever of one thing substance and of another power, it is
not absurd that the destructive of power ... exceed in evil, <as> if the power
of another is better than the substance of this, as ... the powers of soul
<are said> <both to generate and> to preserve bodily substance. <This then
