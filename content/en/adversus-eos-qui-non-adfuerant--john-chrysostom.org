* Against Those Who Were Not Present
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

=63.477= An exhortatory homily delivered in the church of Saint Anastasia to
those who were absent; and a demonstration concerning the contests and
prizes of the blessed and righteous Job.

1. The fewer those assembled than usual, the greater the eagerness we too
shall use. For it would not be just that the negligence of those who stayed
away should spoil our zeal, but for this very reason we shall set a more
abundant table, so that those who did not come, learning the loss by
experience, may thereafter become more earnest about these gatherings.
Therefore I also exhort your charity to attend to what is said; for so your
gain will be twofold: by conveying what is said to those who stayed away,
you will make them more earnest, and you will make your own soul more
philosophic. For just as the nature of the earth, when neglected, brings
forth noxious weeds, but when it continually enjoys the hands of husbandmen,
yields ripe fruit; so too the soul of a man: the one that lies in negligence
begets thorns of transgressions; the one that enjoys providence bears the
flourishing fruit of virtue. Therefore a wise man also exhorts, saying: Like
a field is a foolish man, and like a vineyard is a man lacking sense; if you
leave him, he will become waste and overgrown with grass. So that this may
not happen now, we continually take in hand the sickle of the word; and if
anything wicked sprouts, we cut it out without ceasing; but if anything is
ripe and has fruit, we nourish and water it, and bring it to its prime,
showing continual care. For we need a double providence, or rather even a
triple: one, that we may be delivered from vice; another, that we may
acquire virtue; and after these, that we may guard what has been acquired;
and there especially much toil is needed; since that wicked demon, envying
our goods, attacks those who have succeeded the more fiercely; and just as
pirates and wreckers pass by ships that have sand =63.478=.20, but if they
see one carrying precious cargo and wealth stored within, they attack these,
boring from below, assaulting from above, setting every device in motion; so
too the devil is accustomed to assault and envy and plot against those who
have gathered most virtue. So many men, then, were there throughout the
world in the time of Job, and against him alone he stripped for combat, and
set all his devices in motion; yet he could not make a shipwreck, but even
made the cargo greater and the commerce larger. For such is virtue: when
struck it becomes stronger, and when plotted against it is made more secure.
Which indeed happened also in the case of that blessed man, who, struck from
every side, stood more secure, and receiving countless darts, did not yield,
but emptied the devil's quiver, and himself did not fall nor was tripped,
but like an excellent helmsman, neither when the sea was raging and the
waves rising was he sunk, nor when there was calm did he become more
negligent; but in either difference of seasons he kept his own skill equal,
and neither did wealth puff him up, nor did poverty bring him low. Neither,
then, when affairs were running with the current was he supine and relaxed,
nor when almost the whole house was overturned and utter ruin came was he
thrown into confusion and put his own courage to shame. Let the rich hear,
let the poor hear; for the narrative is useful to both; or rather the
history is profitable to all men, both those in prosperity and those in
misfortunes. For having taken up both kinds of weapons, the champion of
piety, the crowned victor of the world, set up the trophy in both, and when
that demon came against him with every kind of war, he arrayed himself
against all, and in all was proclaimed; and just as a noble soldier who
knows how to fight by night, to fight against walls, to fight at sea, to
fight on foot, and to shoot, and to brandish a spear, and with slings and
javelins and every manner of battle to prevail over the enemy and to conquer
everywhere; so too that noble man =63.479= bore every trial with great
courage: that from poverty, that from famine, that from disease, that from
pain, that from the loss of his children, that from friends, that from
enemies, that from his wife, that from his servants. For there was no human
misfortune that was not emptied into that body. Yet he was carried above all
the nets and became higher than the devil's fishing-rods; and what is more
wonderful, that all things came upon him, and all with excess, and all at
once.

2. For do not look only at this, that he suffered so many things, but add
that it was not little by little nor at intervals, but at once and together.
And this is no small addition to the trials; for of all other men first no
one would be found who endured all things together, but if he wrestles with
poverty, still he enjoys health; and if he has been pierced through by both
poverty and disease, yet he has often enjoyed a wife comforting him to bear
the terrible things, and becoming a harbor to him; and if he has not enjoyed
such a wife, still not one counseling such ruinous things; and if even one
counseling such ruinous things, still he did not also lose all his children
at a stroke; and if even at a stroke, still not by such a manner of death;
and if even by such a manner of death, still he had friends exhorting him;
and if he did not have them exhorting, still not also trampling upon him
thus; and if even trampling upon him, still not also servants reproaching
him; and if even reproaching, still not also spitting in his face; and if
even spitting in his face, still he was not also pierced through by so great
a disease; and if even pierced through by so great a disease, still he
enjoyed a chamber and a shelter, and did not sit upon the dunghill; and if
even he sat upon the dunghill, still he had those who stretched out a hand;
and if he did not have those who stretched out a hand, still not also those
who trampled upon him. But this man endured all these things, and what is
more wonderful, as I said before, that all together, which makes the
hardships appear double and triple, when the combatant does not even have
the consolation from a pause, but by the continuity of the onset the tumult
becomes greater and the confusion larger; which happened in his case. For
the loss of the sheep and the burning was succeeded by the seizure of the
oxen, and that by the taking of the asses, and that by the captivity of the
camels and the slaughter of the servants, and that by the loss of the
children and that terrible and novel death, and the more terrible tomb: for
the same thing became at once both death and tomb, and the table, which now
received the bodies feasting, now cut to pieces, and the bowls and the cups
receiving blood with the wine, and the limbs cut in pieces. Yet this harsh
tragedy still waits for other things harsher again, without even a little
breathing-space. For from here came the springs of worms, the streams of
ichor, the sitting on the dunghill, the potsherd that scraped the sides, the
stench of the sores that brought in that novel famine, and did not let him
touch the food even when it was seen, and put in a disgust worse than the
famine; and these things not for two and ten and twenty and a hundred days,
but for many months. And not even here did the wrestlings stop, but while he
was in these things and being fried from every side, from within, from
without, =63.480= the devices of the wife also came upon him. For the
partner of his house becomes a weapon of the demon, and she shoots the
husband, lending her tongue to the devil, and she strikes with darts more
bitter and more destructive than those already mentioned. And not even here
did the wrestlings come to an end, but it was again a beginning and prelude of
the array. For when nothing more came to the devil from this, the chorus of
friends arrived, displaying the things of enemies in a mask of sympathy, and
they leap upon him as he lies, and they scrape the sores again, succeeding
one another and not even letting him breathe, and circling many circles,
dancing a kind of harsh dance. Shall I speak also of the unbearable trial of
the night, itself novel and strange? For to all other men, even if they
suffer countless terrible things, even if they dwell in prisons, even if
they are bound with a chain, even if they mourn misfortunes, even if they
endure mutilation of body, even if they are pressed by poverty, even if by
disease, even if by toils, even if by hardships, still when night comes it
brings a drug of consolation, releasing the body from toils, loosing the
soul from cares; but in his case then even the harbor became a reef, and the
drug became a wound, and the consolation was a kind of addition of harsher
pain, and the storm became more fearful in the night that affords calm to
all men; and he fled from the day as from waves, because of those
intolerable pains, but found billows and whirlwinds and reefs and rocks, so
as to seek again the waves of the day. Therefore he himself, narrating this
novel suffering, cried out saying: If I lie down, I say: When is day? If I
rise, I say: When is evening? Why, tell me? For in the day you reasonably
seek the night; for he calls night "evening," as granting to all a release
from the day's evils; but when you have come into night, and calm, and
forgetfulness of those pains and cares, why do you seek the day again?
Because for me the night is harsher than the day; for it does not grant me a
release from toils, but intensification and tumults and confusions; and
narrating this very thing he said: You terrify me in dreams, and in visions
you strike me with dread. For he was terrified, seeing fearful sights in the
night, and enduring unbearable fear, and much dismay and astonishment.

3. Have you not grown weary hearing these successive misfortunes? But he did
not grow weary suffering. Therefore I exhort your charity to wait a little
longer; for we have not yet said the whole, nor have we added the other
excess. For one was that all the evils among men one body endured; another,
that all together, and he did not even have the slightest pause; but I wish
to speak a third also; what then is this? That each of the things mentioned
not only came upon him together, but also with great excess and intensity;
for the poverty was harsher than all poverty, and the disease, and the
sitting, and the loss of the children, and that of all his goods. Consider:
Has someone lost his goods? but not so completely, nor in such a manner. Has
he lost children? but never all at once, nor so many, nor such. Has he
fallen into disease? but not such a one, but either fevers, or mutilation,
or some other customary affliction. But that blow was something strange, and
clear only to the one who suffered it. For no word could present the
bitterness of those sores and the painfulness of the wounds, but it is
enough merely to name the one who worked it and his ungovernable wrath, to
show the magnitude of the blow. =63.481= And the sitting too was novel and
strange; for there is not, there is not any poor man who ever sat thus in
the open through the whole time, as that man endured, naked of clothing,
deprived of all shelter, sitting ulcerated upon the dunghill. Someone has
often had a wicked wife, but no one ever became so wicked as to attack her
husband in such a misfortune, and to whet a sword against his soul, and to
counsel such counsels. And that of the friends too was strange, and that of
the servants; and that of the famine again more novel, that though the table
lay beside him he did not taste it. Shall I speak also a fourth excess? I
mean the wealth before and the prosperity. For he who from the beginning
lived in poverty would have borne this more easily, as having practiced the
suffering; but he who was brought down from such prosperity, through being
untrained and unpracticed, is likely to endure a harsher sensation, and a
more bitter pain, and a greater confusion. It is possible to speak a fifth
also; what then is this? That each of other men, being conscious of many
wicked things in himself, knows the cause of what he suffers; and this is no
small thing toward consolation; but that man could not even reckon this,
that he was paying the penalty of transgressions and sins; which especially
threw his reasoning into tumult. For when he looked at his own life, and the
conscience shining above the sun, and the multitude of his achievements, he
knew that he was worthy of crowns and prizes and countless awards; but when
he looked at the body and the sores and all that had befallen, seeing
himself suffering harsher things than those who had dared the worst, he
could not even find the cause to name for which he suffered these things;
therefore taking refuge in the incomprehensible nature of God's providence
he said: As it seemed good to the Lord, so it has come to pass. And stopping
his wife's mouth with the arguments that were available, he also composed a
reasoning that greatly displayed his piety, saying thus: If we have received
good things from the Lord, shall we not bear the evil? Shall I speak also
another excess that especially crowns and proclaims that athlete, and shows
his soul high and touching the heavens? What then is this? The difference
of the time; for having lived before grace and before the law, he
philosophized such things. And this is no small thing, but even able to
weave countless crowns. For of the same achievements the same rewards are
not laid down, when one has accomplished these in the earlier times, and
another in the last, but much greater for the former. For it was not equal,
after Christ had come and shown so many things and exhorted and counseled to
philosophize, and before his coming and before law and before prophets to
display such things. Therefore even when he came he seeks a greater addition
of virtue, saying: Unless your righteousness abound more than that of the
Scribes and Pharisees, you shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. For
the more the teaching has become, the juster and greater measures of virtue
he has set. But this man, not having enjoyed teaching, not having met with
letters, nor books, not having seen others, not being able to run back to
the former time and consider those who had succeeded; for there was not yet
scripture, or history handing down what had happened; but on an unbeaten
road, on an unsailed sea, in so great a gloom of vice, he alone and first
then =63.482= cut this road of philosophy, being shown, with excess,
supreme in the chief of goods. For it is a very great thing even to
accomplish the
lesser parts of virtue, but much greater to be most extreme in the most
extreme of all; and that endurance is the most extreme of all, no one will
contradict. These things then the devil himself also knowing, said: Skin for
skin, and all that a man has he will give for his life; but send forth your
hand, and touch his flesh. Whence it is clear that this achievement is the
most extreme of all, and needs a certain vigorous and iron soul.

4. Have you seen him in the trials, as far as it was possible for you to
see? for not even the word itself was able to present the exactness. For
pain and tortures and the tyranny of despondency and the confusion arising
from so many snowstorms no power of speech can interpret to those who have
heard, as the experience of the things themselves can. Come then, let me
show you him again (for this too is no small thing, but needs a soul
exceedingly philosophic) displaying much philosophy in wealth and in
prosperity. What then was he when he was rich? A common harbor of all, a
common father of all, a common physician, or rather even greater than a
physician; hear him saying: I was eyes to the blind, and feet to the lame.
Do you see how greater than a physician, he became in place of nature to
those who were maimed? and what physicians were not able to correct by art,
these things he himself recovered by consolation, through his great
providence toward them becoming in place of their limbs. For as if healthy
and sound of foot and seeing, so were those who had been mutilated in these
limbs disposed, not perceiving the lameness nor the maiming because of this
man's great providence. Therefore he did not even say, I comforted the lame
and the blind, but, I was their foot and eye; and again, I was a father of
the weak. Nor here did he say, I comforted orphans, but, I was their father;
showing by this that he did not even let a perception of the orphanhood
arise, nor did he allow the terrible thing to appear; just as there he
removed the maiming by the abundance of providence for those who had
suffered such things, taking away even the perception of the terrible
things. And he did not heal mutilated bodies only, nor correct orphanhoods,
becoming to some in place of limbs, to others in place of parents, but he
also became a self-appointed judge; or rather even more than a judge; For a
cause, he says, which I knew not, I searched out, and I crushed the molars
of the unjust, and from the midst of their teeth I snatched the prey. This
is far more than a judge. For those who judge sit waiting for the wronged,
and after their petition, then the most approved of those who judge furnish
the alliance from themselves; as for the many, not even this; but this man
both surpassed the most approved, and overshot them by a great surplus. For
he did not wait for the wronged to come to him, nor after their petition did
he come with alliance, but himself going first he went about seeking the
wronged, and not even simply seeking, but with much wakefulness, with much
care. And this you will know clearly, when you have learned the force of the
expression; for he did not say, I sought, but, A cause which I knew not I
searched out; that is, I tracked, I busied myself inquiring, I continued
=63.483= setting everything in motion, so as to find whether somewhere
someone wronged might be hidden. Have you seen the sleepless soul? see also
the courage and the zeal: And I crushed the molars of the unjust. The biting
power itself, the domination I dissolved, he says, so that thereafter they
became useless even for another injustice. He therefore benefited both,
delivering the ones from suffering evil, and the others from doing evil, and
making them more temperate. Then the vigor and the endurance: And from the
midst of their teeth I snatched the prey. For I did not despair nor give up,
even if the thing had already been seized beforehand, but I even drew up
what had already been swallowed, displaying the providence of some excellent
and sober shepherd concerning his fellow-servants. And what of the humility?
for consider how great it was: If I also despised the cause of my manservant
or maidservant, when they contended with me. For what shall I do, if the
Lord should make a visitation of me? Did not they too come to be in the womb
as I came to be? Have you seen a crushed mind, examining the nature of men
with exactness and knowing what a slave is, what a free man is, this thing
that is bandied about by many? Casting out this inequality, then, from the
equal honor according to birth he introduces the account concerning
philosophy. And the wonderful thing about him is that in doing these things
he did not even think he was being humble, but fulfilling a debt. Therefore
he also set down a reasoning that persuades all men to think themselves no
more than their servants, even if they are masters ten thousand times. For
these
names, slave and free, are only bare names, empty of things; but slavery is
defined in sin, and freedom in righteousness. Was he then humble in this
way, but not also longed for and beloved? Yes, beloved; and consider the
excess here too. For just as in the terrible things he bore what was brought
upon him with all intensity, so also in prosperity he accomplished each
virtue with great abundance, not simply nor as it chanced, but arriving at
the very peak. And if my maidservants also often said: Who would give us to
be filled with his flesh? I being exceedingly kind. Here he narrates the mad
love of the servants, which they had toward him, having become ardent lovers
of him from the things he displayed toward them. For so, he says, they hung
upon me, so they clung, so they were nailed, so loving, as to desire even to
be filled with the flesh itself, and to swallow and eat it up through loving
fiercely and being kindled.

5. What would one say of the disdain of money? for this too was accomplished
by him with excess. For not only did he not desire what belonged to others,
which is what most suffer now, but not even his own, but he was alienated
even from these with all excess; therefore he also said: If I rejoiced
because much wealth had come to me, nor did I put my trust in a precious
stone. Therefore even when they were taken away, he bore the taking with
great ease, and when they were present he practiced abundant almsgiving,
=63.484= stretching out his right hand to all, and opening his house. For he
did not do what most suffer, being over-curious and inquiring into those who
receive, but, My door, he says, was open to every one that came. For the
weak, whatever need they ever had, were not disappointed; and a stranger
did not go
out my door with empty bosom. Have you seen the abundance? have you seen the
loving-kindness, the kindness, the humility? Do you wish to learn the
chastity also? I made a covenant, he says, with my eyes, not to think upon a
virgin belonging to another. What Christ coming afterward commanded, those
things that man accomplished through his deeds. Have you seen him rich, have
you seen him poor, have you seen him healthy, have you seen him sick? have
you seen his affairs running with the current? have you seen him stripped of
all? have you seen how he prepared himself toward children, toward servants,
toward the wronged, toward orphans? If I also had gone with jesters, he
says; that is, I did not even pursue these associations of buffoons. And
this too is no small sign of chastity. For he went through every virtue, and
being clothed with so many things he was disposed more exactly than those
who have nothing; for not so was he who had nothing rid of money, as that
man who had so much. For everywhere it is the mind that is crowned. And he
went up to the very summit of chastity, and accomplished every virtue with
the fitting exactness. Zeal for these things, beloved; imitate these
things, and taking up this image that has been drawn, fix it in your own
conscience; and if you are in despondency, take refuge in him, and if in
wealth, take the drug from here, so as neither to be overwhelmed by poverty,
nor to be puffed up by wealth; and if you lose children, you have the
consolation from here; for here you will find the excess both of the
misfortunes and of the endurance; and if you fall into disease, consider the
springs of worms, which that man's flesh boiled with, and you will bear all
things gently; and if a friend plot, bring the saint into the midst again,
and you will prevail over the passion; and if chance people abuse you,
consider what he suffered from his servants, and you will receive much
healing; and if some wicked suspicion encompass you, reckon what they said
about this man, that he had not yet paid a penalty worthy of his sins, and
how they reproached him, and you will prevail over this passion too. For
there is not, as I said at the beginning, a misfortune among men which this
man, firmer than any adamant, did not endure, having borne famine and
poverty and disease and loss of children and damage of so much money at a
stroke; and after that, that being plotted against by a wife, insulted by
friends, warred against by servants, through all he was shown firmer than
any rock, and these things before the law and grace. For we shall not even
have the slightest defense, when we who after law and grace, having enjoyed
so great a gift, bear less than this man who at the beginning and preludes
of human life displayed such philosophy. So that we may have both
consolation of painful things, and teaching of the best philosophy, =63.485=
having written these things thus let us depart, and let us emulate the
athlete, and let us imitate his wrestlings; so as also to attain the
good things to come, by the grace and loving =63.486= kindness of our Lord
Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father be glory, together
with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
