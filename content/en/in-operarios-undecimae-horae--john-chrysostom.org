* On the Workers of the Eleventh Hour
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of the same John Chrysostom, on those of the eleventh hour.
God fashioned man from two natures, I mean soul and body. And to each he
supplied what is fitting for healing: to the body, skilled physicians and
manifold preparations of medicines; to the soul, wise teachers and the helps
of the divine Scriptures. And great is the difference between the two things.
For Scripture is as much superior in art as the soul given from heaven is
superior to the earth-born body. He therefore who has suffered in the body
comes to the workshops of the physicians, showing the matter before the word,
that he may buy health which is uncertain. I have said uncertain, because
often even the physicians wish and are not able.
For they are not masters of nature. And the sick man struggles to flee the
disease, and the longed-for health does not come unless it has received the
Master's command. For if God does not nod his assent, the zeal of both is
without effect. But he who comes to the church, that he may heal the soul that
has been wounded in many ways by sins, does not miss his own purpose. He does
not invent money to win over these physicians. For they do not traffic in the
gifts of Christ. For they hear him saying, "Freely you received, freely give,"
and they furnish to each the healing without silver. They heal, then, and they
do not fail, because Christ himself who has the authority works with them. O
the paradoxical swiftness of those who are healed! O the unspeakable goodness
of him who heals! Someone comes here given up by ten thousand votes, already
seeing himself in the very depth of death; and having received the bath of
grace, he returns at once healthy and vigorous, renewed as a noble eagle,
having put off the former oldness of life. Another, after baptism, having
slipped again into the passions of the flesh and defiled the garment of grace,
and having exchanged the freedom of the soul for the slavery of sin, and
having become the plaything of unbridled pleasures, abstaining from no kind of
baseness, comes to the healing-place of the church, not daring, like the
publican, to look up to heaven, having his own conscience as accuser of the
things done, already looking ahead to the fearful assembly before the Judge of
all, the swiftest opening of the books, the sins of each trumpeted forth, the
fierce and unquenchable fire of Gehenna, the all-devouring and endless worm,
the unsmiling and much-groaning Tartarus, the fearful gnashing of teeth, the
ceaseless wailing of those being punished, the inconsolable torment of the
outer darkness.
When one comes here with all these things, thinking of them and fearing and
trembling, at once Christ the Master who loves mankind meets him, as the
father met the son who had consumed the paternal substance, and with words of
compassion and kindness he calms the surge of the one being storm-tossed.
Saying what? "Man, are you downcast? Why do you cast down your reasoning as if
you were without help? Why, having fallen, do you remain in the fall,
shipwrecked and sick with impenitence, being weak? 'Does he who falls not
rise? Or he who turns away not return?' You have worked many evils and
committed innumerable sins. But the multitude of your trespasses does not
overcome the art of the physician. I am, he says, exceedingly loving toward
men. For I have been begotten by nature good from the Good. If, having sinned,
you repent, I am not angry, being won over by the return. If you seek
forgiveness of offenses, I do not proceed against you, awaiting your good
acknowledgment. I do not search out the things done, but I cover them with a
hand of amnesty. Only show genuine repentance, and receive the rich enjoyment
of my compassions. Only do not stand idle in the marketplace of evils the
whole day of your life; but come, having repented of your works, into the
vineyard of my commandments, and you shall receive from me the wage of
righteousness." For the discourse hastens to repay the loan which it formerly
wrote down with the reed of the tongue, when the Savior said to the disciples:
"The kingdom of heaven is like a man, a householder, who went out early in the
morning to hire laborers into his vineyard. And having agreed with them for a
denarius the day, he sent them into his vineyard. And going out about the
third hour he saw others standing idle in the marketplace, and to those he
said, Go you also into the vineyard, and whatever is just I will give you. And
going out about the sixth and the ninth hour he did likewise. And about the
eleventh hour going out he found others standing idle, and he says to them,
Why have you stood here idle the whole day? They say, Because no one hired us.
He says to them, Go work in the vineyard, and whatever is just you shall
receive. And when evening had come the lord of the vineyard said to his
steward, Call the laborers and give them the wage." This then is the Savior's
parable. But we shall search the things said with understanding, that we may
find the unconfused treasure of the faith, and learn who is the householder of
this, and what is the vineyard and what the wage, and what the hours and the
day, and who the laborers and who the steward. The house then is the world.
And the householder is Christ himself who made the world and all things in it.
His vineyard is laws, commandments, ordinances, judgments. "Going out early in
the morning," at the beginning of the world, he hired into his vineyard Abel
the pious, Enoch the well-pleasing, Noah the righteous, and all who lived
piously until the flood. "Having agreed with them for a denarius the day." The
denarius is the kingdom of heaven, which has the royal characters of Father
and Son and Holy Spirit. And he calls day the life of each in this world.
"Going out about the third hour" he hired Abraham the most faithful, Lot the
hospitable, Isaac the self-controlled, Jacob the stout-souled, the twelve
patriarchs, and all who were godly until Moses. "Going out about the sixth and
ninth hour" he sent into the vineyard Moses, Aaron, Joshua the son of Nun,
Samuel, David, the rest of the prophets, and all who were well-pleasing until
the economy.
"And going out about the eleventh hour" and sojourning in the world with flesh
and saying, "I came forth from the Father and have come into the world," he
saw all the Gentiles "standing idle." He calls the eleventh hour the end of
this age and of the times, concerning which John says, "Little children, it is
the last hour." About this hour "he saw them," as I said, "standing idle, and
he says to them, Why have you stood here idle the whole day? And they say,
Because no one hired us": not fathers, not patriarchs, not Moses the lawgiver,
not Isaiah the theologian, not Jeremiah the compassionate, none of the rest of
the prophets hired us, no one pointed out to us the vineyard of your
ordinances. O the divine loving-kindness and goodness! For those standing idle
in the marketplace about the third and the sixth hour and the ninth he does
not charge: "Why have you stood idle?" Why? Because having fathers,
patriarchs, prophets, guides, teachers toward the working of the Master's
commandments, they were not able to say, "Because no one hired us."
But he charges the Gentiles, saying, "Why have you stood idle the whole day?",
that, receiving their most ready and fitting apology as work, he may make
manifest the self-chosen and genuine justification of their obedience. And
what does he say? "Go you also into the vineyard, and whatever is just you
shall receive. And when evening had come," the consummation of the world, "the
lord of the vineyard says to his steward..." Who is the steward? The
Paraclete, the Holy Spirit, who distributes the Master's gifts. Do you have
the unconfused treasure of the faith? Guard it. Do not be led astray as many
are, nor ever confess the steward as householder. "The lord of the vineyard
says to his steward, Call the laborers and give them the wage." O the wonder!
Through himself he agreed upon the wage, through himself he sent the laborers
into the vineyard, and he commands the steward to give the wage to the
laborers, that he may both make manifest his own preeminence and show the
unconfused order of the Spirit. "Give them the wage," that which I agreed
upon, "beginning from the last unto the first," that they may not even in the
very giving, as last, be about to be downcast and to waver in their
reasonings.
Will he then give even to us who are last the wage agreed upon with the first?
"And when the last came they received a denarius each. And when the first
came, they thought that they would receive more; and they also received a
denarius each, and they murmured against the householder." Not against the
steward. For they knew exactly that the steward has no authority to make
either addition or diminution to the wage that was agreed upon. "They murmured
against the householder: These last have worked one hour, and you have made
them equal to us who have borne the burden of the day and the heat." They call
the burden of the day the heaviest conduct in the law, and the heat the legal
austerity. "And he says to one of those murmuring, Friend, I do you no wrong"
(I call you friend because of the communion of the body). "Did you not agree
with me for a denarius? Take what is yours and go. But I wish to give to this
one as also to you." To you I pay because I owe you a wage. But to this one I
give: for he is saved by grace and not by wage. He goes out even now through
the prophetic and apostolic and evangelical preachings, hiring all into the
spiritual vineyard. "He goes out early in the morning," in the age of infancy.
"He goes out about the third hour," in the prime of each one's youth. "He goes
out about the sixth and ninth hour," in the middle and the decline of age. "He
goes out about the eleventh hour," in old age itself and at the end of each
one's life. He exhorts you to work even the one hour in the vineyard, that he
may grant you an eternal kingdom and much rest.
Therefore take up zeal and eagerness, even if late, so as to cast off your
aged, much-patched and filthy and heaviest tunic of sins through baptism or
through repentance, and as an eagle renewed in youth, snatch the Master's
exhortation before you are snatched by angels into Hades. With tears and
groanings and alms bend the Judge of all toward pity, who cries and says, "If
you turn and groan, you shall be saved." I do not delay, I do not linger, I do
not put it off. "While you are still speaking I will say, Behold, I am
present." To him be the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
