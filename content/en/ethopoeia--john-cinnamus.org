Ethopoeia of the imperial grammarian kyr John Cinnamus: What words would
a painter have spoken, painting Apollo on a laurel tablet, and the
tablet not yielding?
O art and hands and colors. What a portent is this that I see: how
greatly has the tablet made war against the painting. The tablet is from
laurel, the laurel from a maiden. And in her, even after the human
state, the hatred sits within; and having changed her nature she did not
change her manner with it. Against a god she rages deathlessly, and a
prophetic plant does not receive a prophetic god. As upon a bridal
chamber I seat Apollo on the tablet, and I become bridesman of a god
from colors and of a maiden from a plant, and as wedding-gifts I give
the colors to the maiden, and from laurel I weave the bridal crown. I
kindle for this bridal chamber also light, having taken it from nearby;
and as from a prophetic god the light, which the Pythian kindled for
many who inquired. But she thrusts the lover away. And she does not heed
the dye and does not turn toward the colors. Daphne is daughter of Ladon
the river, descendant of waters, a child from Earth, gleaming in beauty
as Earth so gleams in beauty, and from a sweet stream drawing down also
a sweet bloom of youth. Was it perhaps from there, from her father, that
she learned not to receive the colors? From there does she learn the
turning-away toward the painter? From nearby, from a river, does she
draw the things of the teaching?
I hear that the moon, when she has her conjunction with the star-ruling
light-bringer, the giant, is emptied of her lights and as it were dies.
I have Daphne too, beautiful as the moon, because Daphne's race also
draws near to the earth, as the moon does in another, newer manner.
Shall I then contrive the conjunction of the sun, of Apollo? But he
rather is eclipsed at the coming-together, and is wholly diminished, and
does not dare the painting. Shall I say this portent is of Daphne's
mind, or of the Pythian's turning-away concerning the maiden? He who is
being painted does not take form; she does not receive the dyes. So then
she refers the uncoloredness to the fear from the earth, and against
love she alone of all things boasts. I once painted Theias too on wood
from myrrh, and she received the father, as of old in the womb she
received Adonis. And I saw the art of painting imitating the things of
nature, and as there the infant little by little, so here too what was
taking form was being set upright and molded bit by bit. O Zeus and
gods, but Daphne has kept her madness even as far as colors, and has
chambered her wrath even as far as the lifeless. And yet not unlike
drunkennesses fell upon Theias and upon Apollo: the one was drunk with
love, the other with wine, the ally of the body. But Daphne does not nod
toward the mixing of the colors, because neither formerly did she toward
that with Apollo. She did not know that even in heaven Eros bent the
wing, and of a sudden fashioned the highest of the gods as a wing, and
taught him to make the music of swans, and with the drug of love dyed
his heart within and whitened him without as a swan. Eros kindled the
fire, and Zeus was fired as gold, and as a hot lover he poured himself
around the beloved. He showed Zeus also a token of beauty toward a
maiden; and Zeus bellowed as a bull, so that not only from a bull might
honey be crafted, but conversely, from Zeus and from honey a bull. And
the child shook the ox-goad at the father, and mocked the bull, Zeus,
into his own fashioning. And from Zeus the plowman a harvest of the
season so sweet sprang up. She did not know that over the sea he stirred
the fire, and raised a sweet love upon waters of salty nature, and
sowing upon the wet he was not ashamed of the impossible. He reached
even against the earth, sending forth the dart, and all are captives of
love, bound of affection. And he devised moist embracings upon the dry.
He squeezed out also upon the hearts of mortals desire as a
grape-cluster, and one tastes of this and is set on fire in the inward
parts, and feeds a flame and is burned up in the soul. And he makes the
eyes wholly new as mixing-bowls of fire, and upon the eyelids he opened
channels of flame. He hurled against the neighboring air, and if some
dog couples near a simple maiden, he borrows even the flame of the fire,
and promises so swift a birth to the lover. Eros also shot the eyes of
Loxias, looked upon the accustomed thing, and drove him mad for the
beauty of a great maiden. Beautiful then is the maiden, golden in
appearance, delightful in bloom, simple in form. Beautiful too is
Apollo. And being an archer this one wronged another archer, Eros, and
yielded to his darts. The Pythian saw the maiden, felt pain, pursued,
hastened, perhaps as if from the tripod about to bear ordinances most
sweetly. A god pursued a human; and a human was the beloved of a god.
The prophetic god did not know the failure. For Eros clouded his
prophetic art for him; he did not know the vain course. For desire
flowing from the inward parts checked the prophetic spring, and blocked
the veins of seeing. He threatened the flight also with bonds, which
many times he had forged in plenty from the fire of love. The race of
both was unequal, and the course unlike. And in what the maiden fell
short by race, in this, in speed, she was first. He conquered the maiden
by nature, she the lover by speed. Earth saw the maiden's force, and as
she ran away, fled, ran, and pressed on concerning virginity, Earth
trenched around her flanks, and opened wide her mouth, and having of old
unfolded her from the belly she received her back again, as often
water-going fishes in the sea do their young, and hid the beloved from
the Delian. And she was being dug open for the maiden's virginity as for
a treasure, thus suspecting a great god as thief of the beauty. And she
was being levered as with iron by the maiden's unyieldingness toward
Eros. And Earth in her stead gave back to Apollo a plant of the same
name, fair to the sight, august in blossom, golden in beauty,
well-colored to the view, still breathing fear and still imagining
brightness by the motion from the winds. But this does not receive
Apollo even in colors. No longer take courage, seers, in the laurel
crown, no longer trust the branches of the laurel; if Daphne does not
receive Apollo being painted, neither altogether does she receive him
prophesying.
