OF THE SAME, ON THE RESURRECTION OF THE DEAD
To every dogma and argument that holds to the truth in these matters some
falsehood grows beside it; and it grows beside it not as starting from some
underlying principle according to nature or from the cause according to what
each thing is, but as being pursued by those who have honored the lawless
sowing for the corruption of the truth.
This one may find first from those who of old have been occupied with cares
about these things and from the difference that arose of those men toward
both their own elders and their contemporaries, and not least from the very
disturbance of those who are turning about in the midst. For such men have
left none of the true things unslandered, not the being of God, not the
knowledge, not the activity, not the things that follow these in sequence and
that sketch out for us the account of piety; but some altogether and once for
all despair of the truth about these things, some twist it toward what seems
good to themselves, and some even make it their practice to be at a loss
about the things that are evident.
Whence I think those who labor about these things need a twofold set of
arguments, some on behalf of the truth, and some concerning the truth; and
those on behalf of the truth toward those who disbelieve or those who doubt,
and those concerning the truth toward those who are well-disposed and receive
the truth with goodwill. For which reasons those who wish to examine these
things must look to the need that is set forth at each time and measure the
arguments by this and adapt the order about these things toward what is
required and not, by seeming to keep the same beginning always, neglect what
is fitting and the place that belongs to each.
For as toward demonstration and the natural sequence, the arguments
concerning it always stand first before those on behalf of it, but as toward
what is more useful, conversely those on behalf of it before those concerning
it. For neither would a farmer be able fittingly to cast the seeds into the
earth without first taking out the things of the wild wood and those that
ruin the cultivated seeds that are being sown, nor a physician to put any of
the healthy drugs into a body that needs healing without first cleaning out
the vice that is in it or checking what is flowing in; nor indeed would one
who wishes to teach the truth, speaking concerning the truth, be able to
persuade anyone while some false opinion lurks in the mind of the hearers and
stands against the arguments.
Therefore looking toward what is more useful we too sometimes set the
arguments on behalf of the truth before those concerning the truth; and to do
in the same way also now upon the arguments of the resurrection does not
appear useless to those who look to what is required. For even in these we
find some disbelieving altogether, some doubting, and of those at least who
have accepted the first hypotheses some


test that they suffer these things though they have not even any occasion of
unbelief from the facts nor finding a reasonable cause to name on account of
which they disbelieve or are at a loss.
Let us look at it thus. Every unbelief that does not arise in some readily
and according to some unjudged opinion but with some strong cause and the
security according to the truth then keeps the reasonable account, when the
very thing about which they disbelieve seems to be unbelievable; for to
disbelieve things that are not unbelievable is the work of men who do not use
a healthy judgment about the truth.
Therefore those too who disbelieve or are at a loss about the resurrection
must not, toward what seems good to themselves without judgment and what is
pleasing to the unrestrained, put forth their opinion about this, but either
attach the generation of men to no cause at all (which is indeed very easy to
refute) or, assigning the cause of the things that are to God, look toward
the hypothesis of this dogma and through this show that the resurrection has
the credible from nowhere.
And they will do this if they are able to show that it is either impossible
for God or unwanted by him to unite again the bodies that have been made dead
or even wholly dissolved and to gather them together toward the constitution
of the same men. But if they cannot do this, let them cease from this godless
unbelief and from blaspheming what is not lawful; for that they speak truly
neither in saying the impossible nor the unwanted will become clear from what
will be said.
The impossible is known as truly such for someone either from not knowing
what will come to be or from not having sufficient power toward doing well
what has been known. For he who is ignorant of something of the things that
ought to come to be would be able neither to attempt nor to do at all what he
does not know, and he who knows well what is to be done and from where it
would come to be and how, but either has no power at all toward doing what is
known or does not have sufficient power, would not attempt it in the first
place, if he were of sound mind and considered his own power, and having
attempted it without consideration he would not complete what seemed good.
But it is not possible that God is ignorant of the nature of the bodies that
will be raised, both as a whole in part and in member, nor indeed where each
of the things that are being dissolved goes and what part of the element has
received what has been dissolved and has gone toward its kindred, even if
among men what has been united again fittingly to the whole seems to be
wholly indistinguishable. For he to whom, before the proper constitution of
each, neither the nature of the elements that would come to be, from which
the bodies of men, nor the parts of these, from which it was going to take
what seemed good toward the constitution of the human body, was unknown, it
is quite clear that neither after the whole has been dissolved will it be
unknown where each of the things he has taken toward the completion of each
has gone.
For as much as according to the now prevailing among us


order of things and the judgment upon others, it is greater to know
beforehand the things that have not come to be; but as toward the worth of
God and his wisdom, both are according to nature and easy equally, to know
also the things that have been dissolved as to know beforehand the things
that have not come to be.
And indeed that the power is sufficient toward the resurrection of the
bodies, the generation of these very things shows. For if he made the bodies
of men and their beginnings when they were not according to the first
constitution, he will also raise them when they have been dissolved in
whatever way they happen, with equal ease; for this too is equally possible
for him.
And there is no harm to the argument, even if some suppose the first
beginnings from matter, even if the bodies of men from the elements as first,
even if from seeds. For of the power it is both to form the substance that is
among them held to be shapeless and to order the formless and unordered with
many and various forms and to gather the parts of the elements into one and
to divide the seed that is one and simple into many and to articulate the
unarticulated and to give life to what is not living, of the same it is also
to unite what has been dissolved and to raise what is lying down and to make
the dead living again and to change the corruptible into incorruption.
And it would belong to the same one and to the same power and wisdom also to
distinguish from there what has been broken up into a multitude of animals of
all sorts, as many as are accustomed to run upon such bodies and to gather
the satiety from these, and to unite it again to its own parts and members,
whether it has gone into one animal from those, or into many, or from there
into others, or having been dissolved together with those very ones has been
carried to the first beginnings according to the natural analysis into these;
which indeed has seemed to trouble some even of those admired for wisdom most
of all, who have somehow held the difficulties brought forward by the many to
be strong.
These men say that many bodies of those who die hard in shipwrecks or rivers
have become food for fishes, and many of those who die in wars or from some
other harsher cause and circumstance of affairs, lacking burial, lie as prey
for the animals that happen upon them.
Of the bodies then that are thus consumed and of the parts and members that
complete these, being broken up into a great multitude of animals and being
united through the food to the bodies of those that are nourished, first they
say the distinction of these is impossible, and besides this the second is
more aporetic.
For of the animals that have fed on the bodies of men, as many as are
suitable for food for men, going through the belly of these and being united
to the bodies of those who have partaken, there is every necessity that the
parts of men, as many as have become food for the animals that have partaken,
pass over to


other bodies of men, the animals that were fed in between conveying the food
from which they were fed to those men of whom they became food.
Then they add in tragic fashion the child-eatings dared in famines and
insanities and the children eaten by those who begot them according to the
plotting of enemies and that Median table and the tragic dinners of Thyestes
and they string on besides such misfortunes as have been newly worked among
Greeks and barbarians and from these they construct, as they think, the
resurrection as impossible, as the same parts not being able to be raised
together with both other and other bodies, but either the bodies of the
former not being able to be constituted, the parts that complete these having
passed over to others, or these having been given back to the former the
bodies of the later will be in want.
But to me such men seem first to be ignorant of the power and wisdom of him
who crafted and administers this all, who has fitted to each animal by nature
and kind the food that is fitting and corresponding and has not thought it
right that every nature go toward union or blending with every body nor is at
a loss toward distinction of the things that have been united, but both
permits to the nature according to each of the things that have come to be to
do or suffer what it is naturally fitted for and prevents another and grants
or turns everything that he wishes and toward what he wishes, and besides
what has been said not even to have considered the power and nature of each
of the things that nourish or are nourished.
For they would have known that not everything which one takes in by yielding
of the necessity from without becomes fitting food for the animal; but some
things are naturally fitted to be destroyed as soon as they meet the
enclosing parts of the belly, being vomited and passing through or being
dispersed in another way, so as not even for a little to undergo the first
and according-to-nature digestion, much less the blending into what is
nourished, just as neither does everything that has been digested and has
received the first change altogether approach the members that are nourished,
some things being separated according to the belly itself from the nutritive
power, and some being distinguished according to the second change and the
digestion that comes to be in the liver and passing over toward something
else which has gone out from the power of nourishing, and not even all of the
change that comes to be in the liver going into food for men, but being
distinguished into the residues that it is naturally fitted for, and the food
that has been left behind in the nourished parts and members themselves
sometimes changing toward something else according to the prevalence of what
is in excess or overflowing and accustomed somehow to destroy or turn toward
itself what has come near.
There being then much natural difference in all the animals and even the food
according to nature for each kind of animals also


changing along with the body that is nourished, and a threefold cleansing and
distinction coming to be according to the food of each animal, everything as
much as is alien to the food of the animal as not being able to be blended
must altogether be destroyed and pass through as it is naturally fitted or
change toward something else, and the power of the nourishing body must be
corresponding and according to nature to the powers of the animal that is
nourished, and this, having come through the criteria through which it is
naturally fitted and having been cleansed exactly by the natural cleansings,
must become a most unmixed addition into substance;
which indeed one speaking truly of the facts would name food alone as throwing
off everything as much as is alien and harmful to the constitution of the
animal that is nourished and that great bulk that has been brought in toward
the filling of the belly and the service of the appetite.
But this one would not dispute is united to the body that is nourished, being
interwoven together and molded around all its parts and members; but that
which is otherwise and against nature is destroyed quickly, if it mixes with
a stronger power, and with ease destroys that which has been overpowered and
is turned into wretched humors and poisonous qualities as bringing nothing
proper or friendly to the body that is nourished.
And of this the greatest proof is that from these there follows for many of
the animals that are nourished pain or danger or death, if by a more vehement
appetite it has drawn in together something poisonous and against nature
mixed with the food; which indeed would be altogether destructive of the body
that is nourished, if at least the things that are nourished are nourished by
the proper and according-to-nature things, and are destroyed by the
opposites.
If then with the difference of the animals that differ by nature the food
according to nature has been divided together, and of this itself neither
everything which the animal may take in nor the chance thing from this
receives the blending toward the body that is nourished, but only that which
has been cleansed through all digestion and has changed unmixedly toward the
union of the body of such a quality and is well-fitted to the parts that are
nourished, it is quite clear that none of the things against nature would
ever be united to these for which it is not fitting and corresponding food,
but either it passes through according to the belly itself before it
generates some other raw and corrupted humor, or having stood together for
longer it begets a passion or a disease hard to heal, destroying together
also the food according to nature or even the flesh itself that needs the
food.
But even if it is ever driven out by some drugs or better foods or having
been conquered by the natural powers, it has flowed out with not a little
harm as bringing nothing peaceful to the things according to nature through
its being unblendable toward nature.
And in general even if one should grant that the food that enters from these


(let this be said more customarily), though it is against nature, is
distinguished and changes into some one of the things that moisten or dry or
heat or cool, not even so will anything useful come to be for them from what
has been granted, the bodies that are raised being constituted again from
their own parts, and none of the things mentioned being a part or holding the
relation or order as of a part, nor indeed remaining always with the parts of
the body that are nourished or being raised together with those that are
raised, since blood, phlegm, bile, breath no longer contribute anything
toward living. For neither of the things the bodies that are nourished once
needed will they need then also, the need from the things from which they
were nourished having been taken away together with the want and corruption
of those that are nourished.
Then if one should suppose that the change from such food even reaches as far
as flesh, not even so will there be some necessity that the flesh newly
changed from food of this sort, having approached the body of some other man,
again count as a part toward his completion, because neither does the flesh
that takes it on always keep what it has taken on, nor is this that has been
united lasting and remaining where it was added, but it also receives much
change toward the other side, being at one time dispersed by toils or cares,
at another being wasted together with griefs or labors or diseases, and by
the ill-blendings that come on from burning or chilling, the fat-masses that
in remaining what they are receive the food not changing together with flesh
and fat.
And such passions having come to be upon the flesh, one would find still much
more the flesh that is nourished from things not proper suffering these, now
going forward into bulk and being fattened from what it has taken on, then
again spitting it out in whatever way it happens and being diminished either
by one of the things mentioned before or by more; and that only remains with
the parts which is naturally fitted to bind or cover or warm, that which has
been selected by nature and has grown upon these with which it completed the
life according to nature and the toils in the life.
But (for neither when the things just examined are judged as they ought nor
when the things exercised upon those are accepted by concession is it
possible to show what is said by them true) the bodies of men would never be
blended with those that are of the same nature, even if they are ever
deceived in perception by ignorance having partaken of such a body through
some other, even if from want or madness they are defiled with a body of like
form; unless some beasts in human form have escaped our notice or having a
mixed nature from men and beasts, such as the bolder of the poets are
accustomed to fashion.
And what need is there to speak about the bodies that have been allotted to
no animal for food and have been given only burial into earth for the honor
of the nature, when indeed the maker has not allotted even any other of the
animals to those of the same


kind for food, even if in some others of different kinds it becomes food
according to nature?
If then they are able to show that the fleshes of men have been allotted to
men for eating, nothing will prevent the eatings of one another from being
according to nature just as some other of the things granted to nature and
those who dare to say such things from reveling in the bodies of their
dearest as more proper or even from feasting those most well-disposed to them
with these very things.
But if this is not even holy to say, and for men to partake of the fleshes of
men is something most hateful and wholly polluted and more accursed than
every lawless and against-nature eating or act, and what is against nature
would never go into food for the parts and members that need this, and what
does not go into food would not be united to these which it is not even
naturally fitted to nourish, neither would the bodies of men ever be blended
with like bodies, for which it is against nature for food, even if they often
go through the belly of these according to some most bitter misfortune; but
departing from the nutritive power and being scattered toward those things
again from which they had the first constitution, they are united to these
for as much time as each happens, and having been distinguished from there
again by the wisdom and power of him who compounded every nature of an animal
with its proper powers each is united fittingly to each, whether it is burned
with fire, or rots in water, or is consumed by beasts or the animals that
happen upon it, or having been cut out from the whole body is dissolved
before the other parts; and having been united again to one another they have
the same place toward the harmony and constitution of the same body and the
resurrection and life of what has been made dead or even wholly dissolved.
It is not timely then to lengthen these things further; for it has the
judgment agreed by those at least who are not mixed-beasts.
And there being many of the things more useful toward the examination set
forth, I now decline those who take refuge in the works of men and the
craftsmen of these, men, who are unable to make new the works that have been
shattered or aged by time or even otherwise destroyed, then from a likeness
to potters and carpenters trying to show that God too would neither wish nor
having wished be able to raise a body that has been made dead or even
dissolved, and not reckoning that through these they insult God equally with
the worst, making equal the powers of things that stand altogether apart, or
rather even the substances of those who use these and the things made by art
to the natural things.
To be in earnest about these things is not without blame; for it is truly
foolish to speak against the superficial and vain. Far more reputable and


of all most true is to say that the impossible among men is possible with
God. And if through these very things as reputable and through all the things
examined a little before the argument shows it possible, it is quite clear
that it is not impossible. But neither is it unwanted.
The unwanted is unwanted either as unjust to him or as unworthy. And again
the unjust is observed either about the one who will be raised himself or
about some other besides him. But that none of those outside and of those
numbered among the things that are is wronged is quite clear.
For neither would the intelligible natures be wronged from the resurrection
of men; for neither is there some hindrance to these toward being, nor harm,
nor insult, the resurrection of men; nor indeed the nature of the irrational
things nor of the soulless; for they will not even be after the resurrection,
and about what is not there is nothing unjust.
And if one should suppose them to be through all, these would not be wronged
when the human bodies have been renewed; for if now, yielding to the nature
of men and having been brought under yoke and slavery of every sort while
these are in want of their use, they are not wronged at all, much more, when
they have become incorruptible and without want and no longer needing their
use, and having been freed from all slavery, they will not be wronged.
For not even, if they had a share of voice, would they accuse the craftsman
as being lessened contrary to the just than men, because they have not
obtained the same resurrection as these. For to those whose nature is not
equal, the just one does not measure out an equal end either. And apart from
these, among those for whom there is no judgment of the just, there is not
even a complaint of injustice.
Nor indeed is it possible to say that some injustice is observed about the
man who is raised himself. For this one is from soul and body, and he
receives the injustice neither into soul nor into body. For no one of sound
mind will say that the soul is wronged; for he will escape notice throwing
out together with this also the present life; for if now dwelling in a
corruptible and passible body it has not been wronged at all, much more
living together with an incorruptible and impassible it will not be wronged.
But neither is the body wronged in anything; for if now a corruptible being
together with an incorruptible will be wronged.
Nor indeed would one say that it is an unworthy work of God to raise the
dissolved body and gather it together; for if the worse is not unworthy, that
is to make a corruptible and passible body, much more the better is not
unworthy, which is incorruptible and impassible.
If through the things first according to nature and those that follow these
each of the things examined has been shown, it is quite clear that the
resurrection of the dissolved bodies is both possible and wanted and a work
worthy of him who crafted it; for through these was shown to be false the


opposite and the unreason of those who disbelieve.
For what need is there to speak about the conversion of each of these toward
each and the joining toward one another, if one must even say joining as of
things separated by some otherness, and not also say that the possible is
wanted and that what is wanted by God is altogether possible and according to
the worth of him who wanted it?
And that the argument concerning the truth is one thing, and that on behalf
of the truth another, has been said through what preceded, moderately, in
what each differs and when and toward whom it has what is useful; and perhaps
nothing prevents, both for the common security and for the joining of the
things said toward the things that remain, from making the beginning again
from these very things and those that belong to them. And it belonged to the
one to have the first place according to nature, and to the other to escort
the first and to make a way and to drive off beforehand everything as much as
is in the way and uphill.
For the argument concerning the truth, being necessary to all men toward
security and salvation, stands first both by nature and by order and by need;
by nature, as furnishing the knowledge of the things; by order, as existing
in these and together with these of which it becomes the reporter; by need,
as becoming a sponsor of security and salvation for those who know.
And that on behalf of the truth is lesser by nature and by power, for to
refute the false is less than to confirm the truth; and second in order, for
it has its force against those who hold false opinions; and false opinion
grew on from after-sowing and side-corruption; but even with these things
being so it is often set first and sometimes becomes more useful as taking
away and cleaning out beforehand the unbelief that troubles some and the
doubt or false opinion for those who are just now approaching.
And each is referred to one end; for both he who refutes the false and he who
confirms the truth have their reference toward piety; yet they are not once
for all one, but the one is necessary, as I said, to all who believe and who
care for the truth and their own salvation, and the other is at times and for
some and toward some more useful.
And let these things have been said by us in summary toward a reminder of
what has already been spoken; but one must go on to what is set forth, and
one must show the argument concerning the resurrection true both from the
cause itself, according to which and through which the first man came to be
and those after him, even if they have not come to be in the like way, and
from the common nature of all men as men, and still from the judgment of the
maker upon these, according to as much time as each lived and according to
what laws he conducted himself, which no one would dispute is just.
The argument from the cause is, if we consider whether


man has come to be simply and in vain or for the sake of something; and if
for the sake of something, whether upon his living and remaining when he has
come to be according to the nature in which he came to be or through need of
someone; and if according to need, either that of the maker himself or of
some other of those that belong to him and have been thought worthy of more
care.
Which things indeed looking at more commonly we find that everyone of sound
mind and moved toward doing something by rational judgment does none of the
things he works according to purpose in vain, but either for the sake of his
own use or through need of some other of those he has cared for or through
the thing that is coming to be itself, being moved by some natural pull and
affection toward its own generation; as for example (for let it be said
through some image, in order that what is set forth may become clear) a man
makes a house through his own need, and he makes for oxen and camels or the
other animals of which he is in want the shelter that fits each of these not
for the sake of his own use according to what appears, but according to the
end through this, and according to the proximate through the care of these
whom he has cared for; and he also begets children neither through his own
need nor through some other of the things that belong to him, but upon the
being and remaining as far as possible of those begotten by him, consoling
his own end by the succession of children and descendants and thinking by
this to immortalize the mortal.
But these things by these; God however would neither have made man in vain;
for he is wise, and no work of wisdom is vain; nor through his own need; for
he is in want of nothing at all, and for him who needs nothing at all none of
the things that have come to be by him would contribute toward his own need.
But neither did he make man through some of the works that have come to be by
him. For none of those that use reason and judgment, neither of the greater
nor of the lesser, has come to be or comes to be toward the need of another,
but through the life and remaining of the things that have come to be
themselves.
For neither does the argument find some need a cause of the generation of
men, the immortals being without want and needing no contribution at all from
men toward being, and the irrational things being ruled according to nature
and filling up for men the needs toward what each is naturally fitted for but
not being naturally fitted to use these themselves; for it was not nor is it
lawful to bring the ruling and leading down into use for the lesser or to
subject the rational to the irrational, who are unsuited toward ruling.
Therefore if man has come to be neither without cause and in vain (for none
of the things that have come to be by God is vain, at least according to the
judgment of him who made it) nor for the sake of need of the maker himself or
of some other of the things made that have come to be by God, it is quite
clear that according to the first and more common account God made man for
himself and the goodness and wisdom observed upon all the crafting, and
according to the account more proximate to the things that have come to be
through the life of the things that have come to be themselves, not


kindled for a little then wholly quenched.
For to creeping things, I think, and winged things and swimming things, or
even to speak more commonly to all the irrational things, God assigned such
a life, but to those who bear the maker as an image in themselves and who
carry mind along with them and have been allotted a share of rational
judgment the maker allotted remaining forever, in order that knowing their
own maker and his power and wisdom and following law and justice they may
spend the ages together without toil with those things by which they
confirmed the life that went before even though they were in corruptible and
earthly bodies.
For as many things as have come to be for the sake of something else, when
those things for whose sake they have come to be have ceased, the things that
have come to be themselves will reasonably also cease from being and would
not remain in vain, as there would be no place of the vain in the things that
have come to be by God; but the things that have come to be through being
itself and living as it is naturally fitted, as the cause itself has been
taken together with the nature and is observed according to being alone,
would never receive any cause that wholly destroys being.
And this being always observed in being, the animal that has come to be must
altogether be saved, both working and suffering what it is naturally fitted
for, each of these from which it has come to be contributing the things from
itself, and the soul both being and remaining evenly in the nature in which
it came to be and laboring through what it is naturally fitted for (and it is
naturally fitted to oversee the impulses of the body and always to judge and
measure what falls upon it by the fitting criteria and measures), and the
body being moved according to nature toward what it is naturally fitted for
and receiving the changes allotted to it, and after the others that are
according to ages or according to form or size the resurrection.
For the resurrection is a certain form of change and the last of all, and the
change of those still remaining at that time toward the better.
And upon these things having taken courage no less than upon the things that
have already come to be and looking at our own nature, we both are content
with the life with want and corruption as belonging to the present life and
we hope firmly for remaining in incorruption; which we do not fashion from
men in vain, feeding ourselves with false hopes, but we have trusted a most
unerring pledge, the judgment of him who crafted us, according to which he
made man from an immortal soul and a body and constructed mind together with
him and an inborn law for the salvation and keeping of the things given by
him, belonging to a thoughtful life and a rational living, knowing well that
he would not have constructed such an animal and adorned it with all the
things toward remaining, if he did not wish what has come to be to remain.
If then the craftsman of this all made man upon sharing in a thoughtful life
and, when he has come to be, a spectator of his magnificence and of the


wisdom in all things, to remain always with the contemplation of these
according to that one's judgment and according to the nature he has obtained,
the cause of generation confirms remaining forever, and remaining confirms
the resurrection, without which man would not remain. And from what has been
said it is quite clear that the resurrection is shown plainly by the cause of
generation and by the judgment of the maker.
And such being the cause according to which man has been brought into this
world, it would be consistent to examine the argument that follows these
according to nature or according to sequence; and according to the
examination there follows the cause of generation the nature of the men who
have been generated, and the nature of those who have come to be the just
judgment of the maker upon these, and to all these the end of the life. And
the things set first having been examined by us one must next consider the
nature of men.
The demonstration of the dogmas of the truth or of the things put forward in
any way at all for examination, bringing unerring trust upon what is said,
does not have its beginning from somewhere outside nor from the things that
seem or have seemed good to some, but from the common and natural conception
or from the sequence of the second things toward the first.
For either it is about the first dogmas and needs only a reminder that stirs
up the natural conception, or about the things that follow the first
according to nature and the natural sequence and needs the order upon these,
showing what follows the first or the things set first according to truth,
upon neither neglecting the truth or the security according to it nor
confusing the things ordered and marked off by nature or tearing apart the
natural sequence.
Whence, I think, it is just for those who have been eager about what is set
forth and who wish to judge thoughtfully whether there is a resurrection of
human bodies or not, first to consider well the power of the things that
contribute toward the showing of this and what place each has obtained and
which of these is first and which second or third and which last upon these;
and having arranged these one must set first the cause of the generation of
men, that is the judgment of him who crafted according to which he made man,
and join on to this fittingly the nature of the men who have come to be, not
as being second in order, but through not being able for the judgment of both
to come to be at the same time, even if they coexist with one another as much
as possible and furnish equal power toward what is set forth.
And through these, as first and having the beginning from crafting, the
resurrection being shown clearly, it is possible no less also through the
arguments of providence to take the trust about this, I mean through the
honor or penalty owed to each of men according to just judgment and through
the end according to the human life.
For many taking up the argument of the resurrection have rested the whole
cause upon the third alone, thinking that the resurrection comes to be
through the judgment. And this


is shown to be false conspicuously from the fact that all men who die are
raised, but not all who are raised are judged; for if only the just according
to the judgment were the cause of the resurrection, those who have neither
sinned nor done right ought presumably not even to be raised, that is the
quite young children; and from the fact that they themselves also think it
right that all be raised, both the others and indeed those who have ended in
the first age, the resurrection does not come to be through the judgment
according to the first account, but through the judgment of him who crafted
and the nature of the things crafted.
And the cause observed upon the generation of men being sufficient even
alone to show the resurrection following according to natural sequence the
dissolved bodies, it is perhaps just to shrink from none of the things set
forth, and consistently with what has been said also to indicate the
occasions from each of the things that follow to those not able to see
together from themselves, and before the others the nature of the men who
have come to be, leading to the same conception and furnishing equal trust
about the resurrection.
For if the nature of men in common altogether has its constitution from an
immortal soul and the body fitted together with it according to generation,
and God allotted such a generation or the living and the whole life neither
to the nature of the soul by itself nor to the nature of the body apart, but
to the men who have come to be from these, in order that, having lived
through from the things from which they come to be and live, they may end in
some one and common end, there being altogether one animal from both, which
both suffers as many passions of soul and as many of the body and works and
does as many things as need the perceptive or the rational judgment, the
whole sequence from these must be referred to some one end, in order that
all things and through all things may run together toward one harmony and the
same sympathy: generation of man, nature of man, life of man, deeds and
passions of man and life and the end belonging to the nature.
And if there is one harmony of the whole animal and sympathy, both of the
things that grow from the soul and of the things accomplished through the
body, the end upon all these must also be one. And there will be one end
according to truth, the same animal being according to its own constitution,
whose end the end is. And it will be purely the same animal, all the same
things being from which as parts the animal is. And they will be the same
according to the peculiar union, the things that have been dissolved being
united again toward the constitution of the animal.
And the constitution of the same men shows of necessity the resurrection of
the bodies that have been made dead and dissolved following; for without this
neither would the same parts be united to one another according to nature nor
would the nature of the same men be constituted.
And if mind and reason have also been given to men toward distinction of
intelligibles, not of beings only but also of the goodness and wisdom and
justice of him who gave, it is necessary, the things for whose sake the
rational judgment has been given remaining, that the judgment given upon
these also remain; and for this to remain is impossible, if


the nature that received it and the things in which it is does not remain.
And he who has received both mind and reason is man, not soul by itself; man
then who is from both must remain forever, and for this one to remain is
impossible unless he is raised.
For if resurrection does not come to be, the nature of men as men would not
remain; and if the nature of men does not remain, in vain has the soul been
fitted to the want of the body and to its passions, and in vain has the body
been bound toward obtaining what it reaches after, yielding to the reins of
the soul and being bridled, and mind is vain, and thought is vain, and
observance of justice or even practice of every virtue and setting and
arrangement of laws and, to speak wholly, everything whatever is noble in men
and through men, or rather even the generation and nature of men themselves.
And if the vain has been driven away from all the works of God and from the
gifts given by him from every side, the remaining of the body according to
its proper nature must altogether spend the ages together with the
unendingness of the soul.
And let no one be surprised if we name remaining the life that is cut through
by death and corruption, reckoning that the account of the name is not one,
the measure of remaining is not one, because neither is the nature of the
things that remain one.
For if each of the things that remain has remaining according to its proper
nature, one would find remaining equalizing neither upon the purely
incorruptible and immortal, because neither are the substances of the better
made equal together with those that differ by a descent, nor is it right to
seek upon men that even and unchangeable remaining, inasmuch as some have
come to be from the beginning immortal and remain endlessly by the judgment
of the maker alone, and men have according to the soul the unchangeable
remaining from generation, and according to the body take on incorruption
from change;
which is what the argument of the resurrection wishes; looking toward which
we both await the dissolution of the body as following the life with want and
corruption and after this hope for remaining with incorruption, neither
making our end equal together with the end of the irrational things nor the
remaining of men with the remaining of the immortals, in order that we may
not escape notice making equal together by this also the nature and life of
men with things to which it does not belong.
It is not then right to be displeased at this, if some unevenness is observed
about the remaining of men, nor, since a separation of soul from the body, a
dissolution of parts and members, cuts through the continuous life, must one
on this account despair of the resurrection.
For neither, since the


relaxations of the senses and of the natural powers that come to be
naturally according to sleep seem to cut through the life according to
joint-perception, men sleeping at equal measures of intervals of time and in
a certain way living again, do we decline to call it the same life; for which
cause, I think, some name sleep a brother of death, not as tracing them as
grown from the same ancestors or fathers, but as like passions coming to be
both for those who have died and for those who sleep, at least for the sake
of the stillness and of perceiving none of the things present or coming to
be, or rather not even of being and of their own life.
If then we do not decline to call the same life the life of men so full of
unevenness from generation until dissolution and cut through by all the
things we said before, neither ought we to despair of the life beyond
dissolution, which brings in together with itself the resurrection, even if
it is cut through for a while by the separation of the soul from the body.
For this nature of men, having from above and according to the judgment of
the maker the unevenness allotted together with it, has an uneven life and
remaining, being cut through now by sleep now by death and by the changes
according to each age, the later things that come on not being shown clearly
in the first.
Or who would have believed, unless taught by experience, that in the
homogeneous and unformed seed so many and so great powers lie stored, or so
great a difference of masses being put together and being fixed, I mean of
bones and sinews and cartilages, and still of muscles and fleshes and inward
parts and the remaining parts of the body? For neither in the seeds while
still fluid is it possible to see any of these, nor indeed does anything of
the things that come on to the complete appear in infants, or the things of
those past their prime in the age of the complete, or the things of those who
have grown old in these.
But even though some of the things said show the natural sequence and the
changes that come on to the nature of men some not at all and some dimly,
still all who are not blind through vice or indolence about the judgment of
these know that first there must come to be a casting of the seeds, and when
these have been articulated according to each part and member and those
conceived have come forth into light there comes on the growth according to
the first age and the completion according to growth, and when they have been
completed a letting-down of the natural powers until old age, then when the
bodies have toiled the dissolution.
As then upon these, neither the seed having the growth or form of men written
in it nor the life the dissolution into the first beginnings, the sequence of
the things that come to be naturally furnishes trust to those who do not have
the credible from the things that appear themselves, much more the argument
tracking the truth from the natural sequence confirms the resurrection, being
more secure and better than experience toward confirmation of truth.


Of the arguments set forth to us earlier for examination and confirming the
resurrection all are of the same kind, as grown from the same beginning; for
their beginning is the generation of the first men from crafting; but some
are confirmed from the first beginning itself from which they grew, and some
following the nature and the life of men take the trust from the providence
of God about us; for the cause, according to which and through which men have
come to be, yoked together with the nature of men has its force from
crafting, and the argument of justice, according to which God judges men who
have lived well or badly, from the end of these; for they grow from there,
but they hang more from providence.
And the first things having been shown by us as far as possible, it would be
well also through the later to show what is set forth, I mean through the
honor or penalty owed to each of men according to just judgment and through
the end according to the human life, and of these very things to set first
the one that leads according to nature and first to examine the argument
about the judgment, suggesting this much only by care for the beginning and
order belonging to what is set forth, that those who have accepted God as
maker of this all must assign the keeping and providence of all the things
that have come to be to his wisdom and justice, if at least they would wish
to remain with their own beginnings, and thinking these things about these
must hold nothing either of the things on earth or of the things in heaven
unsupervised or unprovided-for, but must know the care from the maker
extending upon every unseen thing likewise and appearing, small and greater.
For all the things that have come to be need the care from the maker, and
each peculiarly according to what it is naturally fitted and toward what it
is naturally fitted; for it is of a useless ambition, I think, to wish now to
divide according to kinds or to list what is suitable to each nature.
Man at least, about whom it is now set forth to speak, as in want needs food,
as mortal succession, as rational judgment. And if each of the things said is
to man according to nature and he needs food through the life, and needs
succession through the remaining of the race, and needs judgment through the
lawful of the food and of the succession, there is presumably a necessity,
the food and the succession being carried upon the compound, that the
judgment also be carried upon this, I mean the compound the man from soul and
body, and that such a man become accountable for all the things that have
been done and receive the honor or punishment upon these.
And if the just judgment carries the penalty upon the things that have been
worked against the compound and neither the soul alone must carry off the
wages of the things worked with the body (for this by itself is without
passion of the


faults that come to be about the bodily pleasures or foods and services) nor
the body alone (for this by itself is without judgment of law and justice),
and the man from these receives the judgment upon each of the things worked
by him, and the argument finds this coinciding neither according to this life
(for the according-to-worth is not saved in the present life through many
godless men and men practicing every lawlessness and vice remaining until the
end untried by evils and on the contrary those who have displayed their own
life examined according to every virtue living in pains, in despiteful uses,
in slanders, both outrages and sufferings of every sort) nor after


death, being in separation and dissolution.
But according to neither of the things said is it possible to find the just
judgment being saved; for neither do the serious in the present life carry
off the wages of virtue nor indeed the base those of vice.
For I pass over saying that, the nature in which we now are being saved, not
even the mortal nature is able to bear the commensurate penalty carried for
more or heavier faults.
For both the robber or dynast or tyrant who has slain ten thousand upon ten
thousand unjustly would not by one death pay off the penalty upon these, and
he who holds no true opinion about God, living together with every insult and
blasphemy and overlooking the divine things, and dissolving laws, and
outraging children together with women, and tearing down cities unjustly, and
burning houses with those dwelling in them and ravaging country and
destroying together with these peoples and nations or even a whole race, how
would he suffice in a corruptible body toward the penalty commensurate with
these, death anticipating the according-to-worth and the mortal nature not
even sufficing toward one of the things worked? Neither then according to the
present life is the judgment according to worth shown nor after death.
For either death is a complete quenching of life, the soul being dissolved
together with the body and corrupted together, or the soul remains by itself
undissolved undispersed uncorrupted, and the body is corrupted and dissolved,
saving no longer either memory of the things worked or perception of the
passions upon it.
For if the life of men is quenched altogether, there will appear no care of
men not living, no judgment of those who have lived according to virtue or
vice, and there will be wheeled in again the things of the lawless life and
the swarm of the absurd things that follow this and the head of this
lawlessness, atheism.
And if the body were corrupted and each of the things dissolved went toward
its kindred, and the soul remained by itself as incorruptible, not even so
will the judgment against it have a place, justice not being present; since
it is not even lawful to suppose that some judgment comes to be from God and
beside God to which the just is not present. And the just is not present to
the judgment if the one who accomplished the justice or the lawlessness is
not saved; for the one who accomplished each of the things according to the
life upon which the judgment is was man, not soul by itself. And to say the
whole, this argument will keep the just upon none.
For when right deeds are honored, the body will be wronged plainly from
having shared with the soul the toils upon the things pursued, but not having
shared the honor upon the things done right, and the soul often obtaining
pardon for some transgres18


sions through the body's want and need, and the body itself falling out of
the sharing upon the things done right, over which it bore through the toils
together in the life.
And indeed when faults are judged the just is not saved for the soul, if at
least it alone would pay penalty for the things in which, the body troubling
and dragging toward its own appetites or motions, it faulted now according to
seizure and theft, now according to some more violent pull, at another time
according to a running-together in the part of favor and service of its
constitution.
Or how is it not unjust for the soul to be judged by itself for things for
which it has not even any appetite according to its own nature, nor motion
nor impulse, such as wantonness or violence or greed [injustice] and the
wrongs upon these?
For if most of such evils come to be from men not mastering the passions that
trouble them, and they are troubled by the want and need of the body and the
eagerness and service about this (for the sake of these is all acquisition
and before this use, and still marriage and as many deeds according to the
life, in which and about which both the faulty and what is not such are
observed), where is it just, in things in which the body first suffers and
drags the soul toward sympathy and sharing of the deeds toward which it is
moved, for it to be judged alone, and for the appetites and the pleasures,
and still fears and griefs, upon which everything not moderate is
accountable, to have the motion from the body, but the sins from these and
the punishments upon the things sinned to be carried upon the soul alone
which neither needs something of this sort nor reaches after nor fears or
suffers something of this sort by itself such as man is naturally fitted to
suffer?
But even if we set the passions as not of the body alone but of man, speaking
rightly through this one's life being one from both, we will not presumably
also say that these belong to the soul, whenever we consider purely its own
nature.
For if it is without want of all food once for all, it would never reach
after these things which it in no way needs toward being, nor would it start
out upon any of these things which it is not at all naturally fitted to use;
but neither would it be grieved through lack of money or possessions as
belonging to it in nothing.
And if it is also better than corruption, it fears nothing at all as
destructive of itself; for it has not dreaded hunger nor disease nor
mutilation nor outrage nor fire nor iron, since it cannot even suffer from
these anything harmful or painful, neither bodies nor bodily powers touching
it at all.
And if to attach the passions to souls peculiarly is absurd, to carry the
sins from these and the punishments upon these upon the souls alone is
exceedingly unjust and unworthy of the judgment of God.
And besides the things said how is it not absurd that


vice cannot even be conceived apart upon the soul (for we know the virtues to
be virtues of a man, just as also the vice opposed to these is not of a soul
separated from the body and being by itself), but the honor or punishment
upon these to be carried upon the soul alone?
Or how would one even conceive courage or endurance upon soul alone, it not
having fear of death nor of wound nor of mutilation nor of loss nor of
outrage nor of the pains upon these or of the suffering from these?
And how temperance and soundness of mind, no desire dragging it toward food
or mixing or the other pleasures and delights nor anything else either
troubling from within or provoking from without?
And how prudence, there not lying under it things to be done and not to be
done nor things to be chosen and fled, or rather there being in it no motion
at all or natural impulse toward any of the things to be done?
And where altogether is justice of souls toward one another fitting, or
toward some other of the things of the same kind or of different kinds, they
not having either from where or through what or how they may assign the equal
according to worth or according to proportion, honor toward God being
excepted, nor otherwise having impulse or motion toward use of their own or
toward abstinence from what is another's, use of the things according to
nature and abstinence being observed upon those naturally fitted to use, and
the soul neither needing something nor being naturally fitted to use some
things or something and through this not even the so-called proper-working of
the parts being able to be found upon a soul so disposed?
And indeed that too is of all things most beside reason, to carry the laws
that have been ordained upon men, but to turn the penalty of the things done
lawfully or unlawfully upon the souls alone.
For if he who received the laws would also justly receive the penalty of the
lawlessness, and man received the laws, not soul by itself, man must also
undergo the penalty over the things sinned, not soul by itself; since God did
not ordain for souls to abstain from the things belonging to them in nothing,
such as adultery murder theft seizure dishonor according to those who begot
and every desire coming to be commonly upon injustice and harm of the
neighbors.
For neither is "Honor your father and your mother" well-fitted to souls
alone, such names not belonging to them; for souls begetting souls do not
appropriate the address of father or of mother, but men begetting men;
nor then would "You shall not commit adultery" ever be said upon souls or
conceived as it ought, there not being in them the difference according to
male and female nor some fitness toward mixing or appetite toward this. And
such appetite not being, neither is it possible for mixing to be; and among
those in whom mixing altogether is not, neither is there lawful mixing, which
is marriage; and lawful


mixing not being, neither is it possible for the unlawful and the appetite or
mixing that comes to be upon another's wife to be, for this is adultery. But
neither is it fitting to souls to forbid theft or the desire of the more; for
neither do they need these things which those who need through natural want
or need are accustomed to steal and rob, such as gold or silver or an animal
or some other of the things suitable toward food or shelter or use; for
everything as much as is reachable for those in want as useful is useless to
an immortal nature.
But let the more complete argument about these things be left to those who
wish to consider each more seriously or to contend more ambitiously toward
those who differ, and for us, the things just now said and the things
confirming the resurrection in agreement with these being sufficient, to
spend time still further upon the same would no longer have a season; for we
have not made it our aim to leave aside none of the things possible to say,
but to indicate in summary to those who have come together what one must
think about the resurrection and to measure to the power of those present the
occasions that carry toward this.
And the things set forth having been examined in some measure it would remain
also to examine the argument from the end, already being shown in the things
said, and needing only so much attention and addition, so as not to seem,
having left some of the things said a little before unmentioned, to harm the
hypothesis or the division that came to be from the beginning.
For the sake of these things then and of the things that will be charged upon
these it would be well to mark this much only, that both of the things
constituted from nature and of the things that come to be according to art
there must be a proper end of each, this somewhere both the common conception
of all teaching us and the things turning about before the eyes bearing
witness.
For do we not observe some other end lying under those who farm, and another
under those who heal, and again some other of the things growing from the
earth, and another of the animals nourished upon it and generated according
to some natural sequence?
And if this is clear and the end according to nature must altogether follow
the natural or artistic powers and the activities from these, there is every
necessity that the end of men also, as being of a peculiar nature, be excepted
from the commonness of the others; since it is not even lawful to set the
same end of those who have no share of rational judgment and of those who
work according to the inborn law and reason and use a thoughtful life and
justice.
Neither then would the painless be a proper end for these, for even those
wholly without perception would have a share of this; but neither enjoyment
of the things that nourish or delight the body and a multitude of pleasures;
or the bestial


life must have the first place, and that according to virtue be incomplete.
For this end is proper, I think, to beasts and cattle, not to men who use an
immortal soul and rational judgment.
Nor indeed blessedness of a soul separated from a body; for we were not
considering the life or end of one of these two from which man has been
constituted, but of the one constituted from both; for such is every man who
has obtained this life and there must be some proper end of this one's life.
And if the end is of the compound, and this can be found neither while they
themselves are still living according to this life through the causes already
often stated nor indeed when the soul happens to be in separation, because
such a man is not even constituted when the body has been dissolved or even
wholly scattered even if the soul remains by itself, there is every necessity
that the end of men appear according to some other constitution of the
compound and of the same animal.
And this following of necessity, there must altogether come to be a
resurrection of the bodies that have been made dead or even wholly dissolved
and the same men be constituted again; since at least the law of nature does
not set the end simply nor of chance men, but of those very ones who have
lived according to the life that went before, and for the same men to be
constituted again is impossible, unless the same bodies have been given back
to the same souls. And for the same body to take back the same soul is
impossible otherwise, and possible according to the resurrection alone; for
when this has come to be the end suitable to the nature of men also follows.
And one would not miss it if one said that the end of a thoughtful life and
rational judgment is to spend the ages together without distraction with
those things with which the natural reason has been fitted most and first,
and to exult unceasingly in the contemplation of him who gave and of the
things that have seemed good to that one; even if the many of men, having
been more passionately and more vehemently affected toward the things here,
continue to miss this.
For the multitude of those who fall away from the end belonging to them does
not unmake the common allotment, the examination upon these being peculiar
and the honor or penalty measured to each over those who have lived well or
badly.
