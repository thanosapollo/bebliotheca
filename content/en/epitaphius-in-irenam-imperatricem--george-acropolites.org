Epitaph verses of the megas logariastes on the lady Komnene kyra Irene.
Seeing my tomb here, O stranger, do not pass this by as a common tomb, for it
hides within no nameless corpse, not one inglorious in fortune, not in
dignity, not of the obscure nor of the cast-away. But having learned who I am
and from whom I was born, weep, if any share of tears is yours, and mourn the
feeble life of mortals, seeming to bear nothing more than a shadow and the
night-phantoms in sleep. For he who today lives and breathes great and shines
in the greatest dignities, splendid in youth and conspicuous in race, withers
having died tomorrow like some grass, like some dry wild-grass, as Scripture
writes. Therefore hear my words with sympathy and attend, if any hearing is
present to you. I am empress of the new Romaïs, blessed with ancestral power
and having the purple as the boast of my race; I was born the midwifed child
of the sacred porphyra and a beloved delivery of the palaces. My mother's
father was emperor of the land of the Ausones, Alexios Augustus of the race
of the Komnenoi; and a father splendid both in race and in fortune, Theodore
Laskaris, whose great report and solemn pride and much-envied glories were in
Ares' clashes and victories and in manly wrestlings against enemies. And from
the maternal womb and from the birth I obtained at once the pledges of the
empire and was adorned with imperial symbols, and as heir of the crown as
also of the empire I was established from infant swaddling-clothes. Then
having reached the time of a young woman I had a partner both of the bed and
of the empire, fair to see, noble in appearance, brought forth from a
palace-grown tribe, from the loins of the Doukas-born empress, the manly and
gentle one as a new David and strong of hand as a new Samson. To him I was
joined at once into a common bed, young I was yoked in marriage to a young
man, and we were blended together into one nature by the bonds of marriage
but even more by desire. The bond of marriage joined us into one flesh, and
desire in turn joined us into one soul. I loved him, but I was loved more; I
delighted him again, but I was delighted more. He was breath to me and sweet
light of the eyes; I was to him sweeter than breath and than light and a
seasoning of the heart and a brightness of the mind, and we were a pair
overflowing with all beauties, as many as adorn soul and body together. Not
yet had we been summoned to the evening of life, not yet had time seen us in
decline, but the season of flower held us and of green too, flourishing,
thriving in a pouring of goods. For nothing was absent of the good things in
life, as many as sweeten the hearts of rulers and procure them honor and
glory; but all was present abundantly and to fullness: victories against
enemies, successful generalships, widening of empire and seizure of breadth.
The empire was raised beyond the Hellespont, it reached into Europe from Asia
and occupied the land of the Thracians and of the Macedonians and touched the
gates of Constantine's city. Enslavement of Italians and taking of captives
and continual spoils of these and plunder and complete chasing from the land
of the Ausones, from the quarter toward the rising light-bearer and from the
western and toward-Europe part; no island, no mainland was left them as a
lot, Constantine's city alone was a fortress for them. But alas, the sword of
death came suddenly, unhoped, unexpected, as from an ambush, and reaps me
from life before the season; it had no sparing of my age, it did not shrink
from the untimely cutting, it cut me blooming like a raw ear of grain, it
tore me up by the root as a mortal withering. The endurance in battles of the
husband, and again the skill for the loosing of terrible things, the hardness
of the passion, alas, put to shame; for idle for him is every sword and every
spear and every instrument of battle arrives useless (for against fleshless
foes what even could he do?), and he attends only to wailings and
lamentations, all-night and all-day time together. Alas, who will stay for
him the much lament? Or who will hold the rush of the tears? Who will
moderate the crash of the groanings? And who will calm the storm of the
reasoning? Who will tame the surge of the heart? Who, coming as comforter and
consoler, will ease the roughening of the grief? How I fear (for he is fair
and young) lest he melt with the dirges and the lamentations. For I long for
him to live a long time, even if I myself left life untimely; and I breathe
him, even if I finish without breath. Such an end of life I found, stranger.
But at the very exits of life, having flung far away the purple and every
princely symbol and of the empire, I clothed myself with this cheap rag of
the monks as a burial adornment. And my lot now from so great an empire is a
small portion of earth and this rough stone, which hide my dust here. And
you, if indeed you know to weep with those who weep and to share grief
sympathetically with those in sorrows, judge the common-loving character
welcome, make the Divine favorable to us by prayers, that we may receive Eden
as a new inheritance and the land of the meek as an everlasting lot.
