* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Epaminondas, son of Polymnis, a Theban. Before I write of him, readers should
be warned not to measure foreign manners by their own, or to suppose that what
they themselves think slight was equally slight among other peoples. We know
that among us music is thought beneath a leading man, and dancing is even
counted a vice; yet among the Greeks all these things are welcome and held
worthy of praise. Since I wish to draw a picture of Epaminondas's habits and
life, I think I ought to omit nothing that helps to show them. I shall speak
first of his family, then of the studies in which he was trained and by whom,
then of his character and the gifts of his mind and anything else worth
remembering, and last of his exploits, which most people set above the virtues
of the soul.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

He was born, then, of the father I have named, of a respectable family, but
already left poor by his ancestors; in education, however, no Theban surpassed
him. He learned to play the cithara and to sing to the strings from Dionysius,
whose fame in music was no less than that of Damon or Lamprus, names that are
widely known; he learned to play the pipes from Olympiodorus, and dancing from
Calliphron. His teacher in philosophy was Lysis of Tarentum, a Pythagorean, to
whom he was so devoted that as a young man he preferred that grim and austere
old man in intimacy to all his own contemporaries, and he did not let him go
until he had so far outstripped his fellow pupils in learning that it was easy
to see he would likewise surpass everyone in the other arts. These things, by
our customs, are slight and rather to be despised; but in Greece, at least in
former times, they were a great distinction. After he became an ephebe and
began to attend the wrestling-ground, he aimed less at bulk of strength than
at speed, thinking the former belonged to athletes and the latter to the needs
of war. So he trained chiefly by running and by wrestling, up to the point
where he could still grapple and contend on his feet. In arms, however, he
spent the greatest care.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

To this firmness of body still more goods of mind were added. He was modest,
prudent, serious, wise in using opportunities, skilled in war, brave of hand,
of the greatest spirit, so careful of the truth that he would not lie even in
jest. He was also self-controlled, mild, and wonderfully patient, bearing
injuries not only from the people but even from friends, especially secret in
keeping confidences, and, what is sometimes no less useful than speaking well,
eager to listen, for he thought one learns most easily that way. So when he
had come into a circle where public affairs were being discussed or philosophy
talked of, he never left until the conversation had been brought to an end. He
bore poverty so easily that from the state he took nothing but glory. In
looking after himself he did without the resources of friends; in helping
others he so often used their credit that one may judge he held all things in
common with his friends. For when one of his fellow citizens had been captured
by the enemy, or a marriageable girl could not be given in marriage because of
poverty, he would hold a council of friends and require each to give according
to his means. When he had made up the sum, rather than take the money himself
he would bring the person in need to those who were contributing and have them
count it out themselves, so that the one who received it would know how much
he owed to each.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

His integrity was tested by Diomedon of Cyzicus, who at the request of King
Artaxerxes had undertaken to corrupt Epaminondas with money. He came to Thebes
with a great weight of gold and, with five talents, won over to his will the
young Micythus, whom Epaminondas then loved most. Micythus met Epaminondas and
explained why Diomedon had come. Epaminondas said to Diomedon in his presence:
"There is no need of money. If the king wants what is useful to the Thebans, I
am ready to do it for nothing; if the opposite, he has not gold and silver
enough. I will not take the wealth of the world in exchange for love of my
country. That you tried me without knowing me, and thought me like yourself, I
do not wonder, and I pardon you; but leave at once, so that you do not corrupt
others after failing with me. And you, Micythus, give this man back the
silver, or, if you do not do it at once, I will hand you over to the
magistrate." When Diomedon asked to be allowed to leave in safety and to take
out what he had brought, Epaminondas said, "That I will do, not for your sake
but for mine, so that if the money were taken from you, no one might say that
what I had refused when it was offered had come to me by seizure." When he
asked where Diomedon wished to be escorted, and the man said Athens, he gave
him a guard so that he might arrive safely. Nor did he think that enough: he
also, through the Athenian Chabrias, of whom I have spoken above, arranged
that he should board ship unharmed. This will be enough evidence of his
integrity. I could bring forward a great many more, but a limit must be kept,
since I have resolved to enclose in this one volume the lives of a number of
excellent men, whose deeds several writers before me have set out separately
in many thousands of verses.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

He was also eloquent, so that no Theban equaled him in speaking, and no less
neat in a short reply than ornate in a continuous speech. He had a detractor,
a certain Meneclides, likewise of Thebes, and a rival in public affairs,
practiced enough in speaking, for a Theban, that is: for that people has more
strength than wit. Because he saw Epaminondas flourish in military matters, he
used to urge the Thebans to prefer peace to war, so that they would not need
that commander's services. Epaminondas said to him, "You deceive your fellow
citizens with a word when you call them away from war: under the name of ease
you are procuring slavery. Peace is born of war. Those who wish to enjoy it
for long must be trained in war. So if you want to be first in Greece, you
must use the camp, not the wrestling-ground." The same Meneclides, when he
reproached him for having no children and never having taken a wife, and above
all for arrogance, because he seemed to have won for himself the war-glory of
Agamemnon, Epaminondas said, "Stop, Meneclides, reproaching me about a wife:
there is no one whose advice I would less wish to use in that matter." (For
Meneclides was under suspicion of adultery.) "As for thinking I rival
Agamemnon, you are mistaken. He, with all Greece, barely took one city in ten
years; I, with this one city of ours, in a single day, after putting the
Lacedaemonians to flight, freed all Greece."

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

The same man, when he had come to a meeting of the Arcadians seeking that they
make an alliance with the Thebans and Argives, and Callistratus, the Athenian
envoy, who at that time surpassed everyone in eloquence, was asking that they
rather follow the friendship of the Athenians, and in his speech had inveighed
much against the Thebans and Argives and had put this among his points, that
the Arcadians ought to notice what sort of citizens each city had produced,
from whom they could judge the rest: that the Argives had had Orestes and
Alcmeon, matricides, and that Oedipus had been born at Thebes, who, after
killing his own father, had begotten children by his mother: in answering him,
Epaminondas, when he had finished the rest, and came to those two reproaches,
said he marveled at the stupidity of the Attic rhetorician, who had not
noticed that those men, born innocent at home, after committing the crime and
being driven from their country, had been received by the Athenians. But his
eloquence shone most when he was envoy at Sparta before the battle of Leuctra.
When the envoys of all the allies had gathered there, before a very crowded
meeting of embassies he so exposed the tyranny of the Lacedaemonians that he
shook their power no less by that speech than by the battle of Leuctra. For
then he brought about what later became clear, that the Lacedaemonians were
stripped of their allies' help.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

That he was patient and bore the injuries of his own fellow citizens, because
he thought it a crime to be angry with his country, these are the proofs. When
his fellow citizens, out of envy, had refused to put him in command of the
army, and a leader inexperienced in war had been chosen, whose blunder had
brought things to such a pass that all feared for their safety, because they
were shut in a narrow place and besieged by the enemy, Epaminondas's care
began to be missed: for he was there as a private man in the ranks. When they
asked help of him, he showed no memory of the insult and brought the army home
safe after freeing it from the siege. Nor did he do this only once, but often.
It was most famous, however, when he had led an army into the Peloponnese
against the Lacedaemonians and had two colleagues, one of whom was Pelopidas,
a brave and energetic man. When all of them had fallen into odium through the
charges of their opponents, and for that reason their command had been taken
from them and other generals had succeeded in their place, Epaminondas did not
obey the people's decree, persuaded his colleagues to do the same, and carried
on the war he had undertaken. For he saw that if he did not, the whole army
would perish through the generals' lack of sense and ignorance of war. There
was a law at Thebes that punished with death anyone who had held command
longer than the law prescribed. Epaminondas, seeing that this law had been
passed to preserve the state, would not let it be turned to the city's ruin,
and held command four months longer than the people had ordered.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

After they had returned home, his colleagues were accused on this charge. He
allowed them to shift the whole case onto him and to argue that it was by his
doing that they had not obeyed the law. When that defense had freed them from
danger, no one thought Epaminondas would answer, because he would have nothing
to say. But he came into court, denied none of what his opponents charged,
confessed everything his colleagues had said, and did not refuse to undergo
the penalty of the law, but asked only this of them, that they inscribe on his
tomb: "Epaminondas was punished with death by the Thebans because he compelled
them at Leuctra to defeat the Lacedaemonians, whom before he was commander no
Boeotian had dared to look at in the line of battle, and because in one battle
he not only drew Thebes back from destruction but also claimed all Greece for
liberty, and brought both sides to this, that the Thebans were attacking
Sparta and the Lacedaemonians thought it enough if they could be safe, and he
did not stop making war until, after restoring Messene, he shut their city in
by siege." When he had said this, laughter rose from everyone with good humor,
and no judge dared to cast a vote against him. So he left a capital trial with
the greatest glory.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

At the last, as commander at Mantinea, when with his line drawn up he was
pressing the enemy more boldly, he was recognized by the Lacedaemonians, who
thought their country's safety lay in the destruction of this one man; they
all made a charge at him alone and did not withdraw until, after great
slaughter and many killed, they saw Epaminondas himself, fighting most
bravely, struck from a distance by a spear and fall. By his fall the Boeotians
were somewhat checked, yet they did not leave the fight until they had routed
those who still resisted. But Epaminondas, seeing that he had received a
mortal wound and that if he drew out the iron that had remained from the shaft
in his body he would at once give up his life, held it in until it was
reported that the Boeotians had won. After he heard that, he said, "I have
lived enough: I die unconquered." Then, when the iron was drawn out, he died
at once.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

He never took a wife. When Pelopidas, who had a son of ill repute, reproached
him for this and said he was serving his country badly in that matter by
leaving no children, he said, "See that you are not serving it worse, you who
are going to leave such a son born of yourself. Nor can I lack offspring: I
leave the battle of Leuctra born of me, which must not only survive me but
even be immortal." At the time when the exiles, with Pelopidas as leader,
seized Thebes and drove the Lacedaemonian garrison from the citadel,
Epaminondas stayed at home as long as the slaughter of citizens was going on,
because he did not wish either to defend the wicked or to attack them, lest he
stain his hands with the blood of his own people: for he thought every civil
victory a funeral. The same man, after fighting with the Lacedaemonians had
begun at the Cadmea, stood in the front rank.

It will be enough said of his virtues and life if I add this one thing, which
no one will deny: that Thebes, both before Epaminondas was born and after his
death, was always subject to another's command, but as long as he was at the
head of the state it was the head of all Greece. From this it can be
understood that one man was worth more than a city.
