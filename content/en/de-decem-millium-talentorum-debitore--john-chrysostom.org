ON THE PARABLE of the one who owed the ten thousand talents, and who demanded
the hundred denarii, and that bearing a grudge is worse than every sin.
=51.17=
αʹ. Today I am disposed as one returning to you from a long sojourn abroad;
for those who love, when they cannot be with those they love, have no benefit
from being present. For this very reason we too, though at home, are in no
better state than those who are abroad, since in the time that has passed we
were not able to speak with you; but pardon us; for the silence was not from
sloth, but from weakness. You then rejoice now, because we have been delivered
from the illness; but I rejoice because I have received back your love. For
even when I was sick, worse than the disease for me was not being able to
share in this beloved assembly; and now that I have laid the illness aside,
more to be longed for than health has been the having, with confidence, to
revel in your love. For a fever of the body is not so accustomed by nature to
burn those who have a fever, as separation from those we love is to burn our
souls; and just as those seek bowls and cups and cold streams, so these seek
the faces of those they long for. Those who are accustomed to love know these
things well. Come then, since we have laid the illness aside, let us again
fill ourselves with one another, if it is even possible ever to be filled; for
the nature of love does not know satiety, but always, while enjoying those who
are loved, is raised to a greater flame. And Paul, the nursling of love,
knowing this, said: Owe no one anything, except to love one another. For this
debt alone is always being paid, and is never discharged. Here, to owe
continually is good and worthy of praise. For in the case of money we praise
those who owe nothing, but in the case of love we accept and marvel at those
who owe continually; and what is there a sign of ingratitude, this is here a
sign of gratitude, that the debt of love is never dissolved. Do not be annoyed
at the length of what is about to be said; for I wish to teach you a certain
wonderful lyre-playing, not handling a dead lyre, but stretching the histories
of the Scriptures and the commandments of God in place of strings. And just as
lyre-players, taking the fingers of those being taught, gently bring them to
the notes, and teaching them to feel them out with skill, from the voiceless
notes and strings train them to pre =51.18= pare a voice sweeter and more
pleasant than every voice: so then we too will do, handling your mind in place
of fingers, and bringing it to the commandments of God, we will exhort your
love to touch them with skill, not so that you may raise a theater of men, but
so that through this pleasure you may raise the people of the angels. For it
is not enough only to go over the divine words, but there is need also of the
demonstration from works. And just as on the lyre the skilled man touches the
strings, and the unskilled also touches them, but the one pains the hearer,
while the other charms and delights the soul, and yet the same fingers and the
same strings, but not the same skill: so then also in the case of the divine
Scriptures, many go over the divine words, but not all gain, nor do all bear
fruit; and the cause is that they neither go deep into what is said, nor touch
the lyre with art; for what skill is in lyre-playing, this the demonstration
through works is in the laws of God. Already then we struck one string through
the whole

Lent, reading to you the law concerning oaths, and by the grace of God many
mouths of our hearers were trained in the melody of that law, and having
driven out the wicked custom, instead of swearing by God, they carry on their
mouth continually, in every conversation, Yes, and No, and Believe me; and
even if the necessity of countless affairs constrains them, they would not
endure to go further. βʹ. But since the reception of one commandment is not
enough for our salvation, come, today let us lead you on to another as well;
for even if not all have succeeded in the former law, yet as time goes on
those who lagged behind will overtake those who were first. For I have learned
that such zeal for the matter has come about, that even in the house and at
table there is a contest between men and women, slaves and free, concerning
this commandment, and I blessed those who feast in this way. For what could be
holier than that table, where drunkenness and gluttony and all profligacy have
been driven out, and a certain wonderful contest concerning the keeping of the
laws of God has been brought in instead, and the husband watches the wife,
lest she ever fall into the pits of perjury, and the wife guards the husband,
and a very great condemnation is laid on the one who transgresses; and the
master is not even ashamed to be refut51.19 ed by the slaves, nor to correct
his household servants in these things. One would not err in calling such a
house a church of God. For where there is such temperance that in a time of
luxury they are anxious about the divine laws, and all who are present contend
and rival one another over this, it is quite clear that every demon and wicked
power has been driven out, and Christ is present, rejoicing in the good
rivalry of his servants, lavishing every blessing upon them. For these reasons
then, leaving this commandment hereafter (for I know that by the grace of God
it will run through the whole city, since you have taken up warm beginnings
and a strong start), I will pass to another: the disdain of anger. For just as
on the lyre it is not enough to produce the melody from one string only, but
one must go over all with the fitting rhythm: so also in the virtue of the
soul one law alone is not enough for our salvation, as I said before, but we
must keep them all with exactness, if we are going to produce the melody
sweeter and more useful than all harmony. Has your mouth learned not to swear?
Has the tongue been trained to say Yes and No everywhere? Let it learn also to
turn away from every insult, and to bring even more zeal to this commandment,
since it needs even more toil from us. For there it was necessary only to
overcome habit, but in the case of anger there is need of more strenuous zeal;
for the passion is tyrannical, and often sweeps away those who are sober, and
leads them down to the very pit of destruction. Bear then with the length of
the words. For it is absurd that, being wounded every day in the marketplaces,
in houses, by friends, by kinsmen, by enemies, by neighbors, by household
servants, by a wife, by a child, by one's own thoughts, one should not even
once in the whole week take thought for the healing of those wounds, and this
knowing that this manner of healing is both without expense and without pain.
For I do not now hold the iron in my hand, but I handle a word in place of
iron, being sharper than any iron, and cutting out all the putrefaction of
sin, yet not furnishing pain to the one being cut. I do not have fire in my
right hand, but I have teaching more vehement than fire, not applying a
cautery, but checking the spread of wickedness, and instead of pain furnishing
much pleasure to the one being delivered from wickedness. There is no need of
times here, no need of toils,


no need of money; it is enough only to will, and all the things of virtue have
been accomplished for us; and if we consider the dignity of God who commands
and legislates, we shall receive sufficient teaching and exhortation; for we
do not speak from ourselves, but we lead you all to the lawgiver. Follow then,
hear the divine laws. Where then did he speak about anger and bearing a
grudge? In many places and elsewhere, but especially through that parable
which he spoke to the disciples, beginning somehow thus: Therefore the kingdom
of the heavens was likened to a man who was a king, who wished to take account
with his =51.20= servants. And when he had begun to take account, one debtor
of ten thousand talents was brought to him. And since he had not wherewith to
pay, he commanded him to be sold, and his wife, and his children, and all that
he had, and payment to be made. Falling then at his feet, that servant
besought him, saying: Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay you all. And
the lord of that servant, being moved with compassion, released him, and
forgave him the debt. And going out, that one found one of his fellow-servants
who owed him a hundred denarii, and seizing him he throttled him, saying: Pay
me what you owe. Falling then at his feet, his fellow-servant besought him,
saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay you all. But he would not, but
going away he cast him into prison, until he should pay what was owed. And his
fellow-servants, seeing this, were indignant, and coming they made it clear to
their lord. Then his lord, calling him, said: You wicked servant, I forgave
you all that debt, since you besought me; was it not necessary that you also
should have mercy on your fellow-servant, as I also had mercy on you? Then he
delivered him to the tormentors, until he should pay all that was owed him. So
also my heavenly Father will do to you, if you do not each forgive his brother
from your hearts their trespasses.
γʹ. This then is the parable; but we must say for what reason he set it forth
with a cause; for he did not simply say, The kingdom of the heavens was
likened, but, Therefore the kingdom of the heavens was likened. For what
reason then is the cause attached? He was speaking to the disciples about
forbearance, and he was training them to master anger, and not to make much
account of the wrongs done to us by others, saying thus: If your brother sins
against you, go and reprove him between you and him alone; if he hears you,
you have gained your brother. And while Christ was saying these and such
things to the disciples and teaching them to philosophize, Peter, the chief of
the choir of the apostles, the mouth of the disciples, the pillar of the
Church, the firmness of the faith, the foundation of the confession, the
fisher of the inhabited world, the one who led our race up from the depth of
error to heaven, the one who is everywhere fervent and full of boldness, or
rather of love than of boldness, while all were silent, coming forward to the
teacher says: How often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? up
to seven times? at once he both asks and promises, and before he learns, he is
ambitious. For knowing clearly the mind of the teacher, that it inclines
toward loving-kindness, and that he grants most of all to that one who most of
all passes over the sins of neighbors and does not examine them bitterly,
wishing to please the lawgiver, he says: Up to seven times? Then, that you may
learn what a man is, and what God is, and how this man's ambition, wherever it
arrives, compared with that one's abundance, is cheaper than all poverty, and
that as a drop is to a boundless sea, so is our goodness to his unspeakable
loving-kindness, when he said, Up to seven times? and thought he was =51.21=
making a great show of generosity and being lavish, hear what he says: I do
not say to you, up to seven times, but up to


seventy times seven; some think seven and seventy, but it is not so; but it is
nearly the five hundredth; for seven times seventy is four hundred and ninety.
And do not think the command is hard, beloved. For if you forgive one who has
sinned once and twice and a third time in the day, even if he is exceedingly
of stone, even if the one who has grieved you is wilder than the demons
themselves, he will not be so insensible as to fall into the same things
again, but being chastened by the frequency of the forgiveness, he will be
better and milder; and you again, if you are prepared to disregard so many
times the sins done against you, having been trained from one and a second and
a third forgiveness, will no longer even have toil in such philosophy, having
practiced once for all by the frequency of forgiveness not even to be struck
by the sins of your neighbor. Hearing these things Peter stood agape, not
taking thought for himself only, but also for those who were going to be
entrusted to him. Therefore, so that he might not do the same thing which he
also did in the case of other commandments, he anticipated and shut off every
question for him. And what did he do in the case of the remaining
commandments? If ever Christ commanded something of this kind, which seemed to
have some difficulty, leaping forward before the others he would ask and
inquire about the commandment. For when the rich man came and asked Christ
about eternal life, and having learned the things that produce perfection,
went away grieved because of the money, Christ having said that it is easier
for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter
into the kingdom of the heavens: Peter, although he had stripped himself of
all things, and no longer even held the hook, but had despised both the craft
and the boat, coming forward said to Christ: And who can be saved? And see
both the gentleness of a disciple and the fervor; for he neither said: You
command impossible things, the command is hard, the law is harsh; nor did he
keep silent, but both showed the care for the rest, and rendered the honor
owed by a disciple to a teacher, saying thus: And who can be saved? and not
yet having become a shepherd, he had a shepherd's soul, and not yet having
been entrusted with the rule, he kept the care fitting for a ruler, taking
thought for the whole inhabited world. For if he had been rich, and clothed
with much money, perhaps someone would have said that not caring for the rest,
but for himself, and taking thought for his own affairs, he brought this
inquiry; but as it is, poverty frees him from this suspicion, and shows that
caring for the salvation of others he was anxious and inquisitive, and wished
to learn from the teacher the way of salvation. Therefore Christ also,
encouraging him, says: The things impossible with men are possible with God.
For do not think, he says, that you are being left deserted; I take part with
you in this zeal, and I make the things that are impassable become easy and
ready. Again, when Christ was speaking about marriage and a wife and saying
that He who puts away a wife except for the cause of fornication makes her to
be adulterated, and exhorting to bear every wickedness of a wife except
fornication alone, Peter, while the others were silent, coming forward said to
Christ: =51.22= If the case of the man with the woman is so, it is not
expedient to marry. See here too how he both kept the honor fitting for a
teacher, and took thought for the salvation of the rest, not even here being
anxious about his own affairs. Therefore, so that he might not say something
of this kind here too, he anticipated and by the parable destroyed his
objection beforehand. For this reason the evangelist also said: Therefore the
kingdom of the heavens was likened to a man who was a king, who wished to take
account with his servants; showing that he tells this parable for this reason,
that


you may learn that even if you forgive your brother his sins seventy times
seven in the day, you have not yet done anything great, but you still fall far
short of the Master's unspeakable loving-kindness, and you do not give as much
as you receive.
δʹ. Let us then listen to the parable; for even if it seems to be clear from
itself, yet it also has a certain unspeakable treasure of thoughts hidden
within. The kingdom of the heavens was likened to a man who was a king, who
wished to take account with his servants. Do not simply run past the saying,
but unfold for me that court, and entering into your own conscience, reckon up
the things done by you through all your life; and when you hear that he takes
account with his servants, reckon up both kings, and generals, and prefects,
and rich and poor, and slaves and free, and all. For we must all be made
manifest before the judgment seat of Christ. And if you are rich, consider
that you will give account, whether you spent the money on harlots, or on the
poor, whether on parasites and flatterers, or on those in need, whether on
wantonness or on loving-kindness, whether on luxury and profligacy and
drunkenness, or on the help of those who are abused. And not for the spending
only, but also for the acquiring you will be required to give account, whether
you collected it from just labors, or from robbery and greed, whether having
inherited a paternal inheritance, or having overturned the houses of orphans,
and having plundered the substance of widows. And just as we require of our
household servants an account not only of the outgoing of the money, but also
of the incoming, examining from where they received the money, and from whom,
and how, and how much: so then God also requires of us the audits not of the
spending only, but also of the acquiring. And not the rich man only, but the
poor man also gives account of his poverty: whether he bore the poverty nobly
and thankfully, whether he did not lose heart, whether he did not chafe,
whether he did not accuse the providence of God, seeing another living in
luxury and wantonness, and himself being in want. For just as the rich man is
required to give account of almsgiving, so also the poor man of patience; or
rather not of patience only, but even of almsgiving itself; for there is no
hindrance to almsgiving =51.23= in poverty. And witness is the widow who cast
in the two mites, and who overshot those who laid down much by that small
deposit. And not rich and poor only, but rulers and judges also are examined
with much exactness, whether they did not corrupt justice, whether they did
not vote for those being judged according to favor, whether not according to
hatred, whether being flattered they did not give the vote contrary to what
was due, whether bearing a grudge they did not abuse those who had done no
wrong. And not the rulers from without only, but also those who preside over
the Churches will render the account of their own rule; and especially these
are the ones who undergo the more bitter and heavy audits. For the one who has
been entrusted with the ministry of the word will be examined there with
exactness, whether neither by sloth nor by envy he overlooked anything of the
things that ought to be said, and showed through works that he declared all
things, and hid nothing of the things that are profitable. Again, the one who
has obtained the episcopate, by how much he has risen to a greater bulk, by so
much more account will he be required, not of teaching only and protection of
the poor, but also of the proving of ordinations and of countless other
things. And making these things clear Paul wrote to Timothy: Lay hands hastily
on no one, nor share in others' sins; and exhorting the Hebrews also
concerning the same rulers, he frightened them otherwise, saying thus: Obey
those who lead you and submit; for they watch over your souls, as those who
will give account. Then we shall render not of deeds only, but also of words


audits. For just as we too, having entrusted silver to household servants,
require of them an account of all things, so also God, having entrusted words
to us, will seek the manner of their spending. And we are required and
examined with exactness, whether we did not spend them idly nor in vain; for
silver simply spent has not harmed so much as words brought out idly and in
vain and to no need. For silver spent in vain has often brought the loss in
money, but a word simply brought forth has overturned whole houses, and
destroyed and dissolved souls; and the loss of money it is possible to correct
again, but a word that has once leapt out it is not possible to recover again.
And that we give justice for words, hear what Christ says: And I say to you,
that every idle word which men shall speak upon the earth, they shall give
account concerning it in the day of judgment; for by your words you shall be
justified, and by your words you shall be condemned. And we give audits not of
words only, but also of hearings; as if I receive an accusation spoken falsely
against the neighbor; For a vain hearing, he says, you shall not receive. And
if those who receive a vain hearing would not obtain pardon, what defense will
those who slander and accuse have?
εʹ. And why do I speak of words and hearing, when we even undergo audits of
thoughts? And this very thing Pau51.24 l made clear, saying: So then judge
nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who will both bring to light the
hidden things of the darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the
hearts. And the Psalmist also says that The thought of man shall confess to
you. What is, The thought of man shall confess to you? As, if you spoke with
your brother with deceit and a wicked mind, if with the mouth and the tongue
praising him, in your thought you maltreated him and envied him. For again
Christ, hinting at this very thing, that we give justice not of works only,
but also of thoughts, said: He who has looked at a woman to desire her has
already committed adultery with her in his heart. And yet the sin did not go
forward into a deed, but is still in the mind; but not even so can he remain
without blame who looks around at the beauties of women for this, that he may
kindle a desire of fornication. When then you hear that he takes account with
his servants, do not simply pass by the saying, but consider every rank, every
age, both natures, of men and of women; consider what that court will be then,
reckon up all the things you have sinned. For even if you yourself forget the
things you have transgressed, God will never forget, but will set all things
before our eyes, unless we anticipate and dissolve them now through repentance
and confession, and through never bearing a grudge against our neighbors. And
for what reason does he make the accounting? Not as himself being ignorant,
for how, he who knows all things before their generation? but that he may
persuade you the household servant that you justly owe whatever you owe; or
rather not that you may learn only, but that you may also wash it off; since
he also commanded the prophet for this reason to speak the sins of the Jews.
For say, he says, their lawlessnesses to the house of Jacob, and their sins to
the house of Israel; not that they may hear only, but that they may be
corrected. And when he had begun to take account, one debtor of ten thousand
talents was brought to him. How much then had he been entrusted with, that he
had consumed so much? Great is the bulk of the debts; and not only was this
the grievous thing, but that he was also first brought to the master. For if
after many others who had been well-disposed this one had been brought, it
would not have been so marvelous that the master was not provoked; for the
good disposition of those who went before was going to make him milder toward
those who afterward were ungrateful. But that the one first brought in should
appear ungrateful, and having become so ungrateful,


should meet with a loving master, this is the thing most marvelous and
paradoxical. Men then, whenever they find those who owe, as if they had found
a hunt and a catch, so they rejoice, and they do all things, that they may
exact the whole; and if they cannot because of the poverty of the debtors,
they pour out the anger over the money upon the wretched body of the
miserable, maltreating and beating and inflicting countless evils upon it. But
God was moving and managing all things to the contrary, that he might deliver
him from the debts. For in our case, to exact is wealth; but in God's case, to
forgive is wealth; we, whenever we receive what is owed, then become more
well-off; but God, whenever he forgives the transgressions, then especially is
rich; for the wealth of God =51.25= is the salvation of men, as Paul says: Who
is rich toward all, and upon all who call upon him. But perhaps someone might
say: And how is it that he who wished to remit and forgive the charges
commanded him to be sold? This very thing then especially shows his
loving-kindness. But let us not hurry, but let us proceed in order to the
narration of the parable. And since he had not wherewith to pay, he says. What
is, Since he had not wherewith to pay? Again an intensification of
ingratitude; for when he says, Since he had not wherewith to pay, he says
nothing else but that he was barren of achievements, and had no good work, so
as to be reckoned to him toward deliverance from the sins; for achievements
are reckoned, they are altogether reckoned to us toward deliverance from sins,
just as faith also unto righteousness. For to the one who does not work, but
believes upon the one who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned unto
righteousness. And why do I speak of faith and achievements, when even
afflictions are reckoned to us toward a dissolving of sins? And this Christ
makes clear through the parable of Lazarus, introducing Abraham saying to the
rich man, that Lazarus received the evil things in his life, and therefore
here he is comforted. And Paul also makes it clear, writing to the Corinthians
concerning the one who had committed fornication, and saying thus: Deliver
such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be
saved. And comforting others who had sinned he said thus: For this reason many
among you are weak and sick, and a sufficient number sleep. For if we judged
ourselves, we would not be judged; but being judged by the Lord, we are
disciplined, that we may not be condemned with the world. And if trial, and
disease, and weakness, and destruction of flesh, which we undergo unwillingly,
not ourselves preparing them, is reckoned to us toward a dissolving of sin,
much more achievements, which we pursue willingly and with zeal. But this man
was barren of every good thing, and had an unbearable burden of sins;
therefore he says: Since he had not wherewith to pay, he commanded him to be
sold; from which especially it is possible to learn thoroughly the
loving-kindness of the Master, that he both made the accounting, and commanded
him to be sold; for he worked both these things, so that he might not be sold.
From where is this clear? From the end; for if he wished him to be sold, who
was there preventing it? who was there hindering it?
ςʹ. For what reason then did he command, not intending to do it? That he might
increase the fear; and he increased the fear through the threat, that he might
cast him into supplication; and he cast him into supplication, that he might
take occasion for forgiveness. For he was able even before the entreaty to
remit him, but so that he might not work worse things, he did not do this. He
was able even before the accounting to make the forgiveness, but so that he
might not, being ignorant of the bulk of the sins, become more inhuman and
more savage toward his neighbors, for this reason he first taught him the
greatness of the debt, and then forgave it all. For if


after the accounting had taken place, and the debt had been shown, and the
threat had been brought upon him, and the condemnation which he was just to
undergo had become clear, he became so wild and savage toward the
fellow-servant: if none of these =51.26= had come about, into what wildness
would he not have slipped? For this reason God was doing and managing all
these things, checking beforehand that harshness of his. And if he was
corrected by none of these, the cause is not with the teacher, but with that
one who did not receive the correction. But nevertheless let us see how he
treats the wound for him. Falling then, he says, at his feet, he besought him,
saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay you all. And yet it had said
that he had not wherewith to pay; but such is the custom of debtors, even if
they have nothing to pay, they promise, so as to be delivered from the present
terrors. Let us hear, as many as are slothful about prayer, how great is the
power of petitions. This man displayed no fasting, no poverty, none of such
things, but being barren and naked of all virtue, since he only besought the
master, he was able to draw him toward mercy. Let us not then give up in our
petitions. For who could be more sinful than this man, who was liable to so
many charges, and possessed no achievement, neither small nor great? But
nevertheless he did not say to himself: I have no confidence, I am full of
shame, how can I come forward? how can I beseech? which many of those who sin
say, being sick with a diabolical reverence. You have no confidence? For this
reason come forward, that you may acquire much confidence. For is it a man who
is going to be reconciled to you, that you should be ashamed and blush? It is
God, who wishes more than you to deliver you from the charges; you do not so
desire your own safety, as he longs for your salvation; and this he taught us
through the works themselves. You do not have confidence? For this reason you
will be able to have confidence, because you are so disposed; for the greatest
confidence is not to think that one has confidence; just as the greatest shame
is to justify oneself before the Lord. That man is unclean, even if he is
holier than all men; just as he becomes righteous who has persuaded himself
that he is last of all. And witnesses of what is said are the Pharisee and the
publican. Let us not then despair in our sins, nor give up, but let us come to
God, let us fall down, let us beseech, which this man did, using a good mind
up to this point. Both not giving up, and not despairing, and confessing the
things that had been sinned, and asking a certain delay and postponement, all
these things are good, and of a crushed mind, and of a humbled soul. But the
things from here on are no longer like the former; for what he gathered
through the supplication, these he suddenly poured out all through the anger
against the neighbor. But for the present let us come to the manner of the
forgiveness; let us see how he remitted him, and from where the master came to
this. Being moved with compassion, his lord, he says, released him, and
forgave him the debt. That one asked a postponement, this one gave
forgiveness; so that he received more than he asked. Therefore Paul also says:
To him who is able to do all things exceedingly above what we ask or think.
For you are not able to think so many things as that one has prepared to give
you. Do not then be ashamed, nor blush; or rather be asham51.27 ed at the
sins, but do not despair, nor stand away from prayer, but come forward even
being a sinner, that you may reconcile your Master, that you may give him
opportunity to display his own loving-kindness in the forgiveness of your
sins; since if you fear to come forward you have hindered his goodness, you
have prevented the abundance of his kindness, so far as it lies with you. Let
us not then slacken, nor be slothful in prayers. For even if we are brought

No one has sinned so many things as this man; for he went through every kind
of wickedness; and this the ten thousand talents make clear; no one was so
barren as this man; for this the not having wherewith to pay makes clear. But
nevertheless the power of prayer was able to snatch away the one who had been
betrayed from every side. And can prayer, someone says, do so much, as to
deliver from punishment and vengeance the one who has offended the Master
through works and countless affairs? Yes, it can do so much, O man. For this
is not the only thing that accomplishes the whole, but it has as ally and
greatest helper the loving-kindness of God who receives the prayer, which here
accomplished the whole, and made the prayer powerful. This then he was also
hinting at when he said: Being moved with compassion, his lord released him,
and forgave him the debt; that you may learn that together with the prayer,
and before the prayer, the master's compassions did the whole. And going out,
that one found one of his fellow-servants who owed him a hundred denarii; and
seizing him he throttled him, saying: Pay me, if you owe anything. What then
could be more polluted than this? Having the benefaction still ringing in his
ears, he forgot the master's loving-kindness.
ζʹ. Do you see how great a good it is to remember sins? For this man too, if
he had remembered these things continually, would not have become so wild and
inhuman. Therefore I say continually, and I will not cease saying, that it is
exceedingly useful and necessary that we continually remember all the things
we have transgressed; for nothing can make the soul so philosophic and mild
and gentle as the continual memory of sins. Therefore Paul also remembered not
only the sins after the washing, but also those before baptism, although they
had been wiped out once for all. And if that man remembered those before
baptism, much more must we remember those after baptism; for we not only
dissolve them by the memory, but we shall also be disposed more mildly toward
all men, and we shall serve God with more goodwill, learning thoroughly his
unspeakable loving-kindness from the memory of these. Which this man did not
do, but having forgotten the greatness of the debts, he forgot the benefaction
also; and having forgotten the benefaction, he became wicked toward the
fellow-servant, and through the wickedness toward that one he lost all that he
had gained from the loving-kindness of God. For seizing him he throttled him,
saying: Pay me, if you owe anything. He did not say, Pay me the hundred
denarii; for he was ashamed of the cheapness of the debt; but, Pay, if you owe
anything. And he, falling at his feet, besought =51.28= him, saying: Have
patience with me, and I will pay you all. Through the words by which that one
found the forgiveness, through these this one also asks to be saved. But he,
from the excess of savagery, was not even bent by these words, nor did he
consider that he was saved through these words. And yet even if he had
forgiven, the thing would no longer even so have been loving-kindness, but a
debt and an obligation. For if, before the accounting took place, and that
vote was brought out, and he enjoyed so great a benefaction, he had done this,
what was done would have been of his own magnanimity; but as it is, after so
great a gift and the remission of so many sins, he was thereafter liable as of
a certain necessary debt for forbearance toward the fellow-servant. But
nevertheless he did not even do this, nor did he consider how great was the
interval between the remission which he himself enjoyed, and that which he was
going to display toward the fellow-servant; for one would see much difference
not only in the quantity of the debts, nor in the dignity of the persons only,
but also in the very manner. For those were ten thousand talents, but these a
hundred denarii; and this man had insulted the master, but the one who owed
him had insulted the fellow-servant; and


this man, having been well treated, was going to grant a favor, but the
master, seeing no good thing done by him, neither small nor great, forgave
all. But nevertheless he considered none of these things, but having been once
for all blinded by anger he was choking him, and he cast him into the prison.
And his fellow-servants, seeing this, he says, were indignant, and before the
master the fellow-servants condemn him, that you may learn the master's
gentleness. And his master, having heard, and having called him, is judged
with him again, and not even so does he simply bring out the condemnation, but
first he pleads his case. And what does he say? You wicked servant, I forgave
you all that debt. What could be better than this master? When he owed him the
ten thousand talents, he did not even grieve him as far as a word, nor did he
call him wicked, but only commanded him to be sold; and this, that he might
deliver him from the debts. But when he became wicked toward the
fellow-servant, then he is angry and provoked, that you may learn that he more
easily remits the things against himself than the sins against the neighbors.
And he does not do this here only, but also elsewhere. For if you bring your
gift, he says, to the altar, and there remember that your brother has
something against you, go, first be reconciled to your brother, and then come
and offer your gift. Do you see how everywhere he prefers our things to his
own, and sets nothing higher than peace and love toward the neighbor? And
again elsewhere: He who puts away his wife except for the cause of fornication
makes her to be adulterated. And through Paul he legislated thus: If any man
has an unbelieving wife, and she is content to dwell with him, let him not put
her away. For if she commits fornication, he says, cast her out; but if she is
unbelieving, do not cast her out; if she sins against you, he says, cut her
off; but if she sins against me, hold her. So also here, when he sinned
against h51.29 im with so many sins, he forgave; but when he sinned against
the fellow-servant with things much less and shorter than those against the
master, he did not remit, but he prosecuted; and here he called him wicked,
but there he did not even grieve him as far as a word. For this reason here
also this is added, that being angry he delivered him to the tormentors; but
when he was requiring of him an account of the ten thousand talents, he added
nothing of this kind; that you may learn that that vote was not of anger, but
of care leading toward forgiveness; but this sin especially provoked him. What
then could be worse than bearing a grudge, when it recalls a loving-kindness
of God that has been brought out, and what the sins were not able to do to
him, this the anger against the neighbor is able to work upon him? And yet it
is written that The gifts of God are without repentance.
How then here, after the gift was brought out, after the loving-kindness went
forward, was the vote recalled again? Because of the bearing of a grudge; so
that one would not err in declaring this to be more grievous than every sin;
for all the others were able to find forgiveness, but this not only was not
able to obtain pardon, but even renewed again the others that had been wiped
out once for all. So that bearing a grudge is a double evil, both that it has
no defense before God, and that our remaining sins, even if they have been
forgiven, it recalls again, and sets them up against us; which then it also
did here. For nothing, nothing does God so hate and turn away from as a man
who bears a grudge and keeps anger. And this he showed especially from here,
and from the prayer itself he commanded us to say thus: Forgive us our debts,
as we also forgive our debtors. Knowing all these things then, and having
inscribed this parable in our hearts, when we consider what we have suffered
from the fellow-servants, let us reckon what we have done against the


Master; and by the fear of our own sins we shall be able quickly to
thrust51.30 away the wrath at others' transgressions; for if one must remember
sins, one must remember one's own only; and if we remember our own, we shall
never reckon those of others; just as if we forget these, those will easily
take up lodging in our thoughts. For this man too, if he had remembered the
ten thousand talents, would not have remembered the hundred denarii; but since
he forgot those, therefore he was choking the fellow-servant over these, and
wishing to exact a few things, he neither obtained those, but also drew the
bulk of the ten thousand talents upon his own head. Therefore I would say with
confidence that this is more grievous than every sin; or rather not I this,
but Christ declared this through this parable. For if this were not more
grievous than ten thousand talents, I mean the unspeakable sins, he would not
have recalled those also because of this. Let us then be zealous for nothing
so much as to be pure from anger, and to reconcile those who are ill-disposed
toward us, knowing that neither prayer, nor almsgiving, nor fasting, nor
communion of the mysteries, nor anything else of such things, if we bear a
grudge, will be able to stand for us on that day; just as if we overcome this
sin, even if we have countless transgressions, we shall be able to obtain some
pardon. And the word is not mine, but of God himself who is then going to
judge us. For just as here he said that So also my Father will do, if you do
not each forgive from your hearts; so also elsewhere he says: If you forgive
men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. Therefore,
that we may both here live a mild and calm life, and there obtain forgiveness
and remission, let us be zealous and take pains to reconcile to ourselves as
many enemies as we may have; for so we shall also reconcile our Master, even
if we have sinned countless things, and we shall attain the good things to
come; of which may we all be counted worthy, by the grace and loving-kindness
of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might unto the ages of
the ages. Amen.
