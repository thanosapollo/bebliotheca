621 Phil 1:5 Upon the fellowship. Upon your fellowship unto the gospel. And
the "from the first day": either that from the first day from which you
believed until now I thank my God for you all, making the petition with joy,
or upon your fellowship unto the gospel which has been ministered from the
first day until now. And what follows seems rather to agree with the former
interpretation. 622 For how were you giving thanks from the beginning, when
you had not yet seen them confirmed in the fellowship? Being confident, he
says, that he who implanted such a beginning, eager and full of warmth,
himself would also lead this on to the perfection of the work. Phil 1:7 To
think this on behalf of all. For he thinks and reckons good things and that
the disciples accomplish great things, who has expended great zeal and care
upon them. Or: it is right for me to reckon this, who have such a disposition
concerning you that not even in my bonds do I forget you, but always carry you
in the heart. And what is the cause of having them in the heart and
unforgotten? That they are, he says, partakers with me of the grace: either of
the spiritual gift supplied to them through faith, being variously operative
in them then, or simply of the grace of regeneration and adoption through
baptism. Or I said I have you in the heart, you being partakers with me, both
in my bonds and in the defense and in the confirmation. And "of the grace"
instead of "through the grace"; for without divine grace no one is counted
worthy of such fellowship. Phil 1:8 In the bowels of Jesus Christ. That is:
not human ones, nor such as suffer anything human, but wholly disposed
according to Christ Jesus. Great indeed is it to be longed for by Paul; but
also in bowels is greater; and also in the bowels of Jesus Christ leaves no
excess remaining. Phil 1:13 So that the bonds. Either the bonds in Christ,
that is, those through Christ, so that the syntax may be by hyperbaton. Or the
bonds, he says, that have become manifest in Christ, that is, made manifest by
the good pleasure and economy of Christ; for Paul suffering and succeeding was
nothing, if he did not refer it to Christ. Or the bonds of those are made
manifest in Christ, who do nothing for display, but so act that those who see
are benefited and glorify the God in the heavens. 623 Phil 1:17 Or what else,
he says, if not this? That is: for what else, if not that I am set for the
defense of the gospel? Phil 1, As always, so now also. Always Christ was
magnified in Paul, both when he permitted him to be distressed in the bonds
and the scourges, and when he drew him out and delivered him. For through the
former the preaching was confirmed and strengthened, those who saw Paul's
bonds and the afflictions reckoning that if his preaching were not something
divine and wholly true, he would not gladly have given himself over to myriad
evils, when it was possible, having ceased, to be delivered. Wherefore
reasonably Christ was magnified, the preaching being confirmed. And again when
he drew him out and snatched him from the dangers, from that very thing he was
magnified. For if it were not some divine and superhuman grace that was
delivering Paul, how were the bonds loosed? How were the doors opened? How did
those myriad wonders come to pass? Christ therefore bore witness to Paul
through the things by which he delivered him from the dangers; and Paul again,
through the things in which he endured the trials, bore witness to the
preaching; and through both Christ was magnified. Thus then also now, he says:
whether Christ delivers me from death or not, he will be magnified in me; for
I, even living, will not stand away from the boldness on behalf of the
preaching, and if I must die, with this I will die. Whether through life.
Whether through life, as now snatching me from Nero's murderousness, or
through death, as later deeming me worthy to complete the martyrdom for him by
the sword. Phil 1:24-25 And this being confident I know. Instead of: and this
having taken courage and not doubting I know. And what is it that, taking
courage and not doubting, he knows? That it is more necessary to remain in the
flesh on their account. Then: I know that I shall remain and continue with
you; for "I know" is from the common construction. Or also the whole is from
the common, as: that taking courage I know, not only that it is more necessary
for me to remain in the flesh, but that I shall also remain and continue with
you. For the initiate of the ineffable and supercelestial things did not even
know his own life in doubt, and that he would still now live on. 624 And one
can also, having arranged the saying otherwise, take it more readily thus: to
remain, he says, is more necessary on your account. Then: and taking courage
toward this, that it is more necessary, he says, I know that I shall remain
and continue with you, and so forth. Phil 1:28 And not being terrified in
anything. If you are, he says, not terrified by the adversaries, their
uprising against you will be turned for them into a demonstration of
destruction, that is, either that the things of destruction will be shown to
them and made manifest through your steadfastness, and they will stand away
from the vain glory and their practices, and from this there will rather be
for you a wealth of salvation, as having also procured for others to flee
destruction and gain salvation. Either then it is to be understood thus, or
more simply that their attempt and their war against you, you not being
terrified, will demonstrate and procure for them nothing else except their own
destruction, but for you, instead of harm, it will become both a ground of
salvation and an occasion of more perfect virtue. Phil 2:3 But in lowliness of
mind. Do you wish to know that lowliness of mind does not harm you at all, nor
cause you loss, nor bring you down from the present height, but rather becomes
a proof of it? Consider Christ, he says; for especially in this he is shown to
be God by nature, in being humbled and wearing the man. For he knew that from
this humiliation he would suffer no loss of the height of the Godhead
belonging to him by nature. But if he had this as something acquired, he would
have feared to humble himself, lest he fall from the dignity scarcely acquired
by him. Humiliation therefore is a proof of the height of his Godhead
according to nature. If then anyone is high according to virtue and aspires to
be great, let him be lowly-minded; for humiliation is what shows and bears
witness to the height of virtue. Let him imitate Christ; he will lose nothing
by being humbled, but rather will gain great things. He who is poor in virtue
cannot be humbled; for he fears lest he fall from the counterfeit and assumed
height. Let him who is rich and high in virtue be humbled; for in this he
rather shows himself as truly high, that he has not feared to be humbled, and
knows that he is established. 625 Phil 2:5 For let this mind be. For Christ
and our God did not look to his own, but to the advantage and salvation of
all. For this reason he humbles himself through incarnation, and raises all of
us through his own humiliation. He therefore who humbles himself becomes an
imitator of Christ, although he himself is God by nature and high according to
essence, while those high among men acquired as an addition the height in them
from robbery, from greed, since nature at least is common and equal and of
like honor among all men. If then he who is God by nature and high by nature
humbled himself for us, we who are by nature of like honor and likewise lowly,
ought we not to humble ourselves to one another and to know together our own
measures? And if we are humbled, God also highly exalts us, granting us the
things which eye has not seen and ear has not heard and which have not arisen
upon a man's heart. For blessed, he says, are the poor in spirit, for theirs
is the kingdom of the heavens. And yet even if this were not so, from the
thing itself it is high and great to be counted worthy to become imitators of
Christ. Phil 2:12-14 With fear and trembling. Well said; for each must fear
and tremble in working out his own salvation, lest at some time, being
tripped, he fall from it. For many are the trials and many those who plot,
seeing that even the very practicing of virtue has for some issued in the
contrary, having become a ground of madness and pride. One must therefore on
account of these things, fearing and trembling, work out one's own salvation.
For if God, seeing the contest and the zeal with fear, does not take hold
together, even the willing itself will not issue in a good end, and the
working would not be judged a good deed. For Cain wished to bring a sacrifice
to God, having then wished a thing especially praiseworthy, and he worked, for
he sacrificed, but neither was accepted by God; for it was not with fear and
trembling. Wherefore the divine did not take hold together, nor did these
things issue for him in a good end. And he reasonably says that God works; for
taking hold together with us he himself is the one who perfects and as it were
forms our both willing and working. And "for it is God who works" and so forth
can also have been said as a cause of "without murmurings and reasonings." For
why do you murmur, he says, as if toiling? God is the one who works all
things; and why do you reason about the toilsome of virtue, having God as
fellow-worker? 626 Phil 2:17-18 But if I am even poured out upon the
sacrifice. This passage is either to be taken as those before us have
interpreted, or also thus: but if I am even sacrificed, the sacrifice and
liturgy of your faith which I have ministered as priest to God, I rejoice
indeed as truly (for a teacher's joy is the perfection of those discipled) and
I rejoice with you, because you too, being perfected, enjoy a greatest joy; so
that I on this account rejoice and rejoice with you. And you also ought to
rejoice, because you come to be in perfection, and to rejoice with me, because
you have gladdened the teacher. And perhaps "but if I am even poured out"
seems to introduce the thought that if he were not being poured out upon their
sacrifice, he would have been grieved; for the figure of the speech hints at
this. And we say that this too is not absurd; for if he himself previously
said that he considered it more necessary that he remain in the flesh on
account of the progress and perfection of the disciples, he would wholly have
been grieved, being sacrificed, not yet having perfected them nor having their
faith as a sacrifice and liturgy to bring to Christ. And otherwise: he would
not simply have chosen to be sacrificed, unless on the ground that he had
become a zealous and warm herald of the faith, and on the ground that he had
so fitted the disciples that he was able to bring their faith as a sacrifice
and liturgy to God; for he who is sacrificed for these things, this man as
truly rejoices and exults being sacrificed; for no one chooses in vain to be
slaughtered. And it is possible also thus: I am poured out now in the trials
and the afflictions (for elsewhere too he says: I die daily); but if I am even
sacrificed, he says, in the afflictions and the trials (for bonds were lying
about him), yet upon the sacrifice and liturgy of your faith I rejoice, and so
forth. Or also thus: yet because upon the sacrifice, that is, for your sake
and for the preaching unto you, I rejoice, and so forth. Phil 2:30 Because for
the work of Christ. Well does he call it a work of Christ; for if he who
receives the disciples receives Christ himself (for he who receives you, he
says, receives me); and he who serves the disciples in anything serves Christ.
And otherwise it is a work of Christ: because it is a commandment of Christ to
lay down one's own soul for friends. And if for friends, how much more for
teachers? 627 Phil 3:1 Rejoice in the Lord. And still if the apostle has
restored you, and has been sent again to you, and I have been delivered from
the grief over him, and your lack has been filled up, you ought to rejoice
with a spiritual joy and one worthy of the Lord. Phil 3:4 Although I having
confidence. If I wished, he says, to boast as they do, I too rather have
confidence in flesh, that is: I too was able, as they, to boast upon the
circumcision of my flesh and more than they. Phil 3:7 But what things were
gains to me. He calls gain the things collected for him from the law; so that
he does not speak ill of the law, as those from Marcion babble. And if he has
also considered them loss, he does not say this simply, which would have been
of one speaking ill of the law, but he calls them loss by the comparison and
setting beside Christ, which rather issues in a praise of the law, that he
does not otherwise dare to call it loss except by the setting beside and
comparison toward Christ. Phil 3:8 But indeed I also count all things. If you
count all things loss, O Paul, even the things of the law bodily observed,
because of the surpassing of the knowledge of Christ, how do you say
immediately: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things? For to say "I
have suffered loss" is not to cast away a loss (for this is rather to gain, to
be freed from a loss) but rather signifies a casting away of certain
profitable things. How then, the things you have counted loss, putting these
away, do you say that I have suffered loss? And we say that he did not simply
count these loss, but toward a comparison of the more perfect knowledge
according to Christ, since in itself he also considered them gain; wherefore
"I count loss" is toward the comparison of the better things, and "I have
suffered loss" in itself toward the nature of the law. Or this too must be
said, that the divine Paul does not care about the choice of Hellenic words,
he looks only to make clear the subject. For just as elsewhere, wishing to
make clear the authority of death against us, he was in no way 628 precise
about the diction, but instead of saying "he tyrannized" or "he forced," he
said: "he reigned," signifying by a fair-sounding name his ill-sounding
tyranny, so also now the casting away of the things that bring loss, which is
fair-sounding, he hinted at by a diction not fair-sounding, saying: "I have
suffered loss." Phil 3:13a I do not reckon myself to have laid hold, at once
through the example according to himself teaching them also to be
moderate-minded and not to be puffed up as having laid hold. Phil 3:13b But
one thing, the things behind. The "but one thing" is either to be thought said
by ellipsis, as those before us have taken it (for he also abounds in this
figure), so that there is lacking: but one thing I care for, one thing I
practice and am anxious about. What is this? The things behind, he says, and
so forth. Either then it is to be taken thus, or it rather falls under another
figure, that from the common (for the divine Paul is abundant also in this),
so that "I reckon to have laid hold" is from the common, as: I reckon to have
laid hold of nothing except one thing. And what is it that you reckon to have
laid hold of? That forgetting the things behind, and stretching forward to the
things before, I pursue according to the mark, and so forth. For he needed to
have laid hold, that he pursues according to the mark, and the course is not
vain for him. Wherefore also elsewhere he says: so I run as not uncertainly,
so I box as not beating the air, and again: so run, that you may lay hold.
Phil 3:16-17a Only unto that which we have attained. For the present, he says,
until he reveal to you the true, let us not lose what we have laid hold of.
And how is it lost? If we are negligent both of the former zeal and are turned
aside from the course. But one must walk by the same rule, that is, to keep
the same contest, to exhibit the same manner, to think the same both
concerning the manner of life and concerning the dogmas; for just as, having
altered any of the dogmas, he has overthrown all things even the things
before, so also of the manner of life, having changed any and relaxed and not
keeping the same exactness, he has also ruined the things that went before.
Then, lest he seem to command something hard to accomplish and difficult, to
be exercised together with virtues and piety through the whole of life, he
adds: become imitators of me. Most easy, 629 he says, I demand nothing
impossible; I too am a man of like passions; as you see me, so also yourselves
contend. To walk by the same rule, to think the same. This passage is either
of exhortation or of prayer. But if of exhortation, "must" is lacking: you
must walk by the same rule, think the same; or you ought to walk by the same
rule, you ought to think the same. And if prayer, "he will reveal" will be
taken from the common, as: he will reveal to you to walk by the same rule, and
so forth; for he knows that without divine inclination nothing is
accomplished. Phil 3:17b-18 Mark those who so walk. The "mark" now is not
instead of "guard against," but instead of the example; and make these a mark
for yourselves and have them. "To walk" here he takes simply as to live and to
conduct one's citizenship. Since, he says, many walk, for a distinction of
those who walk rightly and not, mark who walk according to the type which we
walked, and follow these. Phil 3:21 Who will transform. He sets a strong and
indisputable faith that the body of our humiliation will wholly be
transformed, if only we will, so as to become conformed to his glory. For if,
he says, our Lord Jesus Christ comes for this, who will dispute that this will
wholly be? Then do you want also another proof? He wishes to subject all
things to himself; for he must, he says, reign until all things are subjected
to him, and: and when all things are subjected to him. If then the Son himself
reigns, in order that he may subject all things to himself, and all things
will wholly be subjected to him, clearly also the body of our humiliation, if
only we ourselves will, will be subjected to him. And it is subjected, when it
bears in itself nothing factious and hostile toward the one who subjected, but
all things concordant and in harmony both in faith and in manner of life. And
when the body of our humiliation is thus subjected to him, it is transformed,
clearly, from the humiliation according to sin and corruption, and becomes
conformed to the body of his glory. Vain is the fear. 630 And one must not say
that the Son will subject all things to the body of the Son, but that he
himself to himself, he who said: all authority has been given to me in heaven
and on earth, as also Paul says elsewhere: for he must reign until he put all
the enemies under his feet, and: and when all things are subjected to him. The
divine oracles therefore saying these things clearly, how must one not say
that our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ himself is also the one transforming the
body of our humiliation, and the one working the subjection of all things unto
him? And while the Son subjects, the Father also subjects; for the things of
the Father and of the Son are common. Phil 4:11-18 Not that according to want.
I said that I rejoiced in the Lord greatly that you have revived to think on
my behalf. But do not suppose, he says, that I said this on account of the
want which I had; for I, he says, have learned, and so forth. I am, he says,
exercised in all things, and not even being in want do I take it ill. Yet I
rejoiced over you, because you did well; for having made yourselves partakers
of my affliction you would also partake of the prizes. For this reason I
rejoiced greatly. Since he said that I rejoiced greatly, because you did well,
having become partakers with me of the affliction, he reminds them again that
from the beginning he was laying down this grace and benefaction for them, the
receiving from them the things toward need, and through this preparing them as
partakers both of the afflictions through Christ and of the prizes stored up
upon these. For do not suppose, he says, that now, because I have come to be
far off and in a place in which the preaching has not been widened as among
you, nor are there those so eagerly supplying the things toward need, for this
reason I accepted your supply and counted you worthy to become partakers both
of my affliction and of the recompense. For even while still near and having
many, if I wished, who would supply, I preferred you; for even going out from
Macedonia and in Thessalonica I counted you worthy to be suppliers of my need.
And do not suppose that I say these things and exalt this deed of yours, in
order that you may still and still supply and minister to my needs; for so far
am I from saying them for this reason, that behold I say: I have all things,
instead of: I have been delivered toward all the things toward need. And "I
have been delivered" as: I no longer have need of these. Then, lest he grieve
them as disdaining: not so, he says, but that I abound, and so forth.
