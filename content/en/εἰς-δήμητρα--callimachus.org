To Demeter edited by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff

As the basket comes down, cry out, you women: /Hail, Demeter,
much-nourishing, lady of many measures/. You who are uninitiated, watch from
the ground the basket coming down; do not look from the roof or from on high,
no child, no woman, nor she who has let her hair fall, nor when we spit from
dry mouths, still unfed. Hesperus has looked out from the clouds (when is he
coming?), Hesperus, who alone persuaded Demeter to drink, when she was
following the unknown tracks of her stolen girl. Lady, how could your feet
carry you as far as the sunsets, as far as the Black men and the place of the
golden apples? You did not drink, nor eat in that time, nor bathe. Three
times you crossed silver-eddying Achelous, and as many times you passed each
of the ever-flowing rivers, and three times you sat on the ground at
Callichorus' well, parched and thirsty; you did not eat, nor bathe. Let us
not, let us not tell these things that brought a tear to Deo; better, how
she gave cities ordinances they were glad of; better, how she first cut the
straw and the sacred handfuls of ears and set them among the oxen to tread,
when Triptolemus was learning the good art; better, how (that someone too
may shun transgression)

Not yet the Cnidian land; they still lived in holy Dotium, and the Pelasgians
made her a fair grove, thick with trees; an arrow would scarcely have gone
through it. There was pine, there were great elms, pears too, and fair
sweet-apples; and water like amber welled from the channels. The goddess
loved that place as she loved Eleusis, and Triopas as she loved Enna. But
when the kindly spirit grew angry with the sons of Triopas, then worse
counsel laid hold of Erysichthon. He dashed out with twenty servants, all in
their prime, all giant men, enough to lift a whole city; he armed them with
axes and hatchets, and shameless they ran into Demeter's grove. There was a
poplar, a great tree that reached the sky, on which the nymphs used to sport
toward midday; this was the first struck, and it cried a cry of pain to the
others. Demeter knew that her sacred wood was hurting, and said in anger
/Who is cutting my fair trees?/ At once she took the form of Nicippe, whom
the city had set as its public priestess; she took garlands and a poppy in
her hand, and she had a key on her shoulder. She spoke, trying to calm the
evil and shameless man: /Child, whoever you are who cut trees set apart for
the gods, child, rest; child so longed for by your parents, stop, and turn
your servants back, lest lady Demeter be angry, whose holy grove you are
stripping/. Then he looked at her more fiercely than a lioness fresh from
birth looks at a huntsman in the Tmarian mountains, whose eye they say is
the grimmest of all: /Stand off/, he said, /or I will plant my great axe in
your flesh. These trees will make my roofed hall, where I shall always hold
feasts enough to please my companions' hearts/. So the boy spoke, and
Nemesis wrote down the evil voice. Demeter was angry beyond telling, and she
showed herself the goddess: her steps were on the earth, but her head
reached Olympus. They were half-dead when they saw the lady, and they sprang
away at once, dropping the bronze among the oaks. She let the others be, for
they followed under a master's hand by necessity, but she answered the cruel
lord: /Yes, yes, build your house, dog, dog, where you shall make your
feasts; for frequent banquets shall be yours hereafter/. Having said so
much, she made misery for Erysichthon.

At once she cast into him a hard and savage hunger, burning and strong, and
a great disease wore him down. Wretched man: as much as he ate, so much he
wanted again. Twenty prepared the feast, twelve drew the wine; for Dionysus
is angered as much as Demeter is; † for Dionysus too shared Demeter's anger.
His parents, ashamed, sent him neither to private feasts nor to shared meals,
and they found every pretext. The sons of Ormenus came inviting him to the
games of Itonian Athena; then the mother refused: /He is not at home, for
yesterday he went to Crannon to claim a debt of a hundred oxen/. Polyxo came,
mother of Actorion, preparing a marriage for her child, calling both Triopas
and his son. Sick at heart, she answered her, shedding a tear: /Triopas
is coming to you, but a boar drove Erysichthon over the fair valleys of
Pindus, and he has lain nine days/. Wretched mother, so fond of your child,
what lie did you not tell? Someone was giving a banquet: /Erysichthon is
among strangers/. Someone was taking a bride: /A discus struck Erysichthon/,
or /he fell from his horses/, or /he is counting flocks on Othrys/. Then he
feasted indoors all day and ate countless things, all of them; and his belly
leapt out, worse the more he ate, and all the food ran down thankless, in
vain, into a gulf like the sea. As snow on Mimas, as a wax doll in the sun,
and even more than these † he wasted away down to the sinews; only sinews
and bones were left to the wretched man. The mother wept, the two sisters
groaned heavily, and so did the nurse who had suckled him, and the ten
slave-women, again and again. Triopas himself laid his hands on his gray
hair, calling on Poseidon who did not hear, with words like these: /You are
no true father; look on this third scion of yours, if I am of your stock and
of Aeolian Canace, and this wretched child was born of me. Would that my
hands had buried him, struck by Apollo; but now an evil hunger sits in his
eyes. Either take this hard disease from him or take him yourself and feed
him; for my tables have given out. The stalls are empty, and my folds of
livestock are already empty, for already the cooks have refused/. But they
even unyoked the mules from the great wagons, and he ate the cow that his
mother was raising for Hestia, and the prize horse and the war-horse, and
the cat that small creatures used to fear. As long as wealth still lay in
the house of Triopas, only the household chambers knew the evil. But when
his teeth had emptied the deep house dry, then the king's son sat at the
crossroads, begging for scraps and the refuse thrown from the feast.
Demeter, let not that man be a friend to me who is hateful to you, nor even
one who shares my wall; I hate a bad neighbor. Sing, maidens, and you
mothers cry in answer: /Hail, Demeter, much-nourishing, lady of many
measures/. And as four white-maned horses lead the basket, so for us the
great goddess, wide-ruling, bringing white spring, white summer and winter,
will come, and autumn too, and will keep us into another year. And as we
tread the city unshod and without headbands, so we shall always have our
feet and our heads unharmed. And as the basket-bearers carry
winnowing-baskets full of gold, so may we taste gold without stint. As far
as the city hall, those who are uninitiated; but those who have completed
the rites, those under sixty, are to go with her as far as the goddess. The
women heavy with child, and whoever stretches a hand to Eileithyia, and
whoever is in pain: so far is enough, as far as their own knee can go; and
to them Deo will give all things in full measure even as they come to the
temple. Hail, goddess, and save this city in concord and in prosperity, and
bring from the fields all that comes home; feed the cattle, bring flocks,
bring the ear of grain, bring the harvest, and feed peace as well, so that
he who plowed may reap. Be gracious to me, thrice-called, great queen among
goddesses.
