* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

What the pontiffs, who had the power of writing history, recorded as having
happened after the death of Romulus, when the rule of the Roman city was still
young, so that men might marvel at an interregnum while, after a good prince,
another good one was being sought: this same thing was done after Aurelian,
through a contest between the senate and the Roman army that was not jealous
or gloomy but grateful and pious, and it lasted six full months. In many ways,
however, this occasion differed from that earlier affair. For first of all,
when the interregnum began after Romulus, interrexes were still appointed, and
that whole year was assigned to the hundred senators, five days or four or
three at a time, so that those who were able served as interrexes, each for
his turn only. Thus it came about that the interregnum lasted even more than a
year, lest anyone of equal rank should remain without a share in the Roman
command. Add to this that even under the consuls and the military tribunes
vested with consular power, whenever an interregnum began, there were
interrexes, and the Roman republic was never so empty of that title that no
interrex was created for at least two days or three. I see that it may be
objected to me that among our ancestors the curule magistrates were absent
from the state for four years; but there were tribunes of the plebs with
tribunician power, which is the greatest part of the royal command. Yet it is
not recorded that there were no interrexes at that time; on the contrary, the
more truthful historians have made it clear that consuls were later created by
the interrexes, to hold the elections of the remaining magistrates.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

And so the senate and people of Rome endured what was rare and hard: that for
six months, while a good man was being sought, the state had no emperor.

What concord among the soldiers! How great the quiet of the people! How
weighty the authority of the senate!

No tyrant arose anywhere. The whole world was governed under the judgement of
the senate and the soldiers and the Roman people. They did not need the fear
of any prince, or of tribunician power, to act rightly; they feared
themselves, and that is the best thing in life.

Still, the cause of so happy a delay must be told, and it must be set down in
particular in the public records: that same self-restraint, which later ages
of the human race must wonder at, so that those who lust for kingdoms may
learn not to seize power but to deserve it. After Aurelian had been killed by
treachery, as was written in the previous book, through the cunning of a most
worthless slave and the error of the soldiers (among whom any tale carries
great weight, provided they hear it in anger, often drunk, and almost always
without counsel), when all had returned to their right mind and had been
sternly rebuked by the army, they began to ask who among them all ought to be
made prince. Then the army, which used to create an emperor in haste, out of
hatred for the men of the moment, sent letters to the senate, already
mentioned in the previous book, asking that they choose a prince from their
own order. But the senate, knowing that princes chosen by itself did not
please the soldiers, referred the matter back to the troops, and while this
was done again and again, the sixth month was completed.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

It matters, however, that it be known how Tacitus was made emperor. On the
seventh day before the Kalends of October, when the most distinguished order
had taken its seats in the Pompilian Senate-house, the consul Velius
Cornificius Gordianus said:

/We shall bring before you, conscript fathers, what we have often brought
before. An emperor must be chosen, since the army cannot rightly stand longer
without a prince, and because necessity also drives us. For the Germans are
said to have broken the frontier beyond the Rhine and to have seized cities
that are strong, famous, rich, and powerful. Even if nothing is reported of
Persian movements, consider how light the minds of the Syrians are, that they
would rather have even women reign than endure our austerity. What of Africa?
What of Illyricum? What of Egypt and the armies of all those regions? How long
do we believe they can hold together without a prince? Come then, conscript
fathers, and name a prince. Either the army will accept the man you choose,
or, if it refuses him, it will make another./

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

After this, when Tacitus, the consular who spoke first, was about to speak,
though it was uncertain what he wished to say, the whole senate cried out:

/Tacitus Augustus, may the god keep you. We choose you, we make you prince, we
commit to you the care of the republic and of the world. Take the command by
the senate's authority. It belongs to your place, your life, your mind. You
deserve it. Rightly is the leader of the senate made Augustus. Rightly is the
man who speaks first made emperor. Who rules better than a man of weight?
Who rules better than a man of letters? May it be good, fortunate, and
wholesome. You have long been a private citizen; you know how you ought to
rule, you who have borne other princes. You know how you ought to rule, you
who have judged other princes./

But he said:

/I wonder, conscript fathers, that you wish to make an old man prince in the
place of Aurelian, the bravest of emperors. Look at these limbs, whether they
can throw a javelin, whirl a spear, make the shields thunder, or ride often as
a pattern for training the soldier. We barely fulfill the duties of the
senate; we barely deliver the opinions to which our place binds us. Look more
carefully at the age you would send from the bedchamber and the shade into
frosts and heats. And do you believe the soldiers will approve an old man as
emperor? See that you do not give the republic a prince other than the one you
wish, and that this alone does not begin to harm me, that you have chosen me
with one voice./

After this the senate's acclamations were these:

/Trajan too came to power as an old man./

They said it ten times.

/Hadrian too came to power as an old man./

They said it ten times.

/Antoninus too came to power as an old man./

They said it ten times.

/And you have read: "And the hoary chin of the Roman king."/

They said it ten times.

/Who rules better than an old man?/

They said it ten times.

/We make you emperor, not a soldier./

They said it twenty times.

/Do you command; let the soldiers fight./

They said it thirty times.

/You have prudence and a good brother./

They said it ten times.

/Severus said the head rules, not the feet./

They said it thirty times.

/We choose your mind, not your body./

They said it twenty times.

/Tacitus Augustus, may the gods keep you!/

Nicomachus spoke in these words:

/Always, conscript fathers, this distinguished order has consulted for the
republic rightly and wisely, and from no people of the world has firmer wisdom
ever been looked for. Yet never has a weightier or wiser opinion been spoken
in this sanctuary. We have made an older man prince, a man who may watch over
all as a father. From him nothing unripe, nothing hasty, nothing harsh is to
be feared. All things serious, all things grave, and as if the republic itself
were giving the order, are to be expected. For he knows what kind of prince he
has always wished for himself, and he cannot show us anything other than what
he himself desired and wanted.

Indeed, if you care to recall those old prodigies, I mean the Neros and
Heliogabali and Commodi, or rather the always Incommodi, surely those vices
were not so much of the men as of their ages. May the gods avert boy princes
and beardless fathers of the fatherland, whose writing-masters still hold
their hands for signing, whom sweets and hoops and every childish pleasure
invite to grant consulships. What reason (heaven help us) is there in having
an emperor who does not know how to care for his fame, who does not know what
the republic is, who fears his tutor, looks back to his nurse, lies under the
blows and terror of the schoolmaster's rods, and makes consuls, generals, and
judges of men whose lives, merits, ages, families, and deeds he does not know?
But why, conscript fathers, do I go on so long? Let us rather rejoice that we
have an old man as prince than repeat those things which were more than
tearful for those who endured them. I therefore give and hold thanks to the
immortal gods, and indeed for the whole republic, and I address you, Tacitus
Augustus, asking, beseeching, and freely demanding for our common country and
its laws, that you do not make your little children heirs of the Roman command
if fate overtakes you too soon, and that you do not leave the republic and the
conscript fathers and the Roman people as you would leave your little farm,
your tenants, and your slaves. Look around you, then. Imitate the Nervas, the
Trajans, the Hadrians. Great is the glory of a dying prince who loves the
republic more than his sons./

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

By this speech Tacitus himself was deeply moved, and the whole senatorial
order was shaken, and at once the cry was:

/All, all./

Then they went to the Campus Martius, where he mounted the assembly platform.
There Aelius Cesettianus, the prefect of the city, spoke thus:

/You, most sacred soldiers, and you, most hallowed Quirites, have a prince
whom the senate has chosen by the judgement of all the armies: I mean Tacitus,
a most august man, so that he who has thus far helped the republic by his
opinions may now help it by his orders and decrees./

The people cried, "Most fortunate Tacitus Augustus, may the gods keep you,"
and the rest of what is usually said.

Here it must not be passed over in silence that many have recorded that
Tacitus was named prince while absent and staying in Campania. It is true, and
I cannot hide it. For when a rumor arose that he was to be made emperor, he
withdrew and was at Baiae for two months. But he was brought back from there
and took part in this decree of the senate, as if he were truly a private
citizen and one who truly refused

the command. And lest anyone think I have rashly trusted some Greek or Latin
writer, there is in the Ulpian Library, in the sixth cupboard, an ivory book
in which this senate's decree is written out, and Tacitus himself subscribed
it with his own hand. For a long time those senate decrees that concerned the
princes were written in ivory books.

From there he set out to the armies, and there too, when he first mounted the
platform, Moesius Gallicanus, the praetorian prefect, spoke in these words:

/Most sacred fellow-soldiers, the senate has given the prince you asked for.
That most noble order has obeyed the commands and the will of the camp. I may
not say more among you now that the emperor is already present. Therefore hear
with respect the man himself, who ought to protect us, as he speaks./

After this Tacitus Augustus said:

/Trajan too came to power as an old man, but he was chosen by one man. I, most
sacred fellow-soldiers, have been judged worthy of this name first by you, who
know how to approve princes, and then by the most distinguished senate. I will
take care, I will strive, I will bring it about that you do not lack, if not
brave deeds, at least counsels worthy of you and of an emperor./

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

After this he promised pay and a donative according to custom, and he gave his
first speech to the senate in this form:

/May it be granted me, conscript fathers, so to rule the empire that it may be
clear I was chosen by you, as I have resolved to do all things by your
judgement and power. It is yours, therefore, to order and ratify what seems
worthy of you, worthy of a well-disciplined army, and worthy of the Roman
people./

In the same speech he decreed that a golden statue of Aurelian be set on the
Capitol, and a silver one in the Senate-house, and one in the Temple of the
Sun, and one in the Forum of the deified Trajan. But the golden one was not
set up; only the silver ones were dedicated. In the same speech he provided
that if anyone mixed bronze with silver, publicly or privately, or silver with
gold, or lead with bronze, it should be a capital offense, with confiscation
of goods. In the same speech he provided that slaves should not be questioned
against the lives of their masters, not even in a case of treason. He added
that all should have Aurelian painted, and he ordered a temple of the deified
emperors to be made, in which there should be statues of the good princes, so
that on their birthdays and at the Parilia and on the Kalends of January and
at the Vows offerings should be set before them. In the same speech he asked
for the consulship for his brother Florian and did not obtain it, because the
senate had already closed all the terms for suffect consuls. The senate is
said to have taken great joy in its freedom, because the consulship he had
asked for his brother was denied him. He is even reported to have said,

/The senate knows what prince it has made./

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

He made public the estate he held in revenues, two hundred and eighty million
sesterces. The money he had gathered at home he turned to the soldiers' pay.
He used the same togas and tunics he had used as a private citizen. He forbade
hired lodgings to stand within the city, though indeed he could not keep this
up for long. He ordered all baths to be closed before lamp-time, lest any
sedition arise by night. He ordered that Cornelius Tacitus, the writer of
Augustan history, because he said that man was his ancestor, be placed in all
the libraries; and, lest the book perish through neglect of its housing, he
ordered that it be copied ten times each year at public expense and placed in
the libraries. He forbade all-silk clothing to all men. He commanded that his
own house be pulled down and ordered public baths to be built on that site at
his private cost. He gave the people of Ostia, from his own means, a hundred
Numidian columns of twenty-three feet. He assigned the estates he held in
Mauretania to the repair of the Capitol. The table silver he had owned as a
private citizen he dedicated to the service of the banquets held in the
temples. He freed all his city slaves of both sexes, yet within a hundred, so
as not to seem to go beyond the Caninian law.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He himself was of the most sparing life, so that he never drank a sextarius of
wine in a whole day, and often less than a hemina. His dinner was one cock,
with a pig's cheek added, and eggs. Above all the vegetables served in plenty
he indulged without restraint in lettuces, for he used to say that by that
outlay he was buying sleep. He sought out the more bitter foods. He used the
baths rarely and was for that reason stronger in old age. He took great
delight in the variety and elaborate work of glassware. He never ate bread
unless it was dry, and that seasoned with salt and other things. He was most
skilled in building, eager for marbles, of senatorial polish, and fond of the
hunt. In short, he never loaded his table except with country fare. He did not
serve a pheasant except on his own birthday and those of his household and on
the greatest feast days. He always had his sacrificial victims brought back
home and ordered his people to eat them. He did not allow his wife to use
gems, and he likewise forbade garments studded with gold. For he himself is
said to have been the one who moved Aurelian to put gold away from clothes and
ceilings and hides. Many things of this kind are told, but it would be long to
set them down. If anyone wishes to know all about this man, let him read
Suetonius Optatianus, who wrote his life at length. As an old man he read tiny
letters to a wonder, and he never let a night pass in which he did not write
or read something, except the day after the Kalends.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Nor should it be passed over, and it must often be made known, that the
senate's joy was so great, because the care of choosing a prince had returned
to the most distinguished order, that thanksgivings were decreed and a
hecatomb promised. Then the senators one by one wrote to their own people, and
not only to their own but even to foreigners, and letters besides were sent to
the provinces:

/that all allies and all nations should know that the republic had returned to
its ancient state, and that the senate chose the princes, or rather that the
senate itself had been made prince, that laws were to be sought from the
senate, that barbarian kings would make supplication to the senate, and that
peace and wars were to be handled by the senate's authority./

And so that nothing should be lacking for knowledge, I have placed many
letters of this kind at the end of the book, to be read through, as I judge,
with eagerness and without disgust.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And his first care, once he was made emperor, was this: that he should put to
death all who had killed Aurelian, the good and the bad, though that man had
already been avenged. And since many barbarians had burst out from the
Maeotis, he crushed these same men by counsel and courage. The Maeotians
themselves were gathering as if they had come together at Aurelian's summons
for the Persian war, to give help to our men if need required. Marcus Tullius
says it is more splendid to tell how a man administered the consulship than
how he obtained it; but in this man it was a splendid thing that he obtained
the command with such glory. He achieved nothing great in the holding of it,
however, because of the shortness of the time. For he was killed by a military
plot, as some say, in the sixth month; as others say, he died of disease. Yet
it is agreed that, worn down by factions, he failed in mind and spirit. This
same man ordered that the month of September be called Tacitus, because in
that month he was both born and made emperor. His brother Florian succeeded
him in power, of whom a few things must be set down.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

This man was Tacitus' full brother, who after his brother seized the command,
not by the senate's authority but of his own motion, as if the empire were
hereditary, though he knew that Tacitus had been put under oath in the senate
that, when he began to die, he would make not his own children but some best
man prince. In the end he barely held power for two months and was killed at
Tarsus by soldiers who had heard that Probus was emperor, the man the whole
army had chosen. So great was Probus in war that the senate desired him, the
soldier chose him, and the Roman people itself asked for him with shouts.
Florian was also an imitator of his brother's ways, yet not in every point,
for his frugal brother rebuked the lavishness in him, and this very lust for
ruling showed that he was of other habits than his brother.

Two princes, then, arose from one house, of whom one ruled six months and the
other barely two, like certain interrexes between Aurelian and Probus, named
princes after an interregnum.

:PROPERTIES:

There were two statues of them at Interamna, of marble, thirty feet high,
because empty tombs of theirs were set up there on their own ground; but they
were struck down by lightning and so crushed that they lie scattered limb from
limb. At that time the soothsayers answered that someday a Roman emperor would
come from their family, whether through a woman or through a man, who would
give judges to the Parthians and Persians, who would hold the Franks and
Alamanni under Roman laws, who would leave no barbarian throughout all Africa,
who would set a governor over the Taprobanians, who would send a proconsul to
the island of Iuverna, who would judge all the Sarmatians, who would make his
own all the earth that the Ocean girds, when all nations had been taken, and
would afterward give the command back to the senate and live by the ancient
laws, himself to live a hundred and twenty years and to die without an heir.
They said, moreover, that he would come a thousand years from the day the
lightning was hurled and the statues broken. This was no great cleverness in
the soothsayers, who said that such a prince would come after a thousand
years, making a promise when such a history can scarcely remain, because if
they had foretold it after a hundred years, their lies might perhaps be
caught. I have thought these things should be put into the volume, however,
lest anyone reading me should not believe that I had read them.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Tacitus barely gave a largess to the Roman people within six months. His image
was set up in the house of the Quintilii, fivefold on one panel, in which he
appears once in a toga, once in a military cloak, once in armor, once in a
Greek mantle, and once in hunting dress. A writer of epigrams played on this
so as to say:

/I do not recognize the old man in armor, nor in the military cloak/

among other things,

/but I recognize him in the toga./

There were many children of both Florian and Tacitus, whose descendants, I
believe, are waiting for the thousandth year. Many epigrams have been written
on them, in which the soothsayers are mocked for promising empire. These are
the things I remember I have found worthy of record concerning the life of
Tacitus and Florian.

Now we must take up Probus, a man outstanding at home and abroad, a man to be
set above Aurelian, Trajan, Hadrian, the Antonines, Alexander, and Claudius,
because in those men the gifts were mixed, in this man all the chief ones were
joined. After Tacitus, by the judgement of all good men, he was made emperor
and governed a world at its most peaceful, when barbarian nations had been
destroyed and also very many pretenders who arose in his time. Of him it was
said that he deserved to be called Probus even if Probus had not been his
name. Many even report that he was promised in the Sibylline Books, and that
if he had lived longer the world would have no barbarians. I thought I should
give this foretaste of Probus in the lives of others, lest a day, an hour, a
moment should claim something for itself against me by fatal necessity, and I
should perish with Probus still unnamed. Now, since I have for the time
satisfied my zeal, I will close this volume, judging that my zeal and desire
have been satisfied.

These were the omens of empire for Tacitus. A certain fanatic in the Temple of
Silvanus, with his limbs stretched out, cried,

/Silent purple, silent purple,/

and that a seventh time; which afterward was counted toward an omen. The wine
with which Tacitus was about to pour a libation in the temple of Hercules at
Fundi suddenly turned purple. A vine that bore white Aminnean grapes, in the
year he earned the command, bore purple ones. Very many things turned purple.
These were the omens of his death: his father's tomb opened of itself when the
doors burst apart; his mother's shade showed herself by day to both Tacitus
and Florian as if she were alive, for they were said to have been born of
different fathers; in the household shrine all the gods fell, whether by
earthquake or by some accident. The image of Apollo that they worshipped was
found laid on a couch from the highest gable, without the hand of any man. But
how much further shall we go? There are others by whom such things may be
told. Let us keep ourselves for Probus and for Probus' famous deeds.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

And since I promised that I would set down some letters that showed the
senate's joy when Tacitus was made prince, I will make an end of writing when
these have been added. Public letters:

/The most distinguished senate greets the council of Carthage. May it be good,
fortunate, happy, and wholesome for the republic and the Roman world: the
right of conferring the command, of naming a prince, of proclaiming an
Augustus, has returned to us. Bring before us, therefore, the matters that are
great. Every appeal will belong to the prefect of the city, such as arises
from the proconsuls and from the ordinary judges. In this we believe that your
dignity too has returned to its ancient state, since this order is the first,
and by recovering its own force it preserves the right of the rest./

Another letter:

/The most distinguished senate to the council of the Treveri. As you are free
and always have been, we believe you rejoice. The judgement of creating a
prince has returned to the senate, and at the same time the whole right of
appeal to the urban prefecture has been decreed./

In the same way it was written to the people of Antioch, Aquileia, Milan,
Alexandria, Thessalonica, Corinth, and Athens.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

The private letters were these:

/Autronius Tiberianus to his father Autronius Justus, greeting. Now, holy
father, you ought to have been present in the most distinguished senate, now
to speak your opinion, when the authority of the most distinguished order has
grown so far that, with the republic returned to its ancient state, we give
the princes, we make the emperors, we in short proclaim the Augusti. See to
it, then, that you recover, so that you may take part in the ancient
Senate-house. We have recovered the proconsular right. Appeals of all powers
and all ranks have returned to the prefect of the city./

Likewise another:

/Claudius Sapilianus to his uncle Cereius Maecianus, greeting. We have
obtained, holy father, what we have always wished. The senate has returned to
its ancient state. We make the princes; the powers belong to our order. Thanks
to the Roman army, and truly Roman; it has given back to us the power we
always had. Cast aside your Baian and Puteolan retreats; give yourself to the
city, give yourself to the Senate-house. Rome flourishes, the whole republic
flourishes. We give emperors, we make princes; we who have begun to make them
can also forbid them. A word to the wise is enough./

It would be long to join together all the letters I have found and read. I say
only this: that all the senators were so carried away with joy that in their
houses they all slew white victims, often uncovered the ancestral images, sat
in white, gave more costly banquets, and believed that antiquity had been
restored to them.
