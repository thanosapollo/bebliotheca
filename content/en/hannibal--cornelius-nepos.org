* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Hannibal, son of Hamilcar, a Carthaginian. If it is true, as no one doubts,
that the Roman people surpassed all nations in valor, then it cannot be denied
that Hannibal outstripped the rest of the commanders in skill by as much as
the Roman people outstrips every nation in courage. For whenever he met them
in Italy, he always came off victorious. Had the jealousy of his own citizens
not weakened him at home, it seems he could have overcome the Romans. But the
carping of many defeated the excellence of one man.

He kept his father's hatred of the Romans, left to him as if by inheritance,
so faithfully that he gave up his life before he gave up that hatred. Even
after he had been driven from his country and had to depend on foreign
resources, he never ceased, in spirit, to wage war with the Romans.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

I pass over Philip, whom Hannibal made an enemy of the Romans even while he
himself was far away. The most powerful king of those times was Antiochus.
Hannibal fired him with such a hunger for war that he tried to carry arms
against Italy even from the Red Sea. When Roman envoys came to sound out the
king's mind and, by secret intrigue, to bring Hannibal under suspicion, as if
they had corrupted him and he now held views other than before, they
succeeded. Hannibal learned of it and saw that he was being shut out of inner
counsels. When he found an opening, he went to the king. After speaking at
length of his own loyalty and his hatred of the Romans, he added this: "My
father Hamilcar," he said, "when I was a little boy, no more than nine years
old, was setting out from Carthage as commander for Spain, and he sacrificed
victims to Jupiter Best and Greatest. While that rite was still going on, he
asked me whether I wanted to go with him to camp. I accepted gladly and began
to beg him not to hesitate to take me. Then he said, 'I will, if you give me
the pledge I ask.' At once he led me to the altar where he had begun the
sacrifice, sent the others away, and ordered me to take hold of it and swear
that I would never be a friend to the Romans. I have kept that oath to my
father down to this age, so that no one ought to doubt that I will be of the
same mind for the time that remains. Therefore if you mean to think of the
Romans as friends, you will not act unwisely if you hide it from me; but when
you prepare for war, you will cheat yourself if you do not set me first in
it."

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

At the age we have mentioned, then, he set out with his father for Spain.
After his father's death Hasdrubal was appointed commander in his place, and
Hannibal was put in charge of all the cavalry. When Hasdrubal too was killed,
the army conferred the supreme command on him. The choice was reported to
Carthage and publicly confirmed. So Hannibal, made commander when he was not
yet twenty-five, subdued all the peoples of Spain by war in the next three
years, stormed Saguntum, an allied city, and raised three very large armies.
Of these he sent one into Africa, left the second in Spain with his brother
Hasdrubal, and led the third with him into Italy. After he crossed the pass of
the Pyrenees, he fought with all the inhabitants wherever he went: he let no
one go until he had beaten them. When he came to the Alps, which divide Italy
from Gaul, and which no one before him had ever crossed with an army except
the Greek Hercules (and from that crossing the pass is today called the
Graian), he cut down the Alpine peoples who tried to bar his passage, opened
the country, built roads, and made it so that a fully equipped elephant could
go where before a single unarmed man could scarcely creep. By this way he led
his forces across and reached Italy.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

He had already fought at the Rhone with the consul Publius Cornelius Scipio
and driven him back. He fought this same man at Clastidium by the Po and left
him wounded and in flight. A third time this same Scipio came against him at
the Trebia with his colleague Tiberius Longus. Hannibal joined battle with
them and routed both. From there he crossed the Apennines through the
Ligurians, making for Etruria. On this march he was so badly afflicted with a
disease of the eyes that afterward he never used the right one equally well.
While he was still pressed by that illness and being carried in a litter, he
trapped the consul Gaius Flaminius and his army in an ambush at Trasimene and
killed him, and not long afterward he killed the praetor Gaius Centenius, who
was occupying a pass with a picked force. From here he came into Apulia. There
two consuls met him, Gaius Terentius and Lucius Aemilius. He put both their
armies to flight in one battle, killed the consul Paulus and several men of
consular rank besides, among them Gnaeus Servilius Geminus, who had been
consul the year before.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

After this battle he set out for Rome and, with no one to resist him, lingered
in the mountains near the city. When he had held camp there for some days and
was returning to Capua, Quintus Fabius Maximus, the Roman dictator, blocked
his way in the Falernian country. Hemmed in here by narrow ground, Hannibal
got his army out by night without any loss and deceived Fabius, a most cunning
commander. For when night had fallen he bound brushwood to the horns of young
oxen, set it on fire, and turned a great number of them loose to wander. That
sudden sight cast such terror into the Roman army that no one dared to go
beyond the rampart. Not many days after this exploit he drew Marcus Minucius
Rufus, master of the horse with power equal to the dictator's, into battle by
a trick and put him to flight. Though he himself was not present, he had
Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, consul for a second time, led into an ambush in
Lucania and destroyed him. In the same way he killed Marcus Claudius
Marcellus, five times consul, near Venusia. It would take long to recount all
the battles. So this one thing will be enough to say, from which it may be
understood how great he was: as long as he was in Italy, no one stood against
him in the line of battle, and no one after Cannae pitched camp against him in
the open field.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

From here, still undefeated, he was recalled to defend his country and waged
war against Publius Scipio, son of that Scipio whom he himself had put to
flight first at the Rhone, again at the Po, and a third time at the Trebia.
His country's resources were already exhausted, and with this man he wished
for the present to settle the war, so that he might meet him later with
greater strength. They agreed to a conference; they did not agree on terms. A
few days after that he fought the same man at Zama. Beaten (incredible to
tell), he reached Hadrumetum in two days and two nights, a place about three
hundred miles from Zama. In this flight the Numidians who had left the line
with him laid an ambush for him; he not only escaped them but even crushed
them. At Hadrumetum he gathered those left from the rout and in a few days, by
new levies, brought many together.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

While he was most keenly occupied in making ready, the Carthaginians made
peace with the Romans. He nevertheless afterward commanded the army and
carried on affairs in Africa, and so did his brother Mago, down to the
consulship of Publius Sulpicius and Gaius Aurelius. For under those
magistrates Carthaginian envoys came to Rome to thank the senate and the Roman
people for having made peace with them, and for that reason to present them
with a golden crown, and at the same time to ask that their hostages might
stay at Fregellae and that the captives be restored. By a decree of the senate
they were answered that their gift was welcome and accepted; that the hostages
would be where they asked; that the captives would not be sent back, because
they still had Hannibal, by whose doing the war had been undertaken, the
bitterest enemy of the Roman name, in command with the army, and likewise his
brother Mago. When the Carthaginians learned of this reply, they recalled
Hannibal and Mago home. When he returned, he was made king in the
twenty-second year after he had been commander: for as at Rome consuls are
created, so at Carthage two kings were created every year for a year's term.
In that office Hannibal showed the same diligence he had shown in war. From
new revenues he brought it about not only that there was money to be paid to
the Romans according to the treaty, but that there was even a surplus to be
laid up in the treasury. Then, the year after his praetorship, in the
consulship of Marcus Claudius and Lucius Furius, envoys came from Rome to
Carthage. Hannibal, thinking they had been sent to demand him, boarded a ship
in secret before they were given a hearing of the senate, and fled into Syria
to Antiochus. When this became known, the Carthaginians sent two ships to
seize him if they could overtake him, confiscated his goods, razed his house
from the foundations, and judged him an exile.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

But in the fourth year after he had fled from home, in the consulship of
Lucius Cornelius and Quintus Minucius, Hannibal approached Africa with five
ships in the territory of the Cyrenaeans, hoping he might draw the
Carthaginians into war by the prospect of Antiochus and trust in him, for he
had already persuaded that king to set out with his armies for Italy. He
summoned his brother Mago there. When the Carthaginians learned of this, they
inflicted on Mago, though absent, the same penalty as on his brother. When
their situation had become desperate, they weighed anchor and set sail.
Hannibal reached Antiochus. A double tradition has been handed down about
Mago's death: some have recorded that he died by shipwreck, others that he was
killed by his own slaves. But if Antiochus had been as willing to obey
Hannibal's counsels in waging the war as he had been in taking it up, he would
have fought for the supreme command nearer the Tiber than at Thermopylae.
Although Hannibal saw him attempting many things foolishly, still he did not
abandon him in any matter. He commanded a few ships which he had been ordered
to lead from Syria into Asia, and with them he fought against the fleet of the
Rhodians in the Pamphylian sea. In that battle, though his men were
outnumbered by the enemy, he himself was superior on the wing where he fought.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

After Antiochus had been put to flight, Hannibal feared that he would be
surrendered, which would without doubt have happened if he had put himself in
their power. He came to the Gortynians in Crete, so that he might consider
there where he should go next. But that most cunning of all men saw that,
unless he took some precaution, he would be in great danger because of the
greed of the Cretans: for he was carrying a great sum of money with him, and
he knew that a report of it had gone out. And so he formed this plan. He
filled several jars with lead and covered the tops with gold and silver. In
the presence of the leading men he deposited these in the temple of Diana,
pretending that he was trusting his fortunes to their good faith. When he had
led them into this error, he filled bronze statues, which he was carrying with
him, with all his money and set them down in the open court of his house. The
Gortynians guarded the temple with great care, not so much from others as from
Hannibal, for fear that he, without their knowing it, might take his property
and carry it off.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Having saved all his property and fooled the Cretans, the Carthaginian came to
Prusias in Pontus. With him he kept the same spirit against Italy, and he did
nothing else than arm the king and sharpen him against the Romans. When he saw
that the king was less strong in his own resources, he won over other kings
and joined warlike nations to him. Eumenes, king of Pergamum, most friendly to
the Romans, was at odds with him, and war was being waged between them by sea
and by land; but on both sides Eumenes was the stronger because of his
alliance with the Romans. Hannibal wanted him crushed all the more, thinking
that if he removed him the rest would be easier for himself. To kill this man
he formed this plan. They were to decide the matter with their fleets in a few
days. He was outnumbered in ships. He had to fight by a trick, since he was
not equal in arms. He ordered as many live poisonous snakes as possible to be
collected and thrown into earthen jars. When he had gathered a great number of
these, on the very day he was going to fight the naval battle he called the
sailors together and instructed them all to charge the one ship of King
Eumenes, and only to defend themselves against the rest. They would easily
accomplish that by the multitude of snakes. He would see to it that they knew
which ship the king was sailing in, and he promised them a great reward if
they captured or killed him.

* Caput 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

After he had exhorted the soldiers in this way, both fleets were led out to
battle. When their lines had been drawn up, before the signal for battle was
given, Hannibal, to make it plain to his men where Eumenes was, sent a
messenger in a skiff with a herald's staff. When this man reached the enemy's
ships and, showing a letter, said he was looking for the king, he was at once
led to Eumenes, because no one doubted that something about peace had been
written. The messenger pointed out the commander's ship to his own men and
withdrew to the place from which he had come. But when Eumenes opened the
letter, he found nothing in it except mockery. Although he wondered at the
cause of this and could not find it, still he did not hesitate to join battle
at once. In the clash the Bithynians, by Hannibal's order, all attacked
Eumenes' ship. When the king could not withstand their force, he sought safety
in flight, which he would not have gained if he had not withdrawn within his
own guards, which were stationed on the nearest shore. When the rest of the
Pergamene ships were pressing the enemy more sharply, suddenly the earthen
jars which we mentioned above began to be thrown upon them. When these were
thrown they at first raised a laugh among the fighters, and no one could
understand why it was done. But after they saw their ships filled with snakes,
terrified by the strange thing, and not seeing what they should most avoid,
they turned their sterns and withdrew to their naval camp. Thus Hannibal
overcame the arms of the Pergamenes by counsel, and not only then: often at
other times, with land forces, he drove his adversaries back with like
prudence.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

While these things were going on in Asia, it happened by chance that envoys of
Prusias were dining at Rome with Titus Quinctius Flamininus, a man of consular
rank, and there, when Hannibal was mentioned, one of them said that he was in
the kingdom of Prusias. The next day Flamininus reported this to the senate.
The senators, who thought that while Hannibal lived they would never be
without plots, sent envoys into Bithynia, among them Flamininus, to ask the
king not to keep their bitterest enemy with him, and to surrender him to them.
Prusias did not dare to refuse them this, but he refused to do what would
violate the law of hospitality: let them seize him themselves, if they could;
they would easily find the place where he was. For Hannibal kept himself in
one place, in a stronghold which the king had given him as a gift, and he had
built it so that there were exits in every part of the building, fearing, of
course, just what did happen. When the Roman envoys had come here and
surrounded his house with a crowd, a boy looking out from the door told
Hannibal that more armed men than usual were in sight. He ordered him to go
round all the doors of the building and quickly report to him whether he was
besieged in the same way on every side. When the boy had swiftly reported what
he had seen and shown that all the exits were occupied, Hannibal saw that this
had not been done by chance, but that they were after him, and that he could
no longer keep his life. Unwilling to surrender it at another's bidding, and
mindful of his former deeds of valor, he took the poison which he had always
been accustomed to keep with him.

* Caput 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Thus this most valiant man, after undergoing many and various toils, came to
rest in his seventieth year. There is no agreement as to the consuls in whose
year he died. For Atticus left it written in his annal that he died in the
consulship of Marcus Claudius Marcellus and Quintus Fabius Labeo; but
Polybius, in that of Lucius Aemilius Paulus and Gnaeus Baebius Tamphilus; and
Sulpicius Blitho, in that of Publius Cornelius Cethegus and Marcus Baebius
Tamphilus. And this man, so great and occupied with such wars, still gave some
time to letters. For there are several books of his, written in the Greek
tongue, among them one to the Rhodians on the deeds of Gnaeus Manlius Vulso in
Asia. Many have recorded the deeds of this war, but among them two who were
with him in camp and lived with him as long as fortune allowed: Silenus and
Sosylus the Lacedaemonian. And Hannibal used this Sosylus as his teacher of
Greek letters.

But it is time for us to make an end of this book and to set out the Roman
commanders, so that by comparing the deeds of both it may more easily be
judged which men are to be preferred.
