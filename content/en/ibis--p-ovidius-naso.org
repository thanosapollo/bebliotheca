#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Ibis edited by Rudolf Ehwald
#+END_QUOTE

Until this hour, my fiftieth year now spent,
my Muse has never armed a single song.
Of all those thousands that I wrote, no letter
of Naso can be read as wet with blood.
My books have hurt no man except myself:
the maker's head went down by his own Art.
One man (and that itself is a great wrong)
will not let my good name of candor last.
Whoever he is (I still withhold the name)
he drives my unaccustomed hands to weapons.
He will not let me, banished to the cold
rising of the north wind, hide in exile.
He worries, ruthless, at wounds that ask for rest,
and tosses my name through the whole forum.
He will not let the wife bound to me for life
weep the funeral of a husband still alive.
And while I clasp the shaken timbers of my keel,
he fights to own the planks of my wreck.
He who should have quenched the sudden flames
hunts plunder from the middle of the fire.

He strives to starve my old age in exile.
Alas, how much more worthy he is of my woes!

May the gods make it better. Far the greatest
of them to me is he who would not leave
my road destitute. To him, then, wherever
I may, I will always pay the thanks he earned
for so gentle a heart. Let Pontus hear this.
Perhaps he will do the same, so a nearer land
may have to bear me witness.
But you, violent man who trampled me as I lay,
I will be, so far as a wretch may, the enemy
you have earned.
Water will first cease to fight with fire,
and the sun's light will join itself to the moon;
the same quarter of sky will send both west wind
and east, and a warm south will blow from the icy pole;
new concord will come to the brothers' smoke
that old wrath parts upon a kindled pyre;
spring will mix with autumn, summer with midwinter,
and one region will be evening and dawn:
sooner than I lay down the arms we took
and make my peace with you, you shameless man,
the peace your crimes already tore apart.

This will be our peace, while life remains to me:
the peace a wolf is used to keep with a weak flock.
I will join the first battles in the verse begun,
though wars are not commonly waged in this meter:
and as the soldier not yet heated throws his spear
first at the ground full of yellow sand,
so I will not yet hurl at you with sharp iron,
nor will the spear fly straight at the hated head;
and in this little book I will name neither you
nor your deeds, and for a short while I will let you
hide who you are.
Later, if you go on, my iambic book
against you will give weapons dyed in Lycambes' blood.
Now, in the manner Battus' son curses his enemy
Ibis, I curse you and yours.
And as he did, I will wrap songs in blind histories,
though I am not used to follow this kind myself.
Imitating his riddles in an Ibis I shall be said
to have forgotten my own manner and judgment.

And since I do not yet tell those who ask who you are,
take you also, for now, the name of Ibis;
and as my verses will have a share of night,
so may the whole run of your life be black.

See that someone reads these to you on your birthday
and on the Kalends of Janus, with a mouth that will not lie.
Gods of sea and land, and you who hold better realms
than these, the kingdoms among the divided poles with Jove,
hither, I pray, hither turn all your minds,
and let there be weight in my wishes:
and you yourself, Earth, and the sea itself with its waves,
and you yourself, highest aether, receive my prayers;
and the stars, and the sun's image ringed with rays,
and the Moon, who never shine with the orb you had before,
and Night, to be revered in the look of your shadows;
and you who spin the binding work with a triple thumb,
and you who glide through the infernal valleys
with a dreadful murmur, river no god may forswear,
and you whom they say sit before the dark prison doors
with snake-bound hair twisted in fillets;
you also, commons of the gods above, fauns and satyrs
and Lares, and rivers and nymphs and the half-divine kind:
last, gods old and new from ancient chaos
down to our time, be present, all of you,
while dread songs are sung to a faithless head
and wrath and grief play out their parts.

Nod, all of you in order, to our wishes,
and let no part of my vow fall void.
May what I pray be done: so he thinks they were not
my words, but those of Pasiphae's son-in-law.
And the punishments I pass over, let him suffer those too;
let him be more wretched than my talent can make him.
And lest vows that curse a feigned name hurt less,
or move the great gods less:
I devote that man whom my mind knows, Ibis,
who knows by his deeds that he has earned these prayers.

There is no delay in me: I will carry out
ratified vows as priest.
Whoever is present at my rites, keep a still mouth;
whoever is present at my rites, speak words of mourning,
and approach Ibis with cheeks wet with weeping:
meet him with evil omens and left-hand feet,
and let black clothes cover your bodies.
You too, why do you hesitate to take up funeral fillets?
Already, as you see yourself, the altar of your funeral stands.
The procession is ready for you. Let there be no delay
to the grim vows: give your throat to my knives, dread victim.
Let earth deny you crops, the river deny you waves.
Let wind and breeze deny you their breath.

Let the sun not be warm for you, nor Phoebe bright,
and let the clear stars desert your eyes.
Let Vulcan not offer himself to you, nor the air,
nor earth give you a way, nor the sea.
May you wander an exile, destitute, and haunt
others' thresholds, and seek scant food with a trembling mouth.
Let neither body nor sick mind be free of complaining pain,
and let night be heavier than day for you, day than night.
Be wretched always, and be pitiable to none:
let woman and man rejoice in your adversities.
Let hatred be added to tears, and you be thought worthy,
when you have borne very many evils, to bear more.
And (what is rare) let the look of your fortune,
stripped of its usual favor, still stir envy.
Let cause not fail you, but let the means of death fail:
let a forced life flee the death you want;
and after long struggle may the spirit leave the tortured
limbs, and twist you first with a long delay.
They will come to pass. Phoebus himself just now gave me
signs of what is coming, and a sad bird flew from the left.
Surely I shall think what I vow will move the gods,
and I shall always be fed, traitor, on hope of your death.

And sooner will that day, which comes late for me,
take this life you have too often sought,
than this pain can ever vanish with time,
or time and the hour soothe my hatred.
While Thracians fight with the bow, Iazyges with the spear,
while the Ganges is warm and the Hister cold;
while woods belong to mountains, while soft fodder to fields,
while the Tuscan Tiber has liquid waters,
I will wage war with you. Death will not end my wrath,
but will give fierce weapons to harmless hands.
Then too, when I have slipped into the empty airs,
my lifeless shade will hate your ways.
Then too, a shade mindful of your deeds,
I will pursue, and a bony shape will hunt your face.
Whether I am wasted (I would not wish it) by long years,
or released by a death made with the hand;
whether I am tossed a shipwreck on huge waves,
and a far-off fish eats my flesh;
whether foreign birds tear my limbs;
or wolves dye their muzzles in my blood;
or someone deigns to put me under earth
and give the empty body a common pyre:
whatever I shall be, I will strive to burst from Stygian shores,
and stretch cold hands, an avenger, at your face.

You will see me waking; in the still shadows of night
I will shake sleep, seeming present to your sight.
At last, whatever you do, I will fly before your face and eyes
and complain, and you will be quiet in no seat.
Twisted lashes will sound, and knotted snakes,
and torches will always smoke at the guilty face.
By these furies you will be driven living, and by the same dead,
and your life will be a shorter punishment than what is to come.
You will not have a funeral and the tears of your people;
your head will be thrown out unwept.
You will be dragged by the executioner's hand, the people clapping,
and a hook will be fixed in your bones.
The flames themselves, which consume all things, will flee you;
righteous earth will spit out the hated corpse.
A raw vulture will drag the guts with claw and beak,
and greedy dogs will tear the treacherous heart.

And (be proud of this praise if you will) unsated wolves
will brawl over your body.
You will be called away from the Elysian fields,
and you will dwell where the guilty crowd has its seats.
Sisyphus is there, rolling the stone and going after it,
and he who is bound and driven by the wheel's swift circle,
and the Danaids who bear on their shoulders waters that will perish,
the bloody daughters-in-law of exiled Aegyptus.
Pelops's father reaches for fruit that stands before him,
always hungry, always standing in plenty of water;
and he who measures nine acres from top to bottom
offers the guts the unwearied bird is owed.
Here one of the Furies will split your side with a lash,
so you confess the count of your crime;
another will give your limbs, cut, to Tartarean snakes;
a third will cook your smoking cheeks with fire.
The guilty shade will be torn a thousand ways, and Aeacus
will be ingenious in your punishments.
He will copy onto you the torments of old defendants:
you will be a cause of rest to all the ancient ones.
Sisyphus, you will have one to whom you may hand the rolling weight;
swift wheels will now turn new limbs;
this one too will be he who snatches in vain at boughs and waves;
this one will feed birds with unconsumed guts.
Nor will another death end the punishments of this death,
and no hour will be last for such great evils.
I will sing a few of them from there, as if someone plucked
leaves from Ida, or the top water from the Libyan sea.

For I will not say how many flowers are born on Sicilian Hybla,
or how many saffrons the Cilician land bears,
nor, when grim winter bristles on the north wind's wings,
how much hail makes Athos white;
nor can all your evils be told by my voice,
though you grant me mouths multiplied.
So many and such ruins, woe, will come to you in your misery
that I could think even I might be driven to tears.
Those tears will make me blessed without end:
that weeping will then be sweeter to me than laughter.

You were born unlucky (so the gods willed), and no
star was kind or light at your birth.
Venus did not shine, nor Jupiter in that hour,
and Moon and Sun were not in a fit place,
nor did he whom bright Maia bore to great Jove
offer you fires set usefully enough.
The wild stars of Mars, promising nothing calm,
bore down on you, and those of the sickle-bearing old man.
Your birthday too, lest you see anything but sadness,
was foul and black with clouds drawn over it.
This is the day to which heavy Allia gives a name in the calendar,
the day that brought Ibis, a public loss.

As soon as he slipped from his unclean mother's womb
and pressed the Cinyphian ground with a foul body,
a night-owl sat on the roof opposite
and gave grave sounds from a funeral mouth.
At once the Eumenides washed him in marsh waters,
where a hollow wave had flowed from Stygian shallows,
and smeared his breast with the gall of an Erebus-snake,
and three times struck their bloodied hands.
They had soaked the infant throat with dog's milk:
this was the boy's first food to enter his mouth.
From then the nursling drank his nurse's rage,
and he barks dog-words through the whole forum.
They bound his limbs in rags dyed with iron-rust,
which they had snatched from a badly abandoned pyre;
and lest he lie unsupported on naked earth,
they set his tender head on flints.
And already about to leave, they put torches made from green
wood to his eyes, right up under his face.
He wept, as an infant does when touched by bitter smokes,
when one of the three sisters spoke thus:
"We have stirred those tears for you for measureless time;
they will always fall, with cause enough."

She had spoken. But Clotho ordered the promises to stand,
and spun black threads with a hostile hand;
and lest she speak long foretellings from her own mouth,
"A prophet will sing your fate," she said, "he will be."

I am that prophet. From me you will learn your wounds,
if only the gods give their strength to my words,
and if weight of things is added to my songs,
which you may find ratified through your griefs.
And lest you be tortured without examples of an earlier age,
let yours be no lighter than the Trojan woes.
May you bear in a poisoned leg as many wounds
as Poeas's son bore, heir of club-bearing Hercules.
Nor may your pain be lighter than his who drank
a doe's milk, took a wound from an armed man,
and help from one unarmed.
May you fall as he fell, headlong from his horse
onto the Aleian fields, whose own face nearly ruined him.
May you see what Amyntor's son saw, and, robbed of light,
feel out a fearful road with a staff to guide you.
May you see no more than he whom his own daughter led,
whose crime both parents came to know;
such as the old man famous in Apollo's art became,
after he was taken as judge of a playful quarrel;
and such as he became at whose bidding a dove
was given as guide and leader to Pallas's ship;
and he who lost the eyes through which he had ill seen gold,
eyes a bereft mother gave as offerings to her son;
like the Sicilian shepherd, to whom Telemus, Eurymus's son,
had prophesied the chances yet to come;
like the two sons of Phineus, from whom the giver of their light
took it again; like the head of Thamyris and Demodocus.
So may someone cut from you those parts Saturn cut
from the body out of which he himself was made.
Nor may Neptune treat you better on the swollen waves
than him whose brother and wife were suddenly birds;
and the cunning man whom Semele's sister pitied
as he clung to the torn timbers of a broken raft.
Or, lest one man alone have known this kind of punishment,
may horses running apart carry your guts, split;
or may you yourself bear what he whom Rome thought it shame
to ransom suffered from the Punic commander.
Nor may a present god be a help to you, as to him
whom the altar of Rhoetean Jove did not profit at all.
And as the Thessalian leaped from the top of Ossa,
you too be flung from a rocky ridge.

Or as with Eurylochus, who took the scepter from him,
may your limbs be food for greedy snakes.
Or may boiling water poured over the head hasten your
fate, as it hastened the fate of Minos.
And like Prometheus, mild for his works, yet not unpunished,
may you be shackled and feed the winged birds of the air.
Or as Etracides, fifth from great Hercules three times over,
be cut down and thrown into the measureless strait.
Or as Amyntas's son, may a boy loved in a shameful love
hate you and wound you with a savage sword.
Nor may cups mixed for you be more faithful
than his who was born of horned Jove.
Or die as the captured Achaean died, hung wretched,
with gold-bearing water looking on.
Or as Achilles's son, famous by a kindred name,
may a tile thrown by an enemy hand crush you.
Nor may your bones rest more happily than Pyrrhus's,
which lay scattered through the Ambracian ways.
And die like the Aeacid's daughter, with javelins driven in;
Ceres may not disguise this rite.
And like the grandson of the king just named in our song,
may you drink blister-beetle juices at a parent's giving.

Or may a pious adulteress be said to have slain you, as she
at whose stroke Leucon fell was called pious.
And may you send the dearest bodies with you onto a pyre,
the end of life Sardanapalus has.

May south-blown sand bury your face, as it buried
those who set out to violate the temple of Libyan Jove.
And as sinking ash swallowed the faces of those
killed by the fraud of the second Darius,
so may it swallow yours.
Or as once for him who set out from olive-bearing Sicyon,
may cold and hunger be the cause of your death.

Or as the man of Atarneus, sewn in a steer-hide,
be carried in shame as prey to your master.
And in your bedchamber be slaughtered as the man of Pherae
was given to death by his own wife's sword.
And those you think faithful, as Larissan Aleuas learned,
may you learn by your own wound are not faithful.
As Milo, the tyrant under whom Pisa was tortured,
be thrown living into hidden waters.
And may the weapons that came from Jove against him
who held the Phylaceian kingdoms among the Aphidantes
seek you too.
Or as Lenaeus once from the Amastrian shores,
be left naked on Achilles's ground.

And as Eurydamas was dragged three times around
Thrasyllus's pyre by an enemy from Larissa with wheels,
or he who had often guarded walls, and then purified
with his own body walls that did not last,
and as the adulterer, under a new punishment from
Hippomenes' daughter, is said to have been dragged
on Actaean ground,
so, when hateful life has left your limbs,
may avenging horses snatch the foul corpse.
So may some crag pin your guts, as once
Greek guts were pinned under the Euboean gulf;
and as the fierce rapist perished by thunderbolt and sea,
so may fire help the waters that will drown you.
So too may furies drive the mind mad, as they drove
the man who has one wound in the whole body;
as they drove Dryas's son, who held the kingdoms of Rhodope,
and whose two feet were not shod alike;
as they once drove the man of Oeta, and the serpents'
son-in-law, and Tisamenus's father, and Callirhoe's husband.
Nor may a more chaste matron fall to you than she
of whom Tydeus could have blushed as daughter-in-law;
and she who joined her love with her husband's brother,
a Locrian woman hidden in a servant's killing.

So too, may the gods grant, may you rejoice
in a faithful wife as much as the son-in-law
of Talaus and of Tyndareus;
and they who dared to prepare death for their cousin-husbands,
the Danaids, have their necks pressed by unceasing water.
If you kindle in anyone the fire of Byblis and Canace,
let your sister be faithful only through crime.
If you have a daughter, let her be what Pelopia was
to Thyestes, Myrrha to her father, and Nyctimene to hers.
Nor let her be more pious, or a friend to her father's head,
than Pterelas's daughter was, or she to you, Nisus;
and she who made a place infamous by the name of her crime,
and crushed her father's limbs with wheels driven over them.
Die young, as they died whose thrown corpses
and heads the gates of Pisa held;
as he died who often stained the ground with wretched suitors'
blood, and stained it better with his own;
as the treacherous charioteer of the savage tyrant died,
who gave new names to the Myrtoan water;
as they died who sought the swift girl in vain,
until three apples made her slower;
as they died who entered the roof that hid a new monster,
the blind house from which there is no coming back.

Die as they died whose bodies the violent Aeacid
sent into the deep, and another six with the old man's pyre.
Die as they died whom the Sphinx, we read, gave
to unspeakable death, beaten by a dark mouth's riddle;
as they died who fell in Bistonian Minerva's temple,
for which the goddess's face is even now covered;
as they died who once made the Thracian king's mangers
bloody with their own feast;
as they died who felt Therodamas's lions,
and those who felt the Tauric rites of Thoantean goddess;
as they died whom ravening Scylla, and Charybdis
facing her, snatched in fear from the Dulichian ship;
as they died whom Polyphemus sent down into his vast belly;
as they died who came under Laestrygonian hands;
as they died whom the Punic leader drowned in well-waters
and made the waters hoary with the dust he threw.
As twice six maids of Icarius's daughter perished, and the suitors,
and he who was giving arms to the suitors against his master's head;

as the wrestler lies, laid low by the Aonian guest,
who, a wonder, was the victor when he had fallen;
as they died whom Antaeus's strong arms crushed;
and those whom the Lemnian crowd gave to savage death;
as he died who, after a long time, revealed an unjust rite,
and, slain as a victim, drew down rain;
as Antaeus's brother stained the altars with the blood he owed,
and died himself by his own examples;
as he died who, wicked, fed horses on human flesh
instead of grassy fodder;
as two died under the same avenger with different wounds,
Nessus and Dexamenus's son-in-law;
as your great-grandson died, Saturn, whom Coronis's son
saw give back life from his own city;
as Sinis and Sciron died, and Polypemon's son with them;
and he who was man in part of himself, in part a steer;
and he who launched into the air the beams he had bent
to the ground, looking at this sea and that;
and the bodies Ceres saw perish with a glad face,
Cercyon's, by Theseus's hand.
May these things befall you, whom my wrath devotes
with earned prayers, or evils no lighter than these:
such as Achaemenides was, deserted on Sicilian Etna,
when he saw the Trojan sails come;
such too was the fortune of double-named Irus,
and those who hold the bridge, which will be greater for you.

And may Ceres's son be loved by you always in vain,
and desert your wealth though it is sought without cease;
and as, with the wave sliding back in alternate returns,
the soft sand is drawn from under the pressed foot,
so may your fortune always melt by some means,
and slipped through the middle of the hands flow away without cease.
And like the father of her who used to change various shapes,
be left full of hunger that cannot be put out.
Nor let human feasting disgust you. Wherever
you can, you will be the Tydeus of this time.
And may you do some deed, from the sun's evening to his rising,
that the maddened horses are driven back again.
You will seek again the foul banquet of Lycaon's table,
and try to deceive Jove by a fraud of food.
And I wish someone, the god's power set before you, may test you,
that you may be a Tantalid, and Celeus's boy.
And so may your limbs be scattered over the wide fields,
like those that held back a father's roads.
In Perillus's bronze imitate true steers,
the sound matching the shape of a bull.
And like fierce Phalaris, the tongue first cut away with a sword,
groan shut in Paphian bronze in the manner of an ox.

And while you wish to return into the years of a better age,
be deceived as Admetus's old father-in-law was.
Or be a horseman sunk in a gulf of mud in the middle,
provided there are no names of your fate.
And I would you perished as those sprung from teeth
thrown by a Sidonian hand through Greek fields.
And may the ill vows that Pilthides made, and equally
those of the Molinae, befall your head;
and those vows by which a bird is devoted in a little book,
the bird that purges its body with water thrown out.
And may you bear as many wounds as he is said to have borne
from whose funeral rites a knife is wont to be absent.
And cut in a daze, as those the Cybelean mother goads
cut their cheap limbs to Phrygian measures.
And from a man become neither woman nor man, like Attis,
and shake the hoarse drums with a soft hand.
And be turned suddenly into the Great Mother's beasts,
as victor and vanquished were turned at a swift step.
And lest Limone alone have felt that punishment,
may a horse tear your guts with a fierce tooth.
Or like the Cassandrean, no milder than that master,
be wounded and buried under heaped earth.

Or like Abas's descendant, or like the Cygnian hero,
be shut in and flung into sea-waters.
Or be slain as a victim at altars sacred to Phoebus,
the death Theudotus bore from a savage foe.

Or may Abdera devote you on set days,
and stones more than hail seek the devoted man.
Or be struck by hostile Jove's three-forked bolt,
as Hipponoos's son was, and Dexione's father,
as Autonoe's sister, as he whose aunt was Maia,
as he who badly drove the horses he rashly wished;
as the fierce Aeolid, as he of the same blood as that sex
from which Arctos was born, who lacks liquid waters;
as Macelo was struck with her husband by rapid flames,
so, I pray, may you fall by the fire of heaven's avenger.
And be prey to those to whom Latonian Delos
must not be approached after Thasos was snatched before his day;
and those that tore the man who watched
the baths of modest Diana,
and those who tore Crotopus's son Linus.
Nor be struck more lightly by a poisoned snake
than the daughter-in-law of old Oeagrus and Calliope;
than Hypsipyle's boy, than he who first with a sharp point
fixed the hollow timber of the suspected horse.

Nor climb high steps with more care than Elpenor,
and bear wine's force in the way he bore it.
And fall as thoroughly mastered as whoever helped
inhuman Thiodamas calling to arms, the Dryopian;
as fierce Cacus himself perished, sacrificed in his own cave,
betrayed by the mouth of the shut-in ox;
as he who brought gifts dyed in Nessus's poison,
and with his name the Euboean waters take their bound.
And from a sheer rock may you go down to Tartarus,
as he who read the Socratic work on death;
as he who saw the false sails of Theseus's ship,
as the boy sent from the Ilian citadel,
as the nurse, the same aunt, of tender Bacchus,
as him whose cause of death was a saw found;
as the Lydian maid cast herself from high cliffs,
who had spoken evil words to an unwilling god.
May a pregnant lioness, a countrywoman in her father's field,
meet you, and be the cause of Phalaecus's death.

And he who slew Lycurgus's son, and him born from a tree,
and bold Idmon: may a boar rend you too.
And even lifeless may it wound you; as him
on whose own face the snout of the transfixed beast fell.
Or the same man whom a pine killed with a like death:
be a Phrygian and a Berecyntian hunter.

If your ship has touched Minos's sands,
may the Cretan crowd think you Corcyraean.
And enter a house about to fall, as Aleuas's blood did,
when the seat of Leoprepes's son fell without the man.
And as Evenus, drowned in a torrent river,
give names to the rapid water, or as Tiberinus.
And like Astacus's son's corpses fixed on a trunk,
may your head, worthy of beasts, be a man's food;
and what they say Broteas did from desire of death,
give your limbs to a kindled pyre to be burned.
And suffer death shut in a cage, like that
founder of a history that would not succeed.
And as it harmed the inventor of the pugnacious iambus,
so may a shameless tongue be for your ruin.
And as he who harmed Athens with a song too little stable
may you perish hated, with food failing.
And as the bard of the stern lyre is said to have perished,
may your injured right hand be the cause of your death.
And as a snake gave a wound to Agamemnon's Orestes,
you too fall from a bite that has venom.
May the first night of marriage be the last of life:
Eupolis perished thus, and a new bride lately.

And as they tell that buskined Lycophron fell,
may a fixed arrow stick in your inwards.
Or be torn in the wood and scattered by your own men's hands,
as he born of a snake-grandfather was scattered at Thebes.
And be dragged through wild mountains by a seizing bull,
as Lycus's imperious wife was dragged.
And what her sister's unwilling concubine suffered,
may your cut tongue fall before your feet.

As the founder of slow Cyrene, Blaesus by surname,
may you be found in the world's countless places.

And may the bee, that craftsman, bury in your eyes
the shafts it made, the same that harmed the Achaean bard.

And be fixed and have your guts torn on hard rocks,
like him whose Pyrrha was his brother's daughter.

Like Harpagus's boy, repeat Thyestes's example:
be cut, and go into your father's guts.
Bear a truncated body, parts cut away by a savage sword,
such as they say Mamerta's limbs were.
And as the Syracusan poet with a constricted throat,
so may the way of your breath be closed by a noose.
Or let your guts lie open with the skin stripped away,
like him whose name a Phrygian river has.
Unhappy man, may you see Medusa's face that turns flesh to stone,
who alone gave many of the Cephenes to death.
Suffer the bites of the Potnian mares, like Glaucus,
and leap into the sea's waters, like the other Glaucus.

And as he who has the same name as the two just spoken,
may harmful honey choke the path of breath.

And in distress drink what Anytus's most learned defendant
once drank with an unperturbed mouth.
Nor, if you love anything, may it fall out more happily than Haemon;
and as Macareus gained his, so may you gain yours.
Or see what Hector's boy already saw from his father's citadel
when the flames held all things.
Pay for shame in blood, like him begotten with a grandfather
as father, whose sister was made his parent by crime.
And in your bones may that kind of weapon stick
by which Icarius's son-in-law is said to have fallen.
And as the talkative throat was crushed in the maple horse,
so may the path of your voice be closed by a thumb.
Or like Anaxarchus, be reduced in a deep mortar,
and may the struck bones sound in place of ordinary grain.
And as Phoebus buried Psamathe's father in lowest
Tartarus, for what he had done to his own daughter.
And into yours may that plague go which Coroebus's right hand
conquered, and brought help to the wretched people of Argos.

And like Aethra's grandson, about to die for Venus's wrath,
be thrown as an exile from the stunned horses.
As a host destroyed his nursling for great wealth,
may your host destroy you for little wealth.

As they say six brothers were slain with Damasichthon,
so may all your line perish with you.
As the lyre-player added his own death to his wretched children's,
so may you have just weariness of your life.
Or like Pelops's sister, be hardened by a risen stone,
and Battus, harmed by his own tongue.
If you throw a discus through the clear air,
fall struck by the disk as the Oebalid boy.
If the wave is beaten by alternating arms,
may every water be worse for you than Abydos's.
As the comic poet perished while he swam in mid-waves,
so may Stygian liquor strangle your mouth.
Or when you have survived the windy sea, a shipwrecked man,
die at the touch of land, like Palinurus.
And as a crowd of watching dogs tore the buskined bard,
Diana's charge, may they tear you too.
Or like the Sicilian, leap upon the giant's mouth,
where Sicilian Etna vomits most flames.
And may Strymonian mothers tear your limbs with mad nails,
thinking you are Orpheus.
As Althaea's son burned by absent flames,
so may your pyre take fire from a stick's flame.

As the new bride was caught by the Phasian crown,
and the bride's father, and the house with the parent;
as the blood went diffused into Hercules's limbs;
so may a plague-bearing venom eat your body.
By which her offspring avenged Penthalides Lycurgus,
may this blow of a new weapon await you too.
And like Milo try to split the fissile oak,
and not be able to draw your caught hands back.
And be harmed by your gifts, like Icarius, on whom
a drunken crowd laid armed hands.

And what the pious daughter did by grief for her father's death,
make your throat go through a noose's bonds.
And suffer hunger with the threshold of the house blocked,
like him to whom his own mother gave the law of punishment.
By that man's example may you violate Minerva's images,
he who turned his light course from Aulis's harbor.
Or in the manner of Nauplius's son pay the penalty for a false
charge with death, and may it not help you that you did not deserve it.
As the Isindian host stripped Aethalon of life,
whom Ion, remembering, still drives from the rites;
and as the mother herself betrayed Melantheus hiding
in the dark from slaughter, by the service of a light:

so may your breast be pierced by thrown weapons,
so I pray you be blocked by your own aid.
May such a night go for you as went for the timid Phrygian
when the horses brave Achilles drove were driven by pact.
Nor rest in a better sleep than Rhesus,
nor than the men who rode with Rhesus, companions
first of the road, and then of death;
than those whom, with the Rutulian Ramnes, energetic
Hyrtacides and Hyrtacides's companion gave to death.
Or like Cliniades, ringed with black fires,
bear limbs half-burnt to Stygian death.
And as for Remus who dared to cross the new walls,
may rustic weapons be harmful to your head.
Last, among Sarmatian and Getic arrows,
I pray you live and die in these places.
Let these be sent you for a while in a sudden little book,
lest you complain we are forgetful of you.
Few indeed, I confess: but may the gods give more than asked,
and multiply my vows with their favor.
Later you will read more, and bearing your true name,
and in the meter in which sharp wars ought to be waged.
