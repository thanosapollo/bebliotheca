* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The Spirit of God and the Word of God and the Reason of God, Word of Reason
and Reason of Word and Spirit, both of them Jesus Christ our Lord, has set for
us, the disciples of the New Testament, a new form of prayer. For in this
matter too it was right that new wine be stored in new skins, and a new patch
be sewn onto a new garment. Whatever had been before was either changed, as
circumcision; or supplied, as the rest of the Law; or fulfilled, as prophecy;
or perfected, as faith itself. The new grace of God has renewed all things
from the carnal into the spiritual, by laying the Gospel over them, the
canceller of all former antiquity, in which our Lord Jesus Christ was approved
as the Spirit of God and the Word of God and the Reason of God: Spirit by
which he was mighty, Word by which he taught, Reason by which he came. So the
prayer constituted by Christ is constituted from three things: from speech, by
which it is uttered; from spirit, by which it has such power. John too had
taught his disciples to pray; but all that belonged to John was laid down in
advance for Christ, until, when he had increased (as the same John used to
foretell that he must increase and John himself decrease), the forerunner's
whole work passed over with the spirit itself to the Lord.

That is why the words in which John taught them to pray are not even extant:
earthly things have given way to heavenly. He who is of the earth, he says,
speaks earthly things; and he who is here from the heavens speaks what he has
seen. And what of the Lord Christ's is not heavenly, including this rule of
praying as well? Let us then consider, blessed ones, his heavenly wisdom:
first the precept of praying in secret, by which he both required a man's
faith, that he should trust the sight and hearing of almighty God to be
present under roofs and even in a hidden place, and desired the modesty of
faith, that it should offer its worship to him alone whom it trusted to hear
and see everywhere. The wisdom that follows in the next precept belongs in the
same way to faith and to the modesty of faith, if we do not think the Lord
must be approached with a column of words, when we are sure he looks out for
his own unasked. And yet that brevity, to come to the third grade of wisdom,
is braced by the substance of a great and blessed interpretation, and as much
as it is compressed in words, so much it is poured out in meanings. For it has
not embraced only the proper offices of prayer, veneration of God or petition
for man, but almost every discourse of the Lord, every remembrance of the
discipline, so that in truth a digest of the whole Gospel is gathered into the
prayer.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

It begins with a testimony to God and with the reward of faith, when we say,
Father who art in the heavens. For we both pray to God and commend faith,
whose reward this name is. It is written, To those who believed in him he gave
power to be called sons of God. And yet the Lord very often proclaimed God as
Father to us; he even commanded that we call no one on earth father except the
one we have in the heavens. So in praying this way we also keep the precept.
Happy they who know their Father. This is the charge brought against Israel,
which the Spirit calls heaven and earth to witness: I have begotten sons, he
says, and they have not known me. But by saying Father we also name him God.
That title is one of piety and of power. Likewise in the Father the Son is
invoked. For I and the Father, he says, are one. Nor is even our mother the
Church passed by. For in the Son and the Father the mother is recognized, from
whom the name of both Father and Son stands. So by one kind or one word we
honor God with his own, we remember the precept, and we mark those who have
forgotten the Father.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

The name of God the Father had been published to no one. Even Moses, who had
asked him about it, had heard another name. It has been revealed to us in the
Son. For the Son is now the Father's new name. I have come, he says, in the
Father's name; and again, Father, glorify your name; and more openly, I have
manifested your name to men. So we ask that it be hallowed. Not that it is
fitting for men to wish God well, as if there were someone else from whom good
could be wished for him, or as if he would be in want unless we so wished.
Plainly it is proper that God be blessed in every place and time, because
every man always owes the memory of his benefits. But this petition also
serves as a blessing. For when is the name of God not holy and hallowed
through himself, since he hallows others from himself? The surrounding company
of angels does not cease to say to him, Holy, Holy, Holy. Therefore we too,
candidates for the angels if we deserve it, already learn from here that
heavenly cry toward God and the office of the glory to come. So much for the
glory of God. For the rest, as to our own petition, when we say, Hallowed be
thy name, we ask that it be hallowed in us who are in him, and at the same
time in the rest whom the grace of God still awaits, so that we may also obey
this precept by praying for all, even for our enemies. And therefore, leaving
the utterance hanging, we do not say, Hallowed be it in us, but we say it of
all.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

On this pattern we add, Thy will be done in heaven and on earth: not that
anyone stands in the way so that God's will should not be done, and we pray
for his will to succeed, but we ask that his will be done in all. For by a
figured reading of flesh and spirit we are heaven and earth. And even if it is
to be taken simply, the sense of the petition is still the same: that God's
will be done in us on earth, so that it may of course be done in heaven as
well. And what does God will except that we walk according to his discipline?
We therefore ask that he furnish us the substance and the power of his will,
that we may be saved both in heaven and on earth, because the sum of his will
is the salvation of those he has adopted. There is also that will of God which
the Lord carried out by preaching, working, and enduring. For if he himself
declared that he did not his own will but the Father's, then without doubt
what he did was the Father's will, to which we are now called as to patterns,
that we too may preach and work and endure even to death. To fulfill these
things we need the will of God. Likewise in saying, Thy will be done, we are
even wishing ourselves well, because there is nothing evil in God's will, even
if something otherwise is laid on us according to each one's deserts. Already
by this word we warn ourselves beforehand unto endurance. The Lord also, when
he had wished to show even in his own flesh the weakness of the flesh by the
reality of the passion, said, Father, take this cup away; and then,
remembering, Yet not my will, but thine be done. He himself was the will and
the power of the Father, and yet, to show the endurance due, he gave himself
over to the Father's will.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Thy kingdom come also belongs to the same thing as Thy will be done, in us,
that is. For when does God not reign, in whose hand is the heart of all kings?
But whatever we wish for ourselves we take as an omen for him, and we assign
to him what we look for from him. So if the bringing on of the Lord's kingdom
belongs to God's will and to our hanging upon it, how do some demand a
stretching of the world, when the kingdom of God, whose coming we pray for,
tends toward the consummation of the world? We wish to reign the sooner, and
not to serve the longer. Even if it had not been laid down in the prayer that
we should ask for the coming of the kingdom, we would have sent forth that cry
of our own accord, hurrying to the embrace of our hope. The souls of the
martyrs under the altar cry to the Lord in zeal: How long, Lord, do you not
avenge our blood on the dwellers of the earth? For their vengeance is surely
appointed from the end of the world. Nay, how quickly may thy kingdom come,
Lord: the wish of Christians, the confusion of the nations, the exultation of
angels, the thing for which we struggle, or rather the thing for which we
pray.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

But how finely the divine wisdom has arranged the order of the prayer, so that
after heavenly things, that is after God's name, God's will, and God's
kingdom, it should also make room in the petition for earthly needs! For the
Lord had also decreed, Seek first the kingdom, and then these things too will
be added to you. And yet we should rather take Give us this day our daily
bread spiritually. For Christ is our bread, because Christ is life and life is
bread. I am, he says, the bread of life. And a little above: The bread is the
Word of the living God, who came down from the heavens. Then his body too is
counted in bread: This is my body. So by asking for daily bread we ask for
continuance in Christ and for not being parted from his body. But because that
utterance is also admitted in a fleshly sense, it cannot happen without the
religion of the spiritual discipline either. For he commands that bread be
asked for, which alone is necessary for the faithful; the nations seek the
rest. So he both drives it home by examples and works it again in parables,
when he says, Does a father take bread from his children and give it to dogs?
Likewise, Does he give a stone to a son who asks for bread? For he shows what
sons look for from a father. That night-time knocker too was knocking for
bread. And he added with good reason, Give us this day, since he had said
before, Do not think about tomorrow, what you are to eat. To this he also
fitted the parable of that man who, when fruits came in, planned the widening
of his barns and long stretches of security, and died that very night.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

It followed that, after we had marked God's generosity, we should also pray
for his clemency. For what good will food do if we are truly being fattened on
it like a bull for the slaughter? The Lord knew that he alone was without
offense. So he teaches that we ask, Forgive us our debts. The petition for
pardon is confession, because he who asks pardon confesses the offense. So too
repentance is shown acceptable to God, who prefers it to the death of the
sinner. And debt in the scriptures is a figure of offense, because it is
likewise owed to judgment and exacted by it, and does not escape the justice
of the exaction unless the exaction is remitted, as the lord remitted the debt
to that slave. For to this the example of the whole parable looks. For the
same slave, once freed by his lord, does not likewise spare his own debtor,
and is therefore reported to the lord and handed to the torturer to pay the
last farthing, that is, even a slight offense: this fits, because we too
profess that we remit to our debtors. Already elsewhere from this form of the
prayer he says, Forgive, and it will be forgiven you. And when Peter had asked
whether a brother should be forgiven seven times, Nay, he said, seventy times
seven; so that he might recast the law for the better, because in Genesis
vengeance was reckoned seven times for Cain, but seventy times seven for
Lamech.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

To the fullness of so ready a prayer he added that we should plead not only
for offenses to be remitted but also for them to be turned away altogether:
Lead us not into temptation, that is, do not suffer us to be led into it, by
him of course who tempts. Far be it that the Lord should seem to tempt, as if
he either did not know each one's faith or were eager to cast it down.
Weakness and malice belong to the devil. For he had commanded Abraham to
sacrifice his son not to tempt his faith but to prove it, that through him he
might make an example for his precept, by which he was soon to command that no
pledges of love be held dearer than God. He himself, tempted by the devil,
showed who is the overseer and craftsman of temptation. He confirms this
place by later ones, saying, Pray that you may not be tempted. They were in
fact tempted, deserting the Lord, because they had given themselves to sleep
rather than to prayer. So the closing clause answers, interpreting what Lead
us not into temptation is. For this is: But carry us away from evil.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

In the short sums of so few words, how many edicts of the prophets, the
gospels, the apostles, how many discourses of the Lord, parables, examples,
precepts are touched! How many offices are discharged at once! The honor of
God in the Father, the testimony of faith in the Name, the offering of
obedience in the Will, the remembrance of hope in the Kingdom, the petition
for life in the Bread, the confession of debts in the plea, the anxiety of
temptations in the request for protection. What wonder? God alone could teach
how he wished to be prayed to. The religion of prayer, then, ordered by
himself, and already at that time, when it was being brought from the divine
mouth, animated by his own Spirit, climbs into heaven by its own privilege,
commending to the Father what the Son taught.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Since, however, the Lord, the foreseer of human needs, said separately after
the rule of praying had been handed over, Ask, and you shall receive, and
there are things to be asked according to each one's circumstance, once the
lawful and ordinary prayer has been laid down as a foundation, the desires
that come after have the right of building petitions on the outside, yet still
with memory of the precepts.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Lest we be as far from God's ears as we are from the precepts, the memory of
the precepts lays a road for prayers to heaven. The chief of them is that we
not go up to God's altar before we have undone whatever discord or offense we
have contracted with the brethren. For what sort of thing is it to come to the
peace of God without peace? To the remission of debts while we keep them? How
will a man angry at his brother appease the Father, when all anger has been
forbidden us from the beginning? For Joseph too, when he sent his brothers to
bring their father, said, And do not be angry on the way. He warned us, of
course (for elsewhere our discipline is called the Way), that when we are set
on the way of prayer we should not go to the Father with anger. After that the
Lord, openly enlarging the law, sets anger against a brother above murder. He
does not even allow it to be spent in an evil word; and if we must be angry,
not beyond the sun's going down, as the Apostle warns. But how rash it is
either to pass a day without prayer while you delay making it up with your
brother, or to lose your prayer by staying in a rage!

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

The intent of prayer ought to be free not from anger only, but from every
confusion of the mind at all, sent out from a spirit such as the Spirit to
whom it is sent. For a defiled spirit will not be able to be recognized by the
Holy Spirit, nor a sad one by a glad, nor a hampered one by a free. No one
receives an adversary; no one admits anyone but his match.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

For the rest, what reason is there in going to prayer with hands washed but
the spirit filthy, seeing that spiritual cleanliness is needed even for the
hands themselves, that they may be lifted pure from falsehood, from slaughter,
from cruelty, from poisonings, from idolatry, and from the other stains which,
conceived by the spirit, are carried out by the work of the hands? These are
the true cleanlinesses, not those that most people tend superstitiously,
taking water at every prayer, even when they come from a washing of the whole
body. When I inquired into this too scrupulously and asked for the reason, I
found it to be a memorial of the Lord's being handed over. We worship the
Lord; we did not hand him over. We ought even to set ourselves against the
example of the one who handed him over, and not wash our hands on that
account. Unless some stain of human intercourse is a matter of conscience, our
hands are otherwise clean enough, which we washed once with the whole body in
Christ.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

Israel may wash all his limbs daily, yet he is never clean. His hands at least
are always unclean, stained forever with the blood of the prophets and of the
Lord himself; and therefore, hereditary guilty ones by the conscience of the
fathers, they do not even dare to lift them to the Lord, lest some Isaiah cry
out, lest Christ shudder. We, however, do not only lift them but even spread
them, and, patterned on the Lord's passion, we also confess Christ as we pray.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

But since we have touched one empty observance, it will not be a burden to
mark the rest as well, which deserve to be charged with vanity, since they are
done without the authority of any precept of the Lord or of an apostle. Things
of this kind are counted not to religion but to superstition, affected and
forced and of a curious rather than a reasonable office, to be checked at
least on this ground, that they put us on a level with the nations. As it is
with some who pray with their cloaks laid aside; for so the nations go to
their idols. If this ought to be done, the apostles, who teach about the dress
of praying, would have included it; unless someone thinks Paul left his cloak
with Carpus in prayer. God, I suppose, would not hear men in cloaks, who heard
the three saints praying in the furnace of the Babylonian king with their
trousers and turbans on.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Likewise I do not see the reason for the custom some have of sitting down when
prayer is finished, except the one children want. For what if that Hermas,
whose writing is commonly titled The Shepherd, after prayer was done, had not
sat down on the bed but had done something else: would we claim that too for
an observance? Surely not. For it is put simply even now, When I had prayed
and had sat down on the bed, for the order of the story, not as a model of
discipline. Otherwise there will be nowhere to pray except where there is a
bed. Nay, anyone who sat in a chair or on a bench would be acting against the
writing. Further, since the nations do the same, sitting down after they have
adored their little images, it deserves on that account to be blamed in us,
because it is practiced among the idols. To this is added the charge of
irreverence, which even the nations themselves would understand if they had
any sense. If it is irreverent to sit under the eye and over against the eye
of him whom you most revere and venerate, how much more is this deed most
irreligious under the eye of the living God, while the angel of prayer is
still standing by? Unless we are throwing it up to God that prayer has tired
us.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

But we commend our prayers to God more when we pray with modesty and humility,
with not even the hands lifted too high, but lifted with measure and decency,
and with not even the face raised into boldness. For that publican who prayed
humbled and cast down not only in his plea but in his face as well went away
more justified than the most shameless Pharisee. The sounds of the voice too
ought to be kept down. Or how large windpipes would we need, if we are heard
by the sound? But God is the hearer of the heart, not of the voice, just as he
is its watcher. The demon of the Pythian oracle says, I understand the mute,
and I hear the one who does not speak. Do God's ears wait for sound? How then
could Jonah's prayer from the bottom of the whale's belly make its way to
heaven through the entrails of so great a beast, from the abysses themselves,
through so great a mass of sea? What more do those who pray more loudly gain,
except that they din upon their neighbors? Nay, by giving their petitions
away, what less do they do than if they prayed in public?

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

Another custom has already grown strong. Those who are fasting, after prayer
with the brethren, withhold the kiss of peace, which is the seal of prayer.
But when is peace more to be conferred with the brethren than when, at the
time of the observance, prayer goes up more commendable, so that they
themselves may share in our observance, and be softened for settling their own
peace with a brother? What prayer is whole when divorced from the holy kiss?
Whom does peace hinder when he is doing his office to the Lord? What sort of
sacrifice is it from which men leave without peace? Whatever the prayer may
be, it will not be better than keeping the precept by which we are commanded
to hide our fasts. For already by withholding the kiss we are known to be
fasting. But even if there is some reason, still, lest you be guilty against
this precept, you can perhaps at home, among those from whom the fast cannot
be hidden altogether, put the peace off. But anywhere else you can hide your
observance, you ought to remember the precept; so you will satisfy discipline
abroad and custom at home. So too on the day of the Passover, when the
religion of the fast is common and as it were public, we rightly lay the kiss
aside, caring nothing about hiding what we do with everyone.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

Likewise on the days of the Stations most think they must not take part in the
prayers of the sacrifices, because the Station would be broken by receiving
the Lord's body. Does the Eucharist then undo an obedience devoted to God, or
does it bind it more to God? Will your Station not be more solemn if you have
also stood at God's altar? When the Lord's body has been received and
reserved, both are kept safe, the sharing in the sacrifice and the carrying
out of the office. If the Station took its name from the military example (for
we too are God's soldiery), surely no joy or sadness that comes upon the camp
cuts off the soldiers' stations. For joy will keep the discipline more gladly,
sadness more carefully.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

As for dress, at least of women, the variety of observance makes it that we,
men of no standing, most of all after the most holy apostle, treat the matter
shamelessly, except that it is not shameless if we treat it according to the
apostle. On modesty of dress and ornament there is an open prescription even
of Peter, who with the same mouth, because with the same Spirit as Paul,
checks the glory of clothes and the pride of gold and the pimping labor of the
hair.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

But what is observed mixed through the churches as if uncertain must be
treated: whether virgins ought to be veiled or not. For those who grant
virgins immunity of the head seem to rest on this, that the apostle designated
not virgins by name but women as those who must be veiled, and not the sex, so
as to say females, but a rank of the sex, by saying women. For if he had named
the sex, by saying females, he would have defined it absolutely of every
woman; but when he names one rank of the sex, he sets the other apart by
silence. For he could, they say, either have named virgins specially as well,
or, by a shortcut, females generally.

* Chapter 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

Those who grant this ought to think again about the standing of the word
itself, what woman is from the first letters of the holy records. For they
find it to be the name of the sex, not a rank of the sex; since God surnamed
Eve, who had not yet known a man, both woman and female: female, as the sex
generally; woman, as a rank of the sex specially. So, because then Eve, still
unwedded, was under the word woman, that word was made common to the virgin as
well. Nor is it a wonder if the apostle, driven by the same Spirit by which
all divine scripture, and that Genesis too, was drawn up, used the same voice
in putting woman, which by the example of unwedded Eve belongs also to a
virgin. The rest, in short, agrees. For by this very fact, that he did not
name virgins, as he does in another place where he teaches about marrying, he
sufficiently declares that it was said of every woman and of the whole sex,
and that no distinction was made of the virgin, when he does not name her at
all. For he who elsewhere remembers to distinguish, where of course difference
requires it (and he distinguishes by marking each kind with its own words),
where he does not distinguish, while he names neither, wants no difference to
be understood. What of this, that in the Greek speech in which the apostle
wrote his letters it is the custom to call them women rather than females,
that is, γυναῖκας rather than θηλείας? Therefore if that word is frequently
used for the name of the sex, which in interpretation stands for female, he
named the sex when he said γυναῖκα. And in the sex the virgin also is
included.

But there is also a plain pronouncement: Every woman, he says, praying and
prophesying with head uncovered dishonors her head. What is every woman, if
not of every age, every rank, every condition? Saying Every, he excepts
nothing of woman, just as he excepts nothing of man from not being veiled; for
likewise he says Every man. As, then, in the male sex under the name of man
even the unbearded youth is forbidden to be veiled, so in the female under the
name of woman even the virgin is commanded to be veiled. Equally in each sex
let the lesser age follow the discipline of the greater; or else let male
virgins also be veiled, if female virgins are not veiled, because they too are
not bound by name. Let man and unbearded youth be different, if woman and
virgin are different. He says they ought to be veiled because of the angels,
because the angels revolted from God because of the daughters of men. Who then
would contend that only women, that is, those already married and done with
virginity, were objects of desire, unless virgins too are not allowed to excel
in looks and find lovers? Nay, let us see whether they desired virgins alone,
since scripture says the daughters of men, because it could have named the
wives of men, or females without distinction. Also what it says, And they took
them to themselves for wives, works this way: those are taken for wives who
are free, of course. Of those not free it would have spoken otherwise. So they
are free by widowhood as well as by virginity. Thus by naming the sex
generally as daughters he mixed the kinds into the genus. Again, when he says
that nature herself teaches that females must be veiled, since she assigned
hair as a covering and an ornament to women, has not the same covering and the
same honor of the head been assigned to virgins as well? If it is shameful for
a woman to be shorn, it is so for a virgin likewise. In those, then, for whom
one condition of the head is counted, one discipline of the head is required
as well, even for those virgins whom childhood defends; for from the first she
was named female. So Israel too observes it. But if Israel did not observe it,
our law, enlarged and supplied, would defend the addition for itself. Let it
be excused for putting the veil on virgins as well.

Now let the age that does not know its sex keep the privilege of simplicity.
For Eve and Adam too, when it befell them to know, at once covered what they
had recognized. At least in those in whom girlhood has already changed, the
age ought to be generous of itself both to nature and to discipline. For they
are being released to women both in their members and in their offices. No one
is a virgin from the time she can marry, since age has already married in her
to its own husband, that is, to time. But some woman has vowed herself to God.
From then on she both reshapes her hair and turns her whole dress to that of a
woman. Let her then assert the whole, and render the whole of a virgin: what
she hides for God's sake, let her plainly overshadow. It is our concern to
commend what the grace of God works to the conscience of God alone, lest we
take from man the pay we hope for from God. Why do you bare before God what
you cover before men? Will you be more modest in public than in the church? If
it is God's grace, and you have received it, why do you boast, he says, as if
you had not received it? Why do you judge others by showing yourself? Or do
you by your glory invite others to the good? And yet you yourself are in
danger of losing it if you boast, and you drive others to the same dangers.
What is taken up from a passion for glory is easily broken. Be veiled, virgin,
if you are a virgin; for you ought to blush. If you are a virgin, do not
suffer many eyes. Let no one wonder at your face; let no one perceive your
falsehood. You do well to feign yourself married if you veil your head. Nay,
you do not seem to feign; for you have married Christ. You have handed your
flesh over to him; act according to your husband's discipline. If he commands
other men's brides to be veiled, his own much more. But let no one think the
institution of his predecessor is to be moved. Many bind their own prudence
and constancy to someone else's custom. Granted they not be compelled to be
veiled, those who do it willingly ought at least not to be forbidden; who
cannot deny themselves to be virgins either, content to wear down their own
name by the security of conscience before God. Of those, however, who are
promised to husbands I can with constancy beyond my small measure pronounce
and attest that they are to be veiled from that day on which they first shrank

married beforehand: age through ripeness, flesh through age, spirit through

Prayer suffers a variety of observance about kneeling as well, through a few
who refrain from kneeling on the Sabbath. Since this disagreement is
especially on trial among the churches, the Lord will give his grace that they
either yield, or use their own opinion without scandal to others. We, however,
as we have received, ought on the day of the Lord's resurrection alone to
beware not of this only, but of every habit and office of anxiety, putting off
even business, lest we give the devil any place. The same in the space of
Pentecost, which we discharge with the same solemnity of exultation. For the
rest, who would doubt on every day to prostrate himself before God, at least
in the first prayer with which we enter on the light? But at fasts and
Stations no prayer should be celebrated without the knee and the rest of the
custom of humility. For we are not only praying, but also pleading, and making
satisfaction to God our Lord. Of times of prayer nothing at all has been
prescribed, except plainly to pray at every time and place.

But how in every place, when we are forbidden in public? In every place, he
says, which opportunity or even necessity has brought. For what the apostles
did, who in prison prayed and sang to God with the guards listening, is not
counted against the precept; nor what Paul did, who on the ship made the
Eucharist before all.

As for time, an outside observance of certain hours will not be idle either,
those common ones I mean which mark the intervals of the day, the third, the
sixth, the ninth, which we may find more solemn in the scriptures. The Holy
Spirit was first poured on the gathered disciples at the third hour. Peter, on
the day he experienced the vision of the community of all in that vessel, had
gone up to the higher parts at the sixth hour for the sake of prayer. The same
man was going with John to the temple at the ninth, where he restored the
paralytic to his own health. Even if these stand simply, without any precept
of observance, still it would be good to set some presumption which may both
bind the reminder to pray and, as by a law, wrench us at times from business
to such a duty, so that, as we read was observed by Daniel too, surely from
Israel's discipline, we may pray at least not less than three times a day,
debtors as we are to three, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit;
besides, of course, the lawful prayers which are owed without any reminder at
the entrance of light and of night. But it also becomes the faithful not to
take food, and not to go to the bath, before they have put a prayer in
between. For the refreshments and foods of the spirit are to be held prior to
those of the flesh, and heavenly things prior to earthly.

Do not dismiss a brother who has entered your house without prayer (You have
seen a brother, he says, you have seen your Lord), especially a stranger, lest
he perhaps be an angel. Nor, when you yourself have been received by brethren,
put earthly refreshments before heavenly. For your faith will be judged at
once. Or how will you say, according to the precept, Peace to this house,
unless you also give mutual peace back to those who are in the house?

The more diligent in praying are accustomed to join Alleluia to their prayers,
and psalms of that kind, to whose closes those who are together respond. And
it is surely an excellent institution, whatever, by setting God first and
honoring him, is fitted to bring a prayer well filled, like a rich victim.

For this is the spiritual victim which has abolished the former sacrifices.
What is the multitude of your sacrifices to me? he says. I am full of burnt
offerings of rams, and I do not want the fat of lambs and the blood of bulls
and of goats. For who has required these from your hands? What God has sought,
then, the Gospel teaches. The hour will come, he says, when the true
worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth. For God is a
Spirit, and he therefore seeks such worshipers. We are the true worshipers and
the true priests, who, praying in spirit, sacrifice in spirit a prayer, the
victim proper and acceptable to God, which of course he has sought, which he
has looked out for himself. This victim, devoted from the whole heart, fed on
faith, tended by truth, whole in innocence, clean in chastity, crowned with
love, we ought to lead with the pomp of good works among psalms and hymns to
God's altar, to obtain all things for us from God.

* Chapter 29
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h29
:END:

For what has God, who requires it, denied to prayer that comes from spirit and
truth? We read and hear and believe how great the proofs of its efficacy are.
Old prayer, indeed, used to free from fires and from beasts and from famine,
and yet it had not received its form from Christ. But how much more largely
Christian prayer works! It does not station the angel of dew in the midst of
the fires, nor shut the lions' mouths, nor carry over the country people's
meal to the hungry; it does not, by a delegated grace, turn away any sense of
suffering, but it equips those who suffer and feel and hurt with endurance; it
enlarges grace by virtue, so that faith may know what it obtains from the
Lord, understanding what it suffers for God's name. But in former days prayer
also used to inflict blows, pour out the armies of enemies, hold back the
usefulness of rains. Now, however, the prayer of righteousness turns away all
God's anger, keeps watch for enemies, pleads for persecutors. Is it a wonder
if it knows how to wring the waters of heaven, which was able to obtain fires
as well? Prayer alone is what conquers God; but Christ willed that it work
nothing evil. He conferred all its power from the good. So it knows nothing
except to call back the souls of the dead from the very road of death, to
restore the weak, to remedy the sick, to cleanse the possessed, to open prison
bars, to loose the bonds of the innocent. The same washes away offenses,
drives back temptations, puts out persecutions, consoles the faint-hearted,
delights the great-hearted, escorts travelers, calms waves, stuns robbers,
feeds the poor, governs the rich, raises the fallen, holds up those who are
falling, keeps those who stand. Prayer is the wall of faith, our arms and
missiles against the enemy who watches us on every side. So let us never go
unarmed. By day let us remember the Station, by night the vigil. Under the
arms of prayer let us keep the standard of our commander; let us wait for the
angel's trumpet as we pray. All the angels pray as well, every creature prays,
cattle and wild beasts pray and bend their knees, and coming out of stalls and
dens they look up to heaven with no idle mouth, shaking their breath after
their own manner. But the birds too, rising from the nest, are lifted toward
heaven, and spread the cross of their wings for hands, and say something that
may seem a prayer. What more, then, of the office of prayer? Even the Lord
himself prayed, to whom be honor and power forever and ever.
