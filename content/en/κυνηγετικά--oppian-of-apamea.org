To you, blessed one, I sing, glorious prop of the earth, well-loved light of
the warlike sons of Aeneas, sweet scion of Ausonian Zeus, Antoninus: him great
Domna bore to great Severus, having lain with the blessed man and in labor
borne a blessed child, a bride of a most excellent husband, a mother of fair
offspring, an Assyrian Cytherea and a Selene that does not fail, in no way
weaker than the lineage of Zeus son of Cronus. /Be gracious, Titan Phaethon
and Phoebus Apollo./ To him therefore his father, having toiled with great
hands, gave to hold all the dry land and all the wet as well. For you the
all-giving field swells in fruit, and again the famous tribes of the sea feed
you in fair weather.

For you all the streams flow from Ocean, and famous Dawn runs smiling and
bright. Therefore I long to sing the famous devices of the hunt. This Calliope
bids me, this Artemis herself. I heard, as is lawful, I heard the divine
voice, and I answered the goddess. First she spoke to me such things as these:

Artemis. Rise, and let us tread the rough path which no one among mortals has
yet walked with his own songs. Oppian. Be gracious, queenly goddess, and what
you desire in your heart we shall tell with our mortal voice. Artemis. I do
not wish you now to sing the three-year reveler of the mountain, nor the
chorus beside the depths of Aonian Asopus. Oppian. We shall leave, as you bid,
the nocturnal rites of Sabazius. Often I have danced around Thyonaean
Dionysus. Artemis. Do not tell the race of heroes, nor seafaring Argo, nor the
wars of mortals, nor sing for me the Bane of Men. Oppian. I will not speak of
wars, nor the worst works of Ares. I have told the woes of the Parthians and
Ctesiphon.

Artemis. Keep silence about deadly loves, and leave the girdles. I hate what
they call the playthings of her born of the sea. Oppian. We have heard,
blessed one, that you are uninitiated in marriage. Artemis. Sing the wars of
beasts and of hunting men. Sing the breeds of hounds and the shifting tribes
of horses, swift-minded counsels, the works of profitable tracking. Tell me
the hates of beasts, sing their loves and the bridal chambers in the mountains
of tearless Cytherea and the birthings among beasts of a midwife-less
lying-in. Such were the covenants of the daughter of great Zeus. I heard, I
sing. May I cast a well-aimed sound. But you, ruling from the rising to Ocean,
glad in fair weather under your ambrosial brows, grant a right hand
all-gracious, giver of bliss, to the earth and to cities and to songs that
hunt well. God granted to men a threefold hunt, of the air, of the land, and
the lovely one of the sea. But the contest is not equal. For how could equal
things be made, to haul a gasping fish from the depths, and to draw
long-winged birds from the air, or to contend with murderous beasts in the
mountains? Not even for the fisherman, and not without reason for the fowler,

is the catch without toil. But with toil delight attends alone, and there is
no slaughter. They are bloodless. The one, sitting on seaside rocks with
rounded reeds and bloodied hooks, the fearless angler has bound the cunning
fish. And the joy is when, piercing him under the jaws of bronze, he carries
through the air the sea dancer leaping high from the depths, gasping. Yes, and
for the fowler the toil is sweet. For they do not go to the hunt bearing sword
or sickle or bronze spears, but with them into the thickets a hawk goes as
companion, and long cords and wet honey-colored birdlime, and the reeds that
walk the path through the air. Who would dare to sing these things as equal in
the balance? Or who would match an eagle against the kingly lion? Who would
liken a moray to the venom of leopards, or jackals to hawks, or rhinoceroses
to hedgehogs,

or a gull to wild goats, or all the sea-monsters to an elephant? Hunters have
slain wolves, fishermen tunnies, huntsmen sheep, reed-men have taken doves,
beaters a bear, and anglers a mormyr, horsemen a tiger, and fish-strikers red
mullets, trackers a boar, fowlers nightingales. But you, Nereus, and the
daemons of Amphitrite, and the chorus of bird-loving Dryads, be gracious. For
dear songs call me this way and that. I turn back to sing to the beast-slaying
gods. First let my young men not be very fat. For there is need, a forced
need, to leap a towering horse over crags, and need to jump a ditch. And often
in the thickets one must chase the beast, lightening with the feet and with
well-burdened limbs. Therefore let them not go fat to the labor of the hunt,
nor yet too lean. For sometimes the man of many catches must contend with
warlike beasts. Therefore let them bear a body mixed thus, both swift to run
and strong to fight. And in the right hand let them brandish double long
javelins, and a sickle at the middle of the belt.

For they would prepare bitter slaughter even for beasts, and they would carry
defenses against wicked men. With the left the man on foot would lead the
dogs, and the horseman would guide the steering bit of the horses. Let him
draw the tunic neatly, fastening it even to the thigh, and let it be bound
with interchangeable straps. And from the neck on either side let a hanging
garment from the hands wrap backward over the strong shoulders, easier for
toil. And let those go with bare feet whose care is the hard-to-see tracks of
beasts, so that the sound of sandals rubbed under sleek feet may not take
sleep from the eye of the beasts. Nor is it much better to have a cloak. For a
garment often moved by the breath of a sounding wind has startled beasts, and
they have started up to flee. Thus let the hunters array their swift body
well. For such the Letoan shooter of arrows loves. And they go after beasts at
a different hour at different times, at the day's standing, and at the day's
ending, and at midday, and sometimes at evening. And sometimes even in
darkness they have mastered beasts under the rays of the moon. Dawn is
stretched all well-fitted for the hunter, all calm, for all-day courses in
leaf-bearing spring and leaf-shedding autumn.

For the well-tempered seasons are outstanding for horses and for mortals and
for flesh-eating dogs to run, in golden spring, driver of the cold clouds,
when the sea is passable for seafarers as they sail, stretching the white
tackle of linen-winged ships; when the earth rejoices in men who tend plants;
when she also looses her knots in buds and flowers; or again at the last
autumnal turnings, when the house of the fruit-gathering farmer is in bloom,
when the fruit of Athena fills the sleek pail and the cluster of the
cultivated vines rejoices as it presses the vats, when the hives of bees are
heavy with lily-like combs. But in midwinter let them hunt at midday, when
someone, having withdrawn under a cave in the thickets, gathering dry sticks
and heaping a short-lived flame, has lain near the fire and prepared supper, a
companion of the hunt. And in summer there is need to flee the flaming rebuke
and the drought of the sun. I bid them come to the contest

under first twilight, when at dawn the country people under the well-made
handle of the yoke-pole drive the earth-cutting plow with heifers; or again at
evening when the sun declines the yokes, when the herdsmen give signs to their
flocks, when they go down again to their own folds, heavy in the breasts and
darkening the udders; and those leaping out unceasingly from the stone stalls
all skip around their dear mothers, around the well-eyed heifers the
grim-faced calves, and around the well-horned sheep the bleating lambs, the
kids around the bleating goats, and the swift foals around the grazing mares.
And let the strong toilers of the glorious hunt carry so many weapons to the
glens and thickets, gear that greatly breathes well-hunting slaughter:
well-twisted nets and withies and the long all-catcher, and nets and props and
the many-groaning bonds of nooses, a three-barbed spear, a broad-headed
hunting-spear, a snare-club and shafts and a well-feathered swift arrow,

swords and ox-goads and a hare-killing trident, crooked hooks and lead-bound
crooks, a cord bound with esparto and a well-plaited foot-trap, and knots and
stakes and a many-eyed dragnet. And let them lead to the hunt horses greatly
glorious, males. Not only because the females are weaker in the feet to
complete a long course in the thickets, but because one must shun the
bed-loving heart of the swift-footed horses, and keep the mare-herd far away,
so that they may not neigh, longing for love, and the fawns and swift gazelles
and the fearful hare, hearing them fully, may not go to cold flight. The
shifting tribes of horses are as many as the countless nations of men, as many
as the races divided among mortals who eat grain. But nevertheless I will tell
those who among all are strong, and those who excel in the horse companies:

Tyrrhenians, Sicilians, Cretans, Mazices, Achaeans, Cappadocians, Moors,
Scythians, Magnesians, Epeians, Ionians, Armenians, Libyans, Thracians,
Erembi. And among all they have judged the horse most excellent

who are skilled in hippodromes and guardians of herds, who is crowned over his
whole body with such looks: let him lift a head a little high, raised above
the neck, himself being large, with rounded limbs; the head high, and let the
lowest jaw nod toward the neck.

Let the mid-brow be broad and bright. And from the temples around the forehead
let dense clusters of hair shake. The eye keen, fire-faced, blood-red in the
brows. Wide nostrils, a sufficient mouth, small ears. Let the neck of the
shaggy-necked horse be rounded, as when a crested helmet nods its plume. Let
the chest be much, the body long, the back broad, and a double spine fattening
the middle of the haunches.

And let a long-haired tail run much behind. The thighs well-set, muscular. And
below, the rounded pipes of the feet, straight-stretched and long and very
slender. And limbs left of flesh, like those of long-horned storm-footed deer.
And let the ankle bend, and let a circling hoof run very high from the earth,
dense, horny, strong. Such a horse would I have go, raging, a
fellow-contestant, warlike, mighty, into the strong war of beasts. Such are
the Tyrrhenians and Armenians and Achaeans and the famous Cappadocians who
dwell before Taurus. And I have seen a great wonder among the swift-footed
Cappadocians. So long as they bear a newborn tooth under the mouth and a milky
body, they are weak. But they become swifter the more they grow old. Those you
would arm for great-hearted war and against blazing beasts. For they are very
bold to meet arms, to break a dense phalanx,

and to contend opposite warlike beasts. For how has a warlike horse heard in
battles the war-stirring sound of the long war-pipes? Or how has he looked
opposite with unblinking eyes at a company of young men packed with hoplites,
and bronze gleaming, and iron flashing, and learned when there is need to
wait, and when again to rush, and learned to hear the signal of strong
captains? Often he has also driven the shield-bearing winged strife of men
against towers, quiet, when for mortals shield leans aslant on shield over the
head, when they long to sack the city of enemies, and they make a raised
plain, of seven oxhides, cunning, dense, many-bossed, and opposite the sunlike
gleam of bronze leaps forth, and at once behind as the ray is bent much air
flashes. For skillful nature has given horses beyond others the heart of
mortals and a shifting heart in the breast. They always know their own dear
charioteer and they neigh when they see their famous leader and they groan
greatly for a companion fallen in wars. A horse in the melee once broke the
bonds of silence and overran the laws of nature and took a human voice

and a tongue like that of men. The horse of the warlike Macedonian king,
Bucephalas, used to contend opposite arms. A horse ran on the asphodel with
light feet, another over the sea, and did not wet the rim. A horse over the
clouds led the Chimera-slaying man, and a foal neighing once by the wiles of a
charioteer set the king of the Asia-born Persians. And they honor nature
outstandingly. And it is wholly unheard of for them to go to a love that is
not lawful. But they remain unstained of abominations, and they love a pure
Cytherea. I have heard how formerly one of the wealthy lords had a fair herded
company of horses in the plains. All these later a horse disease, having
mastered them from the roots, left two, a mother alone and still a foal at the
breast of his dear mother. But when he was grown, the cruel man tried to cast
the mother of his own child in his arms. And when he saw them refusing love
and marriage sworn against for both, at once then, aiming terrible things, he
wove a cunning plan, hoping to call the race back again for the horses. First,
plotting in the deep, he covered both with other skins. Then he anointed the
whole body with fragrant, incense-scented oil. For he hoped to destroy the
scent that leads love.

And he escaped notice, O blessed ones, doing evils. And it was accomplished, a
strange, abhorred bridal, most hateful to horses, such as among men was
formerly wedded, the Cadmeian terrible marriage of wandering Oedipus. And
when, stripped, they knew their own ruin, and looked with slanting eyes in
distress, she the wretched at her barren offspring, and he at once the
terrible-wedded, ill-bedded, at his unmothered wretched mother, they rose very
high, snorting implacably, and having broken the bonds they went neighing
greatly, as if calling the blessed gods to witness of the wickedness, and
praying curses on the much-harming bridegroom. And late, weeping and rushing
ungovernably, dashing their own heads on the rocks opposite, they crushed the
bones together, and they robbed themselves of their own light, self-slayers,
having bent their heads upon one another. Thus report sings great glory for
horses among those of old. Of all the races of horses that countless earth has
reared, swiftest are the Sicilians, who dwell at Lilybaeum and the
three-headed mountain where the covering of Enceladus, as he belches the
thunderbolt with heavenly fires, has splashed up the everlasting fire of
Sicilian Aetna. But swifter than the Sicilians beside the streams of Euphrates
the Armenians and Parthians of deep locks are. But even beyond the Parthians
the Iberians excel, beating the plain with swifter feet. Against those perhaps
only an eagle would match,

driving in the heavenly hollows, or a hawk shaken with long wings, or a
dolphin sliding in the grey surge. So swift are the Iberians in windy feet.
But they are few and small in might and weak in heart, and they are put to
shame in the course in few stades. They have clothed a famous body with
bright-shaped looks, but a hoof not strong, mud-bred, broad-soled. The
shifting tribes of the Moors far excel all in long courses and in painful
toils. And the Libyans after them complete the long course, as many as dwell
in pebble-rich Cyrene. The looks of both are alike, except that again the
Libyans are larger to see and have become strong, but long in body. For they
have around the ribs more of a weaver's comb than the others. Therefore they
are thicker to see and stronger to rush straight, good at bearing the fiery
onset of the sun and the sharp midday rebuke of thirst. The Tyrrhenian horses
and the immense Cretan tribes are both swift to run and long. The Sicilians
are swifter than the Moors, and the Parthians than the Sicilians, and they are
bright-eyed and outstandingly gleaming, and they alone await the great roar of
the lion. For for different beasts different races of horses are well fitted,
which the eyes tell you. Against spotted-footed deer you would arm dark-eyed
ones,

against bears grey-eyed, and against leopards tawny, and blazing fire-shining
horses around swine, but bright-eyed gleaming ones against gleaming lions. And
in beauty among all the Nisaean horse is far supreme, whom wealthy kings lead:
fair to see, and tender to bear with a well-obedient bond; small in the head,
much in the deep-haired neck, glorying on either side in honey-golden manes.
Yes, and you would look on another lovely race, spotted, conspicuous, whom
they call orynges, either because they grow in the fair-haired mountains, or
because they wholly wish to rush upon the females. Two flowery beauties are
upon the orynges. For some have the neck and fair-haired broad back written
with long close bands, like swift tigers, the race of rushing Zephyrus. And
others are cunningly wrought around with well-rounded circling seals, dense,
like leopards. These while still infant skilled men have written, firing the
long hair with burning bronze. And often mortals have planned other all-wise
things, to mark a foal even while still in the mother's womb.

O how much heart, how much mind there is among mortals. They have done as they
wish. They have made horses of many looks, still held in the milky flanks of
the mother. For when the impulse of love has taken the female and she awaits
the famous great-hearted horse coming near, then they adorn the fair husband.
And all around they write the whole body about with well-spotted colors, and
they lead him to the bed, exulting in splendor. And as some youth among women
who dress a bride, crowned with white garments and purple flowers, and
breathing Palestinian perfume, goes into the chamber singing hymen hymenaeus:
so the horse hastening and neighing the marriage, the famous husband foaming
before his wife, they hold back long, longing for the dear bed. And late they
let him go into lovely love. And she, conceiving, has borne a flowery child,
having taken up in the womb the fertile seed of the husband, but having
received the many-colored form with the eyes. Such things also those whose
care is reeds, the fowlers, have planned with dense thoughts, when they adorn
chicks for pigeons. For when the swift doves go to love, mixing with their
deep-voiced wives at the mouths, then the famous tamer weaves a plan, and near
the females he has set many cunning purple garments. And they, casting their
eyes aslant, warming the heart, bear children of sea-purple.

Yes, and thus the Laconians have planned wisely for their dear wives, when
they swell the belly. Having written on tablets they set near the splendid
beauties, the youths who formerly flashed among mortals, Nireus and Narcissus
and Hyacinthus of the ashen spear, and well-helmeted Castor and Amycus-slaying
Polydeuces, and young lads who are admirable among the blessed, laurel-haired
Phoebus and ivy-bearing Dionysus. And they take delight seeing the well-loved
look, and they bear fair children, having fallen upon beauty. So much about
horses. But come down, dear mind, to the path of the whelps. And among all
dogs so many are outstandingly conspicuous, and they are much the care of
hunters: Paeonians, Ausonians, Carians, Thracians, Iberians, Arcadians,
Argives, Lacedaemonians, Tegeatans, Sarmatians, Celts, Cretans, Magnesians,
Amorgians,

and as many as on the many-sanded banks of Egypt are guardians of herds,
Locrians, and bright-eyed Molossians. And if it is dear to you to mix two
races, first in spring prepare the bed for the dogs. For in spring the works
of love are more the care of beasts and whelps and deadly serpents and airy
birds and sea-going creatures. In spring the viper, stained with angry venom,
has come to the bed toward the shore of his sea wife. In spring the whole sea
thunders around for Cytherea, and the creatures marrying bristle on the calm.
In spring also the doves drive upon the pigeons, and horses arm against the
field-grazing mares, and bulls rush upon the wild heifers, and the curling
rams in spring mount the sheep, and fiery boars spear against sows, and
he-goats ride upon shaggy she-goats. And even for mortals themselves loves are
more in spring. For in spring Cytherea presses upon all the people. Let the
nations of dogs as you tend the chamber be your care, fitting ones and tribes
outstandingly like one another. Mix Arcadians with Eleans, Cretans with
Paeonians, Carians with Thracians, Tyrrhenian races with Laconians, and you
would bring a Sarmatian husband to an Iberian bride. Thus you would mix well.
But far the best of all

is for the tribes to remain single-bred, and these outstanding ones the
hunting men have marked. And the tribes are countless, of whom from some
source let the shapes and looks be such. Long, a strong sufficient body, and a
light head, well-eyed. Let the eyes gleam dark. Let the mouth be jagged and
stretched. And above let small ears be wrapped in slender membranes. The neck
long, and the breast below strong, broad. And let the front feet be smaller.
Straight-stretched long long-oared masts of the limbs. Broad shoulder-blades,
crosswise flats of the ribs. Loins well-fleshed, not fat. And behind let the
tail be firm and stretched and long-shadowed. Let such be armed for the long
courses against gazelles and deer and the storm-footed hare. And others again
are rushing, in whom is battle-abiding strength, as many as have attacked
well-bearded bulls and have slain insolent swine rushing upon them; as many as
do not fear even lions their own lords,

well-fed, like high-crested headlands. They are rather snub in the faces, and
above the mid-brows nod terribly with the brows, and fiery eyes gleaming under
bright glances. The whole hide is shaggy. A strong body. A broad back. They
are not swift, but there is much might within, and unspeakable strength, pure,
and a shameless spirit. For the hunt then arm such races of spearman whelps,
who chase all monsters. White colors and very dark ones are very bad. For they
cannot quickly bear the might of the sun nor the might of the snow-struck
much-wintry season. Those excel among all dogs whose shapes are very like
flesh-eating beasts, sheep-slaying wolves or windy tigers or foxes and swift
leopards or as many as have a look all-like Demeter, grain-colored. For they
are very swift and strong. And if wise whelp-rearing is your care, never milk
the whelps at the newly blooming breast of goats or sheep, nor at house dogs.
For they would grow sluggish and worthless and heavy. But at the breast of
deer or perhaps of a tame lioness or perhaps of gazelles or of a night-going
she-wolf. For thus you would make them outstandingly strong and swift, like
the milk-bearing nurses themselves.

And on infant whelps set short names, all swift, so that he may hear a swift
utterance. And let them be companions to the strong hunting horses from still
infants, and to all mortals habitual and dear, and enemies to beasts alone.
And let them not wish to bark. For silence is the law for hunters, and
outstandingly for trackers. And the looks of hard-to-see tracking are double,
of men and of dogs. Mortals of shifting counsel have marked and observed the
paths with the eyes. But dogs have signaled all the tracks with the nostrils.
Yes, and for men the winter season is well-fitted, and they track with
toilless eyes, because all things written in the snows are seen together and
remain stamped in the muds like a sole.

Spring is hateful to dogs, and autumn is dear. For in spring the grass-feeding
earth is full enough with plants and many-breathing with flowers, and all
around well-crowned meadows purple untilled, and the fields destroy for
well-nosed tracking dogs every elder scent. But in well-fruited
sweet-clustered autumn

grasses and plants and flowers grow old, and the beastly breath remains naked
for the whelps. And there is a valiant race of tracking whelps, small, but
worth being of a great song. The wild tribes of the many-backed Britons reared
them. And by surname they have named them Agassaeans. Their size is like
worthless greedy house table-dogs, rounded, most fleshless, shaggy-haired,
sluggish in the eyes, but the feet armed with painful claws and sharpened with
frequent poison-bearing dog-teeth. And in the nostrils again the Agassaeus is
most outstanding and all-best in tracking. For he is very wise at finding the
track of those going even on the earth, but he is also knowing at signaling
the very airy breath itself. And one of the hunters, longing to test the
whelps, before the steep gates carries in his hands a hare either dead or
living. And he creeps before beside the path, first driving a straight, then
an oblique, left, right, winding a crooked road around.

But when he has come very far from the town and the gates, then having dug a
pit he has buried it. And having returned to the town rushing back he at once
brings near the path the care-bringing dog. And he at once is stirred, and
snorts under the hare-breath. And he seeks tracks along the ground, and even
longing he cannot find them. And he wanders much distressed. As when some girl
about the tenth month of the moon, first-bearing, struck under the birth-pangs
of lying-in, has loosed her locks, and loosed the cunning work from the
breasts, and being without tunic, wretched and unveiled, she turns everywhere
through the house and in labor now goes to the porch, and now again rushes to
the bed, and sometimes thrown in the dust she wails, wiping on rosy cheeks. So
he, distressed with heart-eating pains, rushes this way and that and in
succession questions every stone and every hill and every path and trees and
vines and walls and threshing-floors. But when late he happens on the airy
track, he laughs and whimpers glad, as little heifers skip around uddered
cows. So also for him the heart has rejoiced greatly, and rushing he is
coiled, having stepped on the crooked plains. Nor would you turn him aside,
not even if you drive very far, but he rushes clutching fast the sweet scent,
until he passes to the end of the toil and to the starting-line. And if you
arm him against unhunted hares, he approaches in secret, and he presses track
upon track, small, covered under vines or stubble, like a robber thief of
kids, who, having watched the sleeping herdsman near, creeps quiet.

But when he draws near the hare thickets, he has leapt lightly, like a bow or
a hissing snake, whom a reaper or some plowman has stirred, formerly still,
from his venom-holding hole. So he laughing has leapt swift. And if he
succeeds, easily having mastered him with sharper claws and taking him with
the jaws he would meet with a great burden.

Swiftly he carries it and, laboring and heavy, he approaches. As from the crop
a wagon of harvest, heavy with wheat, goes toward the fold, and seeing it the
country people have rushed in a throng, and coming to meet far in front one
presses on the wheels, another on the upper frame, and another helping the
axle for the oxen. And having come to the fold they have loosed the yoke-pole,
and the sweating bulls have breathed from toil, and the heart of the weak
ox-driver has rejoiced greatly. So he arrives bringing the burden in his jaws.
And the swift hunter meets him glad, and lifting both high from mother earth,
he has set in his folds the beast and the beast-slayer himself.

Come then, daughter of Zeus, fair-ankled Phoebe, virgin of the golden fillet,
twin race to Apollo, tell me who of mortals and of mighty heroes first took up
from your hand the great-famed devices of the hunt. Around the feet of windy
Pholoe the wild tribes mixed with beasts, of mortals as far as the loins, and
from the loins of half-mortal horses, found the hunt for supper. And among
mortals first he who reft the Gorgon's neck,

the famous child of golden Zeus, Perseus, found it. But lifted on the swift
wings of his feet he took hares and jackals and the race of wild goats and
swift gazelles and the races of oryxes and the high heads of the spotted deer
themselves. And horse-hunting the light-bringer Castor found. And some he
killed, throwing a well-aimed javelin straight, and others again, driving with
swift horses, he took the beasts at the meeting of the midday course.

And for the wars of beasts first Zeus-born Lacedaemonian Polydeuces armed
jagged-toothed dogs. For in boxing he slew wretched men and with swift whelps
he mastered spotted beasts. And outstandingly in the standing mountain wars
warlike Meleager, son of Oeneus, flashed. And nets and nooses and crooked nets
first Hippolytus showed to hunting mortals. And first the famous daughter of
Schoeneus, Atalanta, the boar-striking girl, found winged slaughter for
beasts. And nocturnal wiles, the all-stealing night hunt, Orion first,
crafty-minded, planned. So many were formerly the strong leaders of the hunt.
And many afterward sharp love has mastered. For no one mastered by the goads
of the lovely hunt would willingly leave it again. Unspeakable bonds hold him.
Such as sweet sleep on flowers in the season of spring, and such again in
summer a sweet bed in a cave, and such a delight for hunters to taste among
the crags. And how much grace attends those plucking themselves the flower of
